# How Bunjil got its capital back


The ASCII form *bunjil* looks like any other lowercase domain string. But **Bunjil** is not any
other name: it is the creator eaglehawk of the Kulin nations of central Victoria, the ancestor who
shaped Country, gave law, and then withdrew into the sky as the star Altair. This post explains why
the Unicode restoration capitalises the first letter, what that capital letter preserves, and how a
Tier 2 classification still honours a figure of immense cultural depth.


## At a Glance


- **Restored name:** Bunjil

- **ASCII form:** bunjil

- **Meaning:** "The eaglehawk"

- **Domain of influence:** Creator, law-giver, sky ancestor, moiety founder

- **Pantheon:** Australian Aboriginal (Kulin nations)

- **Classification:** Tier 2

- **Original script:** None — the Kulin languages were traditionally oral; the name survives in
Latin transcription


## Overview


**Bunjil** (*bunjil*) — Creator Eaglehawk — belongs to the Aboriginal traditions of the Kulin
nations. In the Dreaming he shaped the land, water, plants, and animals of central Victoria and
established the laws that govern relations between people and Country. He is one of two moiety
ancestors, paired with Waang the crow. After his creative work was complete, he ascended to the sky,
where he is identified with the bright star Altair; his two wives became the black swans beside him,
and his son Binbeal is the rainbow.


PuniCodex restores the name as **Bunjil**. The plain ASCII form *bunjil* is a technological
compromise: the early domain-name system could not carry meaning, only letters, and it forced every
name into lowercase. The restoration adds back the capital initial that English and Australian
scholarly convention uses for a proper name. Because no diacritic or special letter is needed to
recover the conventional spelling, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the
philologically complete reference; the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for DNS.


## The Name


The name is attested in the Woiwurrung, Boonwurrung, and related Kulin languages of central
Victoria. It refers to the wedge-tailed eaglehawk (*Aquila audax*) in its ancestral, creative
aspect. Because these languages were — and in revitalisation efforts continue to be — oral
traditions, there is no pre-colonial alphabetic original script. The name survives in the Latin
transcriptions made by early settlers and anthropologists.


The PuniCodex restoration **Bunjil** simply applies the standard scholarly convention of
capitalising a proper name. The ASCII form *bunjil* is therefore not an older or more authentic
spelling; it is the domain-name system stripping away the visual signal that this is a name. The
spoken name and the mythic role remain unchanged.


## Pronunciation and Classification


The conventional scholarly reading is **/ˈbʊn.dʒɪl/** — stress the first syllable, pronounce the 'j'
as in 'jump', and keep the final vowel short.


Bunjil is Tier 2 because the ASCII form *bunjil* already contains the conventional spelling. No
stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the name. The restoration is
a typographic rather than a phonemic recovery: it restores the capital that marks a proper name, not
a sound that would otherwise be lost.


## Myths and Symbols


Bunjil's stories are preserved in the Dreaming narratives of the Kulin nations. A Woiwurrung account
tells how Bunjil shaped the land and then asked Waang, keeper of the winds, to release a whirlwind
that carried him and his family into the sky. A Boonwurrung story tells of a time when the sea rose
in anger at human conflict; Bunjil stopped the flood only after the people agreed to live by the
law. The rock-art site known as Bunjil's Shelter, in Gariwerd, remains one of the most important
Aboriginal art places in Victoria.


His symbols are drawn from the living world: the wedge-tailed eagle, the spear of authority, the
black swans that were his wives, the rainbow that is his son Binbeal, and the whirlwind that lifted
him to the sky.


## Why the Capital Matters


For a name like Bunjil, the difference between *bunjil* and **Bunjil** can look trivial. It is not.
The capital letter is the only visual cue, in plain Latin text, that we are looking at a proper name
rather than a common noun. In a project devoted to restoring the scholarly citation forms of names,
that cue is evidence. The capital does not add sound, but it adds recognition: it says that this
string refers to a specific ancestral person, not to an eaglehawk in general.


The restoration also makes explicit the cultural asymmetry built into DNS. The domain-name system
was designed for ASCII and for lowercase; it cannot preserve the capitalisation, the diacritics, or
the non-Latin scripts that many names require. Bunjil's restoration is a small repair of that
asymmetry.
