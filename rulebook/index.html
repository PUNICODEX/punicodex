The Restoration Rulebook

Every name in the Pantheon is a scholarly restoration, not an invention and never a brand. The marks above the letters are not decoration: they are the original sound of the name — its stress and its long vowels — recovered from the sources and carried into the digital realm. This rulebook is the doctrine every one of the 983 restorations follows, and it is binding on us before it is binding on anyone else.

I

The Restoration Principle

The names were sung with their marks intact. The marks are the name.

Greek names were written for over two thousand years with pitch accent (the acute and circumflex marks) and vowel quantity (long ē and ō beside short e and o). Schoolbooks dropped both; the alphabet of screens dropped the rest. What remained was a flattened echo — apollon, nike, hera — that loses how the name actually sounded in the mouths of the people who worshipped these gods.

A restoration puts the sound back, using the same conventions as the critical editions scholars read — Liddell–Scott–Jones, Beekes' Etymological Dictionary of Greek, and their peers in every tradition:

Ἀπόλλων→apollon→Apóllōnstress on the penult, long ō
Νίκη→nike→Níkēstress on the first syllable, long ē
Ἑκάτη→hekate→Hekátēstress on the penult, long final ē

These forms are restored-stress, length-preserving transliterations: the acute or circumflex carries the original stress position; the macron (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū) carries the original vowel length. Nothing is added that the sources do not contain; nothing the sources contain is silently discarded.

II

What Each Restoration Preserves

The description is computed from the spelling, never assigned by ownership or fame.

Every name in the lexicon carries a description computed mechanically from its displayed form. The same test runs on all 983 entries, and it can never claim more than the form shows:

Plate 1 · The four descriptions

The form showsWhat that meansExample
Stress & lengthA circumflex (or macron + acute) fuses both features on one vowel — the fullest restoration the sources supportAthēnâ (Ἀθηνᾶ) · Hermês (Ἑρμῆς) · Poseidôn (Ποσειδῶν)
Stress or lengthOne prosodic feature survives where ASCII shows none — the form still says something the fallback alphabet cannotZeús (stress) · Hēra (length) · Ártemis (stress)
Distinctive lettersLetters outside the Latin alphabet survive intact — þ, ð, æ, œ, ꜣ, ꜥ and kindred formsÞórr (thorn) · ꜥnḫ (Egyptian ayin)
Multiple forms in the familyMore than one historically valid spelling exists — attested, conventional, or derived — and the temple's Name Variations section enumerates the family with its sources (ownership is an annotation, not a criterion)Apóllōn / Apollōn · Hekátē / Hekatē · Níkē / Nikē

Some scholarly forms are identical to ASCII — Freyja, Loki, Ymir — where the restoration is faithful without adding marks; the description says so plainly rather than inventing ornament. And where the ideal form competes with an attested variant — Athēnâ primary, Athēnā live — both are owned, so the temple presents the pair with the evidence for each.

III

The Spelling Space

A form is known in exactly one of three ways. There is no fourth.

Most ancient names were never spelled one way. Scholarship attests several spellings, conventions generate systematic alternates, and any restoration mechanically implies simpler subsets of itself. PuniCodex enumerates this space for every name — 983 variant families, computed at build time — and labels each form by exactly how it is known:

Plate 2 · The three classes of knowing

ClassWhat it meansCounted as attested?Example
AttestedA spelling backed by a named scholarly source — LSJ, Beekes, Faulkner, Monier-Williams — or by a live registrationYes — attestedCount counts only theseApóllōn (primary, owned) · Apollōn (LSJ convention, owned) · Siva (Monier-Williams, Macdonell)
ConventionA systematic romanization another standard would produce — Egyptological aleph-as-a, IAST simplified, Kunrei-shiki, chi-as-khNo — labelled "convention" wherever shownRꜥ → Ra · Śiva → Shiva
DerivedA mechanical subset of the canonical form — macron-only (LSJ), stress-only, plain ASCII, stacked marksNo — labelled "systematic derivation"Apóllōn → Apollōn / Apóllon / Apollon / Apṓllōn

The rule is the discipline that governs every chapter of this book: a description may never claim more than the data supports. A form is "attested" only with a source attached; a convention form is never presented as a citation; a derived form never pretends to be anything but arithmetic on the restoration. Ownership is an annotation on the family — it gates nothing, and knowledge of a spelling we do not own is published exactly the same as one we do. Every family is served openly at /api/v1/names/{id}/variants.

IV

When the Ideal Form Can't Be Registered

Availability negotiates. Philology does not.

Unicode domains are scarce. When the ideal restoration is unavailable, the rulebook descends a fixed hierarchy — each step still defensible, never a wrong accent for the sake of availability:

Plate 3 · The fallback hierarchy

PriorityFormExampleWhen
1Full restorationAphrodítē, Hḗra, PandṓraStress and length both preserved; macron + acute when Greek has an acute on a long vowel
2CircumflexHermês, Poseidôn, AthēnâGreek has a circumflex — stress and length in one typeable letter
3Macron-onlyHēra, Persephonē, PandōraThe standard LSJ convention; used as the registrable, typeable fallback when the ideal stacked form is impractical
4Attested variantAthēnā (Ἀθηνᾶ)The canonical form would hurt recognizability; the variant is attested
5Plain ASCIIHeraLast resort, never the primary form
Acute on a long vowel is not a circumflex. Greek Πανδώρα has an acute, not a circumflex, so the fullest form is Pandṓra (macron + acute), never Pandôra. Where the ideal stacked form is hard to type or render, the project falls back to the macron-only LSJ convention — but the ideal variant is always preserved in the lexicon.
A wrong accent is worse than no accent. The form ápollōn was scrapped in favour of plain apollōn — a misplaced mark misreads the name's sound, which is the one thing a restoration must never do.
The historical circumflex transliteration. You may occasionally see older European philology write Athênê, Hêraklês, or Nikê — especially in the French tradition, where the circumflex wrote the long vowels η and ω (ê, ô) as a convention for vowel quantity, folding length into a single letter. That convention was common in 19th–early 20th century scholarship, and it remains academically intelligible, but it is not the modern standard: today's critical editions, LSJ, the Oxford Classical Dictionary, Cambridge Greek, and Unicode transliteration standards separate length (macron: ē, ō) from stress (acute: é, ṓ) rather than folding quantity into the vowel letter. PuniCodex follows the modern standard; the older circumflex forms are noted as historical variants, not canonical restorations.

VI

The Registration Constraint

The rule bends only where the registration system forces it — and always on the record.

Internationalized domain names accept every mark this rulebook defends — macrons, acutes, underdots, thorn, ogonek, and the Egyptological aleph and ayin themselves: Rꜥ.com is a live temple. The practical limits are the DNS itself and registry policy: a single label must stay in one script, and individual registrars apply their own character filters on top of the standard. Where the philologically ideal form hits one of those constraints, the temple uses the closest registrable form and documents the compromise openly.
VII

Why It Matters

A restoration is a claim about the world. It must be checkable.

7.1Sound is identity

A god's name was sung, sworn by, and carved into stone with its stress and length intact. Restoring both is the difference between a label and a name.

7.2Provenance is prior art

These names are older than any company. Níkē is the goddess of victory, named in Hesiod's Theogony some twenty-seven centuries ago; the accent above her í is her own, not anyone's brand. Every restoration here cites its sources — LSJ and Beekes for Greek, Faulkner for Egyptian, Monier-Williams for Sanskrit, and the primary texts in our Sacred Texts library.

7.3The rules are load-bearing

The same mechanical rules run in the open: the lexicon validator, the restoration descriptions on every temple, and this page's examples all agree, because they are all computed from one canonical source. If a restoration ever fails the rules, the build fails — not the reader's trust.

✦

Thus the rulebook. It applies to the 983 names in the lexicon, to every temple page, to every API response — and to any name that joins the pantheon hereafter. The doctrine is dated, the edition numbered, and the machinery public; what changes, changes on the record.
