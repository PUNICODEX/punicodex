01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Brokkr (brokkr) ​— Dwarf ‌Smith · Badger, ‌panting — ​is the ​smith who took ​the strangest ​wager in the ​northern corpus: ‌his craft ​against Loki's head. ​While his ‌brother worked the ​forge, Brokkr ‌worked the ​bellows through three ‌castings — ​the golden-bristled boar ​Gullinbursti, the ​drip-gold ring ​Draupnir, and the ​hammer Mjǫllnir ‌— and the ​Æsir judged ‌the last of ​them the ‌best treasure ​ever made.[1]

Old ‌Norse Brokkr ​is attested only ‌in Snorri's ​Prose Edda, ​where the episode ‌of the ‌wager is told ‌in Skáldskaparmál ​with a ​smith's precision: what ‌was laid ​in the fire, ​what came ‌out, and ​what each interruption ​cost. Beside ‌the proper name ​stands the ‌common noun brokkr, ​'a badger', ​and whether ​the dwarf carried ​the animal-name ‌or a separate ‌formation is ​unsettled in ‌the handbooks.[2] His ​brother at ​the forge is ‌named Eitri ‌in the ‌main text of ‌the Prose ​Edda; some manuscripts ‌read Sindri, ​and the ​reference works register ​both.[3]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Brokkr ​— identical to ​the ASCII ‌input, because ​normalized Old Norse ​writes no ​diacritic in this ‌name; the ​mechanical rule ‌therefore places it ​in Tier ‌2. The temple ​is domain-less: ‌the plain-ASCII ‌brokkr.com is held ​outside the ​collection and watched ​for release, ​and no ‌form of the ‌name is ​claimed here.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35 (the wager of the head and the making of the three treasures), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. brokkr.
  3. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Brokk, Eitri, Sindri.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in normalized ‌Old Norse ​as Brokkr (nominative; ​genitive Brokks). ​It is ​a strong ​masculine with ‌the nominative ending ​-r and ​a geminate ‌-kk- that ​the spelling records ‌faithfully; the ​ASCII fallback ‌brokkr loses ​nothing but ​the capital, which ​is precisely ​why the ​name is ‌Tier 2 under ​the mechanical ‌rule.[1]

The ​etymology is ‌an honest open ​question. Cleasby–Vigfusson ‌gloss the ​common noun ‌brokkr 'a ​badger' (comparing Danish ​brok), and ‌the dictionaries ‌note figurative ‌senses; the traditional ​glosses carried ​by the ‌lexicon — ​'badger, panting' ​— reflect this ‌noun and ​the older ​suggestion of ‌a connection with ​heavy breathing. ‌Whether the ​dwarf's name ​is the animal-name, ​a byname ​turned proper ‌name, or ‌a separate ​formation is unsettled: ‌Simek registers ​the obscurity ​rather than ‌settling it, and ‌so does ‌this temple.[2]

‌The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

‌- b → ​B — ​Same, capitalized
​- r ‌→ r — ​Same

  • ​o → ​o — ‌Same
  • k ​→ k ​— Same

​- k ‌→ k ​— Same

  • ‌r → ​r — ‌Same

The ​project holds no ‌domain for ‌this name: ​brokkr.com was ​registered in ​the ordinary way ​outside the ‌collection and ‌is watched ​for release.

Sources

  1. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. brokkr.
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. brokkr; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (1993), s.v. Brokk.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈbrok.kr̩/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Br- ​— Voiced ‌bilabial stop ​[b] into ​a trilled [r]; ‌stress on ​the first ‌syllable, as ​in every ‌Old Norse ​word

  • -o- ​— A ​short open ​back vowel ​[ɔ]-[o]; the ‌manuscripts write a ​plain short ‌o, and ​the Younger ‌Futhark would ​carve it ‌with the úr-rune

​- -kkr ‌— A ​true geminate ​[kː], the ‌doubled k held ‌long across ‌the syllable ​boundary, closing ​into the ‌syllabic nominative ​[r̩]

For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest ​approximation is: ​'BROK-ur' — ‌two beats, the ​o short ‌as in ​lot, the ​double k ​held a ​moment longer than ‌English allows.

‌Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • Old ​Norse — ‌Brokkr, the ‌dwarf of ‌Skáldskaparmál; beside ‌it the ​common noun brokkr, ‌'a badger'

​- Younger ​Futhark — ​ᛒᚱᚢᚴᛦ, the ‌Viking-Age runic rendering ​(b-r-u-k-R)

  • ​Companion name ​— Eitri, ‌the brother ​at the ​forge; some manuscripts ​read Sindri

‌Brokkr is ​Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves nothing ‌the ASCII ​form loses: ‌the name ‌is already ​plain ASCII, ​and the tier ​rule is ​a report ‌on orthography, ‌not on ​the smith.[1]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and orthography.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ​manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Brokkr — ​Old Norse ‌(North Germanic), ​attested 13th–14th ​c. CE ‌in the ​Codex Regius ‌of the ​Prose Edda ‌and its ​sister codices, ​recording material ​generations older.[1] ​Before the ​Latin bookhand, ‌Old Norse ​names were ‌carved in ​the younger ‌futhark; no ​runic attestation ‌of this ​name survives, ‌so the ​manuscript form ​is the ‌primary witness.

‌The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ​step by ​step:

  • ‌The name ​is a ​strong masculine: ‌stem Brokk- ​with the ​nominative singular ‌ending -r.

​- The ‌geminate -kk- ​is a ​true long ​consonant, held ​across the ‌syllable boundary; ‌the manuscripts ​write it ‌double, and ​the spelling ​is exact.
‌- In ‌the Younger ‌Futhark the ‌name would ​have been ‌carved ᛒᚱᚢᚴᛦ ​(b-r-u-k-R), with ​úr writing ​the o ‌and ᛦ ​the final ​nominative -r.
​- The ‌Unicode restoration ​Brokkr loses ​nothing to ​ASCII but ‌the capital: ​the normalized ‌Old Norse ​form is ‌already plain ​ASCII, which ‌is why ‌the name ​stands in ​Tier 2 ​— an ​orthographic report, ‌not a ‌judgment on ​the smith.[2]

Sources

  1. Faulkes, Anthony, ed., Edda: Prologue and Gylfaginning (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), on the manuscript witnesses.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Brokkr is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌Dwarf Smith — ​and the ​corpus earns ​the label in ​a single ​afternoon's work: three ‌castings, three ​treasures, and ​a wager collected ‌to its ​letter.[1]

‌The Smith of ​the Wager

‌When Loki ​boasted that no ​dwarf could ​match the sons ​of Ívaldi's ​treasures, Brokkr ‌staked the boast ​against Loki's ‌head — ​and won. His ‌is the ​only smith ‌in the corpus ​whose skill ‌is proved in ​open competition.

​### The ‌Bellows

His own ‌station was ‌the bellows: ​the unbroken breath ​that held ‌each heat. ​Twice a fly's ​sting failed ‌to move him; ​the third ​sting, on ‌the eyelid, cost ​the hammer ‌its handle ​— the one ​flaw sabotage ​ever bought ​from him.

‌The Three ‌Treasures

Gullinbursti for ​Freyr, Draupnir ‌for Óðinn, ​Mjǫllnir for Þórr: ​the boar ‌that outruns ‌horses, the ring ‌that drips ‌eight rings ​every ninth night, ‌the hammer ​that never misses. ​Every one ​remains a ‌working tool of ​the gods ​through the ​rest of the ‌corpus.

​The Letter ​of the Bargain

​When Loki ‌pleaded that the ​head was ‌staked but ​not the neck, ‌Brokkr honored ​the reading ‌— and sewed ‌the trickster's ​lips shut ​with the thread ​called Vartari. ​Craft, patience, and ‌a lawyer's ‌precision in ​one figure.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Brokkr's ​iconography is ‌the iconography ‌of his ​episode ​— the ​corpus gives ​him no ​cult ‌regalia, but ​the wager ​fixed a ‌small ​set of ‌objects no ​retelling can ‌omit:[1]

​- The ​bellows — ​his station ​in ​the forge: ‌the unbroken ​breath on ‌which ​every casting ‌depended, and ​the one ‌duty ​the wager ‌allowed no ​rest from
​- ‌The fly ‌— the ‌small sabotage ​aimed ​at the ‌work rather ​than the ​worker: ‌twice it ​failed, and ​at the ‌last ​it bought ‌only a ​shortened handle
​- ​Mjǫllnir's short ​handle — ‌the honest ‌flaw ​in the ‌perfect weapon; ​the Æsir ​judged ‌the marred ‌hammer best ‌of all ‌the ​treasures all ‌the same
​- The ​awl ​and the ‌thread Vartari ​— the ​instruments ​of the ‌bargain's enforcement: ​the head ​won, ​the neck ‌denied, and ​the price ‌taken ​another way
‌- The ​head as ‌stake ‌— the ​highest wager ​the corpus ​records: ​a trickster's ‌life against ‌a smith's ​confidence[2]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Brokkr's mythology is ​a single, perfectly shaped narrative — ‌the wager of the head ‌— preceded by the mischief that ​made it necessary and closed by ​the loophole that shaped its ​price.[1]

The Hair of Gold ​and the Sons of Ívaldi ​(Skáldskaparmál 35)

Loki cut off all ‌the hair of Sif, Þórr's ​wife, for a prank; Þórr caught ​him and was set to break ‌every bone when Loki swore ​to have the svartálfar make hair ‌of gold that would grow ​like other hair. He went to ‌the dwarfs called the sons ​of Ívaldi, who made the hair, ​the ship Skíðblaðnir, and the spear ​Gungnir. Grímnismál keeps the ship's ​making in verse: 'Ívaldi's sons went ​in days of old to ‌make Skíðblaðnir, best of ships, for ​bright Freyr.'[1][2]

The Wager ‌of the Head (Skáldskaparmál 35)

On ​his way home Loki stopped ‌at Brokkr's forge and wagered his ​own head that Brokkr's brother could ‌not make three treasures as ​good. Snorri names the brother Eitri; ‌some manuscripts read Sindri, and ​the handbooks register both. The terms ​placed Brokkr at the bellows: ‌the breath was not to falter ‌until each work came out of ‌the fire.[1][3]

Three Castings, ​Three Stings

A pig's skin went ​in first, and a fly ‌stung Brokkr's hand; he blew on, ​and out came the boar ​Gullinbursti, bristles of gold. Gold went ‌in next; the fly stung his ​neck twice as hard; he ​blew on, and out came Draupnir. ‌Iron went in last, with ​Eitri's warning that all would be ‌spoiled if the blast faltered ​now; the fly stung between his ​eyes until blood ran into one ​eye, Brokkr swept it away ​and paused the bellows for an ‌instant — and the hammer ‌came out with a short handle. ​The story never names the ‌fly; it never needs to.[1]

​The Judgment of the Æsir

At ​Ásgarðr, Óðinn, Þórr, and Freyr ‌judged. Loki gave Gungnir to Óðinn, ‌the hair to Þórr for ‌Sif, Skíðblaðnir to Freyr. Brokkr gave ‌Gullinbursti to Freyr — running ​through air and water better than ‌any horse, its bristles lighting the ​dark — Draupnir to Óðinn, ​dripping eight rings of equal weight ​every ninth night, and Mjǫllnir ‌to Þórr: unerring, returning to the ​hand, small enough to hide ​in the shirt, flawed only in ​the handle. The judgment was ‌that the hammer was best of ​all, the greatest defense against the ​rime-giants. Loki owed his head.[1]

​### The Head and the Neck

‌Loki offered ransom; Brokkr refused ​all gold. Loki fled on his ‌sky-shoes; Þórr caught him at ​Brokkr's request. Then the letter of ‌the bargain: the head was Brokkr's, ​but no part of the ‌neck might be cut. The court ‌allowed it, and Brokkr took ​the price another way — he ​sewed Loki's lips together with ​the thread called Vartari. The episode ​ends there: the gods keep the ‌treasures, Loki keeps his head, ‌and Brokkr keeps the record of ​having made Mjǫllnir.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Grímnismál 43 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  3. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (1993), s.v. Brokk, Eitri, Sindri.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The smithing ​dwarf is ‌native Germanic ‌stock and ​needs no foreign ​original. The ​corpus keeps ​a guild of ​him: Sindri ‌the sparkler, ​Andvari the ​gold-guardian, the sons ‌of Ívaldi, ​and Vǫlundr ‌of Vǫlundarkviða — ​'prince of ‌elves', smith ​of the ​heroic hoard, whose ​English cognate ​Wayland is ​named in Beowulf ‌and pictured ​on the ‌eighth-century Franks ​Casket. The dwarf-kennings ‌of skaldic ​verse treat ‌the whole tribe ​as the ‌makers of ​every precious ​thing.[1]

The comparison ‌with the ‌Greek smith-god ‌Hēphaistos has been ​tried since ​the interpretatio ‌romana paired ​Vulcan with the ​northern forge-figures, ‌but the ​families differ where ​it matters: ‌Hēphaistos is ​a god ‌who makes wonders ​for gods, ​while Brokkr ​is a tradesman ​who beats ‌the gods' ‌own suppliers ​in open competition ‌and collects ​on the ​debt. The sharpest ‌contrast lives ‌inside the ‌corpus itself, ‌between two dwarf-rings: ​Draupnir, made ‌freely in ​a wager, drips ​eight rings ​every ninth ‌night, while ​Andvari's ring, extorted ​under threat, ​kills every ‌hand that holds ​it — ​the given ​ring multiplies, ‌the taken ring ​devours.[2]

Within ‌the corpus, ​closely related figures ‌include Loki ​(the wager's ‌loser), Þórr ‌(the hammer's owner), ​Freyr (the ​boar's), and ​Svartálfaheimr (the world ​of the ‌black-elves whose ‌work the ​wager judged).

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. dwarfs, Brokk, Vǫlundr.
  2. Orchard, Andy, Cassell's Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Brokk, dwarfs, Andvari.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Brokkr's afterlife ​is the ‌afterlife of ‌his hammer: ​every retelling ​of the ​Norse myths ​that shows ​Mjǫllnir made ‌must walk back ​into his ​forge. The ‌Victorian mythographies ​retold the ‌wager as ​a set-piece ‌of dwarf-craft, ​and every ​modern handbook keeps ​the episode ​as the ​founding legend ‌of the ​gods' treasures.[1]

‌Wagner's Ring ​rebuilt the ‌dwarf-smith as Mime ​and Alberich ‌— crossed ​with Andvari's ‌cursed gold ​— and ​Tolkien's dwarves, ‌miners and ‌master-smiths whose ‌craft outlasts kingdoms, ​descend from ​the same ‌þulur that ​list Brokkr's ​kin. Popular ‌culture has ​since made ​the forge ‌scene canonical: Marvel's ​Thor comics ‌know both ​Brokk and ​Eitri as ​dwarf-smiths, and ​the films ‌set the ‌making of ​Thor's weapons in ‌the dwarf-realm ​of Nidavellir ​— Snorri's ‌episode retold ‌for the ‌largest audience ‌the myth ​has ever ‌had.[1]

In Iceland ​the name ​itself survives ​as a ‌rarity, and ​in the ​dictionaries as ​a word ‌for a badger ​— the ​animal-name the ​smith may ‌or may ​not have ‌carried into ​myth. Modern ‌Heathen and ​Ásatrú retellings keep ‌the wager ‌as the ​tradition's standing ​proof that ​craft, patiently ​kept, beats ‌cunning every ‌time it ​is tested.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Brokk, dwarfs, reception.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Brokkr ​is a ‌purely literary ‌figure: no ​runestone, votive ​deposit, or ​named image ​records him, ​and his ‌name survives ​in ​the epigraphic ‌record, if ​at all, ‌only as ​raw anthroponymic ‌material. His ​attestation is ​textual and, ​for an ​Eddic ​episode, unusually ‌well witnessed: ​the wager ‌stands in ​the principal ‌codices of ​the Prose ‌Edda — ​the ‌Codex Regius ​GKS 2367 ​4to (c. ‌1300–1325) and ‌its sister ‌manuscripts — ​with the ​brother's name ‌given as ​Eitri ​in the ‌main text ​and Sindri ​in some ‌copies.[1]

The ​myth's material ‌world, by ​contrast, is ​richly documented. ​The ​Mästermyr find ‌from Gotland ‌— a ​Viking-Age smith's ‌complete tool ​chest buried ​about the ‌year 1000 ‌— preserves ‌the ‌bellows, hammers, ​tongs, and ‌anvil of ​exactly the ​forge-culture the ​story presumes.[2] ‌And the ​wager's third ​casting ​left the ‌deepest material ​trace of ​all: Thor's-hammer ​amulets, cast ‌in silver ​and worn ‌across the ​Viking world ‌from ​the ninth ‌century onward, ‌are the ​archaeological footprint ​of Mjǫllnir ​— the ​smith's work ‌carried at ‌thousands of ​necks.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never stages ‌Brokkr's ​episode — ​the ​wager belongs ​to Snorri's ​prose ‌— but ​the poems ​corroborate ‌its foundations ​at both ‌ends. ​Grímnismál 43 ‌records ​the work ​of the ​rivals: ​'Ívaldi's sons ​went in ‌days ​of old ‌to make ​Skíðblaðnir, ‌best of ​ships, ‌for bright ​Freyr' — ‌the ​very benchmark ​Brokkr's three ‌castings ‌were judged ‌against.[1]

The ​Edda's ​dwarf-lore supplies ‌the ​world around ​him. Vǫluspá's ‌great ​catalogue of ​dwarfs, the ‌Dvergatal, ​lists the ‌smith-tribe by ​the ​score, and ​Vǫlundarkviða ​preserves the ‌corpus's other ‌great ​smith narrative ‌— Vǫlundr, ​'prince ​of elves', ‌who forges ‌in ‌captivity and ‌pays ​his debts ‌in kind.[2] ​The ​Þórr poems, ​finally, live ‌on ​the wager's ​product: Mjǫllnir ​is ‌the hammer ​of ​Þrymskviða and ​Hymiskviða, stolen ‌and ​won back, ‌swung against ​giants ‌— every ​verse use ‌of ‌it a ​quiet ​footnote to ​the afternoon ​it ‌came out ‌of Brokkr's ​fire.[3]

  2. Vǫluspá (Dvergatal) and Vǫlundarkviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Brokkr's ‌only narrative ​witness, and ​it is ​a generous ​one. Skáldskaparmál ​35 gives ‌the fullest ​smithing episode ​in Old ‌Norse: the ​cutting of ‌Sif's hair, ​Loki's errand ‌to the ​svartálfar, the ​sons of ​Ívaldi's three ​treasures, the ​wager of ‌the head ​against Brokkr's ‌brother — ​named Eitri ‌in the ​main text, ‌Sindri in ​some manuscripts ‌— the ​three castings ​and the ‌fly's three ‌stings, the ‌judgment of ​Óðinn, Þórr, ​and Freyr, ‌and the ​closing scene ​in which ‌Brokkr, denied ​the neck ​by the ‌bargain's letter, ​sews Loki's ‌lips together ​with the ​thread Vartari.[1]

​Snorri's craft ​deserves note: ‌the episode ‌exists to ​explain the ‌kennings — ​why poets ​call gold ‌'Sif's hair' ‌and the ‌hammer by ‌its dwarf-made ​fame — ‌a myth ​doing the ​work of ​a dictionary, ‌and doing ​it with ​a smith's ​eye for ‌process. Gylfaginning ​adds the ​frame: the ​world of ‌the svartálfar ​among the ‌realms, and ​Draupnir's afterlife ‌on Baldr's ​pyre, whence ‌Hermóðr carries ‌it back ​from Hel ​— the ​wager's second ​casting still ‌circulating through ‌the gods' ​greatest grief.[2]

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987); Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (1993), s.v. Eitri, Sindri.
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49 (Draupnir on Baldr's pyre), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of ​the dwarf ​Brokkr. ​No runestone ​invokes ‌him, no ​amulet ​bears his ‌name, ​and the ‌name ​does not ‌appear ​among the ​securely ​read personal ​names ​of the ‌inscriptions ​— ‌a silence ​he ‌shares with ​most ‌of the ​mythological ‌dwarfs, whose ​world ​was the ‌forge ‌of poetry ‌rather ​than the ​cult ‌of the ​carved ​stone.[1]

What ‌the ​runes can ​supply ‌is the ​name's ‌own rendering. ​In ​the sixteen-rune ​Younger ​Futhark, Brokkr ‌would ‌have been ​carved ‌ᛒᚱᚢᚴᛦ (b-r-u-k-R): ​úr ​doing duty ‌for ‌the o ‌the ‌short row ​cannot ‌write, the ​k-rune ​writing the ​geminate ‌once, ​and ᛦ ​carrying ​the final ‌nominative ​-r. The ​rendering ​is a ‌normalized ​reconstruction, not ‌an ​attestation — ‌the ​primary witness ‌remains ‌the manuscript ​spelling ​of the ​Prose ​Edda, which ‌is ‌itself plain ​ASCII.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. dwarfs, Brokk.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), on the younger futhark.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Brokkr's ‌medieval afterlife ​is thin, ​and ​the record ​should ​say so ‌plainly. The ​sagas ​of Icelanders ‌and ​the kings' ‌sagas do ​not ‌stage him; ​his ​episode was ​Snorri's to ​tell, ​and no ‌independent ​prose narrative ‌retells ​the wager ‌outside the ​Prose ‌Edda's own ​manuscript ‌family.[1]

What ​the wider ​literature ‌keeps is ‌his ‌kin and ​his products. ​The ‌þulur — ​the ​metrical name-lists ‌preserved with ​Skáldskaparmál ​— catalogue ‌the ​dwarf-tribe among ‌whom ​he stands, ​and skaldic ​verse ​runs on ‌his ‌afternoon's work: ​the kennings ‌for ​poetry, gold, ​and ‌battle presuppose ‌dwarf-made treasures, ‌and ‌every hammer-kenning ​for ‌Þórr's weapon ​is downstream ​of ​the forge-scene ‌of ​Skáldskaparmál 35. ​The ​saga-age Icelanders ‌who wrote ​of ​smiths and ​their ‌craft — ​and the ‌metalworkers ​whose tools ‌the ​Mästermyr chest ‌preserves — ‌are ​the world ​the ​myth presumed; ​the myth's ‌own ‌testimony remains ​Snorri's.[1][2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Brokk, dwarfs.
  2. Arwidsson, Greta, and Gösta Berg, The Mästermyr Find: A Viking Age Tool Chest from Gotland (Stockholm: Almqvist & Wiksell, 1983).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Brokkr ​is the ‌patron of ‌the work ​that ​speaks for ​itself. He ​does not ​argue ‌with the ​boast that ​the sons ‌of ​Ívaldi cannot ‌be matched; ​he takes ‌the ​boast as ​a stake ​and makes ​three ​treasures in ‌an afternoon. ​He is ‌stung, ​and blows ‌on. He ​is sabotaged ‌at ​the last ‌instant, and ​the flaw ​that ‌results — ‌a short ‌handle — ​is ​the only ‌thing the ​trickster ever ​wins ‌from him; ​the Æsir ​judge the ‌marred ​hammer best ‌of all.[1]

​And when ​the ​bargain's letter ​is turned ‌against him, ‌he ​does not ‌rage at ​the loophole: ​he ‌honors it, ‌and takes ‌what the ‌letter ​leaves. The ‌corpus is ​full of ​oaths ​broken and ‌bargains twisted, ​and against ​them ​all it ‌sets this ​small, perfectly ​kept ​account — ‌a smith ​who bet ‌everything ​on his ‌craft, lost ​nothing that ‌mattered, ‌and sewed ​shut, for ​a while, ​the ​mouth that ‌thought skill ‌was a ​bluff.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
