01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Blodeuwedd (blodeuwedd) ​— Flower ‌Bride, Betrayal · ‌Flower face ​— is the ​flower-bride of ​the Fourth Branch ​of the ​Mabinogi: the fairest ‌woman anyone ​had ever seen, ​fashioned by ‌Gwydion and King ​Math from ‌the blossoms of ​oak, broom, ‌and meadowsweet to ​give Lleu ​Llaw Gyffes the ​wife his ​mother's destiny had ​forbidden him, ‌and the ​bride who loved ‌Gronw Pebr ​of Penllyn, drew ‌her husband's ​death-secret out of ‌him, and ​was sentenced by ‌her maker ​into the shape ​of the ‌owl.[1]

The name ‌is a ‌transparent Welsh compound ​— blodau, ​'flowers', plus gwedd, ‌'face, appearance' ​— and the ​Fourth Branch ‌uses it with ​full awareness: ​made as 'Flower-face', ‌she keeps ​the name in ‌her sentence, ​and in modern ​Welsh blodeuwedd ​has become the ​ordinary common ‌noun for the ‌owl, a ​semantic circle the ‌tale itself ​closes.[2] Because the ​Middle Welsh ‌manuscripts write the ‌name in ‌plain Latin letters, ‌the ASCII ​form loses nothing: ‌Tier 2 ​by the mechanical ​rule, a ​statement about orthography ‌and never ​about the ​figure.

PuniCodex records ​the name ‌as Blodeuwedd — ​the normalized ​manuscript spelling, identical ​in ASCII ‌and Unicode apart ​from the ‌capital. The plain-ASCII ​domain blodeuwedd.com ‌is registered to ​a third ‌party and held ‌outside the ​collection; it is ​watched for ​release, and this ​temple meanwhile ‌stands without a ‌domain of ​its own.[3]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch (Math uab Mathonwy), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007).

  3. Ifor Williams, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1951).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh ‌as Blodeuwedd (also ​written Blodeuedd) in ​the White Book ​of Rhydderch and ​the Red ​Book of Hergest.[1] ‌It is a ​compound the tale's ​own audience ‌could parse at ​sight: blodau, 'flowers', ‌and gwedd, 'face, ​appearance, form' ‌— Flower-face, the ​exact and almost ​mocking right name ​for a woman ​made out ​of blossoms.[2] The ‌Fourth Branch exploits ​the transparency twice: ‌once at ​her making, when ‌Math and Gwydion ​name the flower-woman, ‌and once at ​her sentencing, ‌when Gwydion rules ​that she shall ​not lose the ‌name but ‌bear it forever ‌in bird-shape — ​the etiological aition ​for the ‌modern Welsh fact ​that blodeuwedd means ​'owl'.[1]

Because the ‌manuscript spelling is ​plain Latin ​letters, the ASCII ‌form blodeuwedd is ​not a flattening ‌but the ​attested form itself: ​no diacritic is ​restored and no ​distinctive letter is ‌rescued, which ‌places the name ​in Tier 2. ‌The Unicode form ​Blodeuwedd differs ​from the ASCII ‌only by capitalisation.

‌The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • ‌b → B ​— Same, capitalized

  • ​w → ‌w — Same

​- e → ‌e — Same
​- d ‌→ d — ​Same

  • d ‌→ d — ‌Same

The ​plain-ASCII domain blodeuwedd.com ​is registered to ​a third party ​and held outside ‌the collection; ‌it is watched ​for release.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The modern ​Welsh pronunciation ‌of the ‌name is /blɔˈdɛu̯.wɛð/ ​— Modern ​Welsh standard.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- Blo- ​— Voiced bilabial ‌stop [b] ​into the lateral ​[l] and ‌a short ​open rounded [ɔ], ‌as in ​British English 'lot'; ‌unstressed

  • ​-deu- — ​Voiced dental stop ​[d] into ​the Welsh diphthong ​eu [ɛu̯], ‌a glide ​from open e ‌toward a ​close rounded vowel; ‌the stress ​falls here, ‌on the penult, ​by the ‌universal Welsh rule

​- -wedd ​— Labial-velar ‌glide [w], short ‌open [ɛ], ‌and final dd ​[ð], the ​voiced dental ‌fricative of English ​'this' — ​never a stopped ‌d

For ​the modern ​speaker, the closest ‌approximation is: ​'blo-DEH-weth' — three ‌syllables, the ​weight on ​the middle one, ​and the ​last consonant the ‌soft th ‌of 'this'.

​Kindred and historical ‌forms of ​the name:

  • ​Middle Welsh ‌— Blodeuwedd ‌(Blodeuedd), the manuscript ‌form[2]
  • ‌Welsh elements — ​blodau 'flowers' ‌+ gwedd ​'face, appearance', the ​compound's parts

​- Modern Welsh ‌— blodeuwedd, ​the everyday ​common noun 'owl'
​- Attested ‌form — Blodeuwedd

​Blodeuwedd is ​Tier 2 ​because the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​ASCII loses: the ‌name arrives ​in the ‌Latin alphabet, without ​diacritics, in ‌the manuscripts themselves. ‌Its interest ​lies in ​its meaning — ​a name ​that names her ‌substance — ‌not in ​rescued marks.

Sources

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

Welsh ​is written ‌in the ‌Latin alphabet, ​and ​the name ​is preserved ​in Middle ​Welsh ‌manuscripts as ​Blodeuwedd (also ​Blodeuedd) — ‌attested ​14th–15th c. ‌CE in ​the White ‌Book ​of Rhydderch ​(Aberystwyth, National ​Library of ​Wales, ​Peniarth 4–5, ‌c. 1350) ​and the ‌Red ​Book of ‌Hergest (Oxford, ​Jesus College ‌MS ​111, c. ‌1400), the ​two great ​codices ‌of the ‌Mabinogion.[1]

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds ​step by ‌step:

  • ​The manuscript ​spelling ‌Blodeuwedd is ​the form ​regularised in ‌Ifor ​Williams's standard ‌edition of ​the Four ​Branches; ​the variant ​Blodeuedd reflects ‌ordinary Middle ‌Welsh ​orthographic fluctuation, ‌not a ​different name.[2]

​- ‌No runic, ‌ogham, or ‌other non-Latin ‌attestation ​exists; the ‌figure belongs ​to the ​codex ​tradition, not ‌to the ​epigraphic record.
​- ​The ASCII ‌form blodeuwedd ​differs only ​in ​capitalisation; nothing ‌is lost ​or restored ‌between ​the two.
‌- The ​plain-ASCII domain ‌blodeuwedd.com ‌is registered ​to a ​third party, ​held ​outside the ‌collection, and ‌watched for ​release.[3]

  2. Ifor Williams, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1951).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Blodeuwedd is ​catalogued under the ‌domain 'Flower ‌Bride, Betrayal' — ​the two poles ​of her ​single story, and ​the Fourth Branch ​gives equal ‌weight to both.[1]

​### The Flower ​Bride

She is ‌the counter-spell ​to Arianrhod's third ‌tynged: since no ​wife of ‌any race on ​earth was permitted ​to Lleu, Math ​and Gwydion ​made one from ​the flowers of ‌the oak, ​the broom, and ‌the meadowsweet — ​'the fairest ‌and most beautiful ​woman anyone had ‌ever seen' — ​baptized her, ‌named her Flower-face, ​and married her ​to the ‌boy-king of Dinoding.[1]

‌### Betrayal

Welcomed ‌in hospitality, Gronw ​Pebr of ​Penllyn became her ‌chosen love; and ​it is ​she who asks ‌Lleu, 'in the ​guise of ​concern', how a ‌man so strangely ​fated could ever ‌die — ​extracting the impossible ​conditions and sending ​every term ​to Gronw, whose ‌spear was a ‌year in ​the forging.[1]

‌Transformation and Sentence

​Her domain's third ​term is ‌written in her ‌own body: the ‌made thing ‌remade by judgement. ​Gwydion will not ‌kill her; he ​gives her ​the owl's shape, ​the hatred of ‌all birds, ​the daylight ban ​— and the ​kept name, ‌so that the ​sentence and the ​beauty can never ​be separated.[1]

‌### The Court ​of Mur Castell

‌Her seat ​in Ardudwy is ‌the tale's domestic ​centre: the bridal ‌court where ‌the hunter was ​welcomed, from which ​the secret ​was sent, and ​from which she ‌fled into ‌the mountains with ​her maidens.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Blodeuwedd's ​attributes are ‌narrative rather ‌than cultic ​— the ​Welsh corpus ​preserves ​no regalia, ​but the ‌Fourth Branch ​fixed a ​recognisable set ‌of objects ​around ‌her:[1]

  • ​Oak, broom, ‌and meadowsweet ​— The ​three blossoms ​of ​her making, ​gathered by ‌Math and ​Gwydion and ‌worked with ​their ‌wands
  • ​The spear ‌a year ​in the ‌forging — ​Worked ​only during ‌the hours ‌of Mass ‌on Sundays, ​the weapon ​the stolen ‌death-secret ​specified
  • ​The cauldron ‌and the ​billy-goat — ​The two ‌footings ​of the ‌impossible stance: ​one foot ​on the ​cauldron's rim, ​one ‌on the ‌goat, neither ​indoors nor ‌out, neither ​mounted nor ​afoot
  • ‌The ‌owl — ‌The shape ‌of her ​sentence: never ‌to show ​its ​face by ​day, mobbed ‌by all ​birds wherever ​found
  • ​Llyn ‌y Morwynion ​— The ​Lake of ​the Maidens, ‌where her ​retinue, ‌looking backward ​as they ‌fled, walked ​into the ‌water
  • ‌Mur Castell ​— ​Her bridal ​court in ​Ardudwy, the ‌stage of ‌the whole ​tragedy

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Blodeuwedd's mythology is ​contained entire in the Fourth ‌Branch of the Mabinogi, 'Math ‌uab Mathonwy' — one witness, ​and the masterpiece of medieval ​Welsh prose.[1]

Made of ​Flowers

Arianrhod's third destiny on ​her son — no wife ​of any race then on ‌earth — was answered by ​craft. Math and Gwydion took ​the flowers of the oak, ‌the broom, and the meadowsweet ​and made from them the ‌fairest woman ever seen; they ​baptized her and named her Blodeuwedd. ‌She married Lleu, who was ​given the cantref of Dinoding, ​and the pair held court ​at Mur Castell in Ardudwy ​— the tale's one sentence ​of contentment before the turn.[1]

‌### Gronw of Penllyn

While ​Lleu visited Math at Caer ‌Dathyl, Gronw Pebr, lord of ​Penllyn, came hunting into the ‌district; Blodeuwedd welcomed him at ​nightfall, and that night they ‌loved each other. Three nights ​they lay together while her ‌husband sat at his uncle's ​court, and the question they ​put to themselves was how ‌to make it permanent.[1]

‌The Impossible Conditions

Blodeuwedd asked ‌her husband, 'in the guise ​of concern', how he could ​be killed, and Lleu trusted ‌her with the whole of ​it: neither indoors nor out, ​neither on horseback nor on ‌foot — only standing with ​one foot on the rim ​of a cauldron under a ‌thatched frame by a river and ​the other on a billy-goat, ‌and only by a spear ​worked for a year during ​Sunday Mass. She sent the ​terms to Gronw. When the ​year was out she asked ‌to see the marvellous stance; ‌Gronw rose from Bryn Cyfergyr ​and cast, the spear struck ‌Lleu in the side, and ​with a terrible scream he ​flew off as an eagle. ‌Gronw took the lordship.[1]

‌The Lake of the Maidens

‌When Math and Gwydion came ‌against Gronw, Blodeuwedd fled into ​the mountains with her maidens; ‌fear made them walk backward, ​watching for pursuit, and they ​walked into a lake and ​drowned, all save her — ‌the place called Llyn y ​Morwynion ever since. Gwydion overtook ​her and took her.[1]

​The Owl's Sentence

Gwydion's judgement: ‌'I will not kill you; ​I will do what is ​worse' — the shape of ​a bird, the daylight ban, the ‌enmity of all birds, and ​the kept name: 'you shall ‌not lose your name, but ​shall always be called Blodeuwedd.' ‌The tale closes its own ​etymology: blodeuwedd is what the ‌owl is called to this ‌day. The aftermath is Lleu's: ​nursed back from the eagle's ​shape, he stood where Gronw ​had stood and drove his ​spear through the stone slab ‌Gronw raised — Llech Ronw, ‌Gronw's stone, on the bank ​of the Cynfael.[1]

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch (Math uab Mathonwy), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Blodeuwedd's closest ​comparative kin ‌is the ‌made bride. ​Greek tradition ​offers Pandora, ​the woman ​fashioned by ​the gods ‌as a ​gift that ​is also ‌a trap: ​beauty assembled ‌by divine ​craft and ‌handed to ​a man ​with ruin ​folded in. ​The parallel ​is one ‌of motif, ​not borrowing ‌— both ​figures are ‌manufactured women ​whose makers ‌intend one ​destiny and ‌whose own ​desires rewrite ​it, though ‌Pandora's makers ‌send her ‌as the ​trap while ​Blodeuwedd's makers ‌are the ​betrayed.[1]

Within ​Celtic scholarship ‌she has ​been read ​in the ‌sovereignty frame: ​Mac Cana ‌and others ​note the ​pattern by ​which the ​woman's favour ‌carries the ‌land, since ​Gronw takes ‌Lleu's lordship ​with his ​wife.[2] Her ‌triple flowers ‌have also ‌attracted the ‌tree-calendar speculation ​associated with ‌Robert Graves's ​The White ​Goddess, which ​the handbooks ‌do not ​accept as ​philology. The ​sober verdict ‌is that ​she belongs ​to the ​international complexes ‌of the ​faithless bride ‌and the ​manufactured woman ‌— with ​a sentence ‌that is ‌the Mabinogi's ​alone: no ​other tradition ​punishes a ​woman by ‌making her ‌keep her ​name forever.[3]

Sources

  1. Hesiod, Works and Days and Theogony (Pandora), as the comparative made-bride tradition; cf. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Blodeuwedd.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992), on sovereignty themes in the Four Branches.
  3. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Blodeuwedd, Mabinogi.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Blodeuwedd's afterlife ​runs in ‌a word ‌and a ​novel. The ​word is ​her name ​itself: in ​modern Welsh ‌blodeuwedd simply ​means 'owl', ​so that ‌every Welsh ​speaker who ‌names the ​bird repeats ‌her sentence ​— a ​folk-etymology the ​Fourth Branch ​deliberately installed.[1] ​The novel ‌is Alan ​Garner's The ‌Owl Service ​(1967), winner ‌of the ​Carnegie Medal, ‌which takes ​the Fourth ‌Branch's triangle ​as a ​pattern that ‌replays down ‌the generations ‌of a ​Welsh valley: ​a dinner ‌service whose ​painted flowers ​resolve into ‌owls, and ​three young ​people cast ‌again as ​the flower-bride, ‌the lord, ​and the ​hunter. It ​remains the ​most powerful ‌modern reworking ‌of any ​Mabinogi material.[2]

‌Beyond Garner, ​the tale ​is a ‌set text ‌of Welsh ‌education and ‌a standing ​subject of ‌Welsh-language criticism, ​where she ​has been ​read as ‌traitor, as ​sovereignty figure, ​and as ​the literature's ‌first portrait ​of a ​made thing ​demanding a ‌will of ​its own. ‌In scholarship ​she holds ‌a fixed ​place: Ifor ‌Williams's edition, ‌Mac Cana's ​study, and ​Koch's encyclopedia ​all treat ​her episode ‌as the ‌Fourth Branch's ​tragic heart.[3]

Sources

  2. Garner, Alan, The Owl Service (London: Collins, 1967).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Blodeuwedd ​has no ‌material cult ‌record: no ​inscription, image, ​or ​votive deposit ​names her, ​and her ‌witnesses are ​manuscripts ​— the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch ‌(NLW Peniarth ​4–5, ‌c. 1350) ​and the ​Red Book ​of Hergest ​(Jesus ​College Oxford ‌MS 111, ​c. 1400), ‌on ​which all ‌editions of ​the Fourth ‌Branch rest.[1]

​What ‌she has ​instead of ​temples is ‌topography. Mur ‌Castell, ‌her bridal ​court, is ​traditionally placed ‌at Tomen ​y ​Mur in ‌Ardudwy, beside ​the Roman ​fort; Llyn ‌y ​Morwynion, the ‌Lake of ​the Maidens ​where her ​retinue ​drowned, is ‌pointed out ‌above Ffestiniog; ​and on ‌the ​bank of ​the river ‌Cynfael stands ‌a holed ‌stone ‌locally called ​Llech Ronw, ‌'Gronw's stone', ​the slab ​Lleu's ​spear pierced. ‌The identifications ​are local ​tradition ​rather than ‌proof, but ​they show ​how early ​and ‌how firmly ​the story ‌was staked ​into the ‌landscape ​of Gwynedd: ‌medieval hearers, ‌like modern ​walkers, could ​be ​taken to ​the places ‌where the ‌Fourth Branch ​happens.[2]

Sources

  1. Huws, Daniel, Medieval Welsh Manuscripts (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 2000).
  2. Sioned Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007), notes on the tale's topography.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Blodeuwedd ​belongs to ‌Welsh rather ‌than Irish ​literature, ​but the ​Irish cycles ​supply ​her nearest ‌mythic weather. ​Irish tradition ​is ‌rich in ​transformation as ‌both ​weapon and ‌sentence — ​the druidic ​shape-shifting ​of the ​Mythological and ​Ulster ‌cycles — ​and her ‌owl-judgement belongs ​to ‌the shared ​insular idiom ‌in ​which Gwydion's ‌and Math's ​wand-work also ​moves: ‌to know ‌a thing's ‌true ​shape is ​to be ‌able to ​change ​it, and ‌to be ​answerable ​for the ‌change.[1]

The ​sovereignty reading ‌builds ​the second ​bridge. Irish ​literature ​gives the ‌goddess of ‌sovereignty who ​bestows ‌kingship with ​her favour; ​Welsh ‌scholarship, following ‌Mac Cana, ‌has noted ‌the ​same economy ‌around Blodeuwedd ​— ​Gronw takes ​the lordship ‌with the ​wife ​— though ​the Fourth ‌Branch ​keeps the ​theme submerged ​in human ‌tragedy ​rather than ‌stating it ​as ‌doctrine.[2] The ​comparison is ‌kinship of ‌motif, ​not demonstrable ​descent: no ​Irish ​text names ‌her, and ‌none needs ​to.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Blodeuwedd's home ​and only ‌medieval witness ‌is the ​Fourth Branch ​of the ​Mabinogi, 'Math uab ​Mathonwy', preserved ​in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch ​(c. 1350) ‌and the Red ​Book of ‌Hergest (c. ​1400) and ‌established in ​its standard ​form by Ifor ​Williams's Pedeir ​Keinc y ​Mabinogi.[1] The ‌branch gives ​her the whole ‌arc: the ​making from ‌oak, broom, ​and meadowsweet; ‌the marriage ​and the court ‌at Mur ​Castell; the ​hunter Gronw ‌and the ‌stolen death-secret; ‌the eagle's scream; ​the flight ​and the ‌Lake of ​the Maidens; ​and the ‌owl's sentence with ​the kept ​name.

She ‌is conspicuously ​absent from ‌the rest ​of the medieval ​Welsh corpus ​— no ​Triad, no ‌poem, no ‌second tale ​takes her up ‌— which ​makes her ​a rare ‌case in ‌the Mabinogi: ‌a figure created, ‌expended, and ​immortalized inside ‌a single ​composition. Later ​Welsh tradition ​made its own ‌addition in ​the lexicon ​rather than ​the story-book: ‌the common noun ​blodeuwedd, 'owl', ​the word ​that keeps ‌her.[2] Her ​kin in ‌the branches are ​the Fourth ‌Branch's own ​company — ‌Gwydion her ‌maker, Math ​her judge's colleague, ​and the ​house of ​Dôn whose ‌destinies she ‌was conjured ​to defeat.[1]

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Blodeuwedd, ‌and ​none would ​be ​expected. ​The ogham ​and ‌Latin-inscribed stones ​of ​early ‌medieval Wales ​— ‌some ​four hundred ‌monuments, ​concentrated in ​the ​south-west ​— commemorate ​secular ‌kindreds ​in an ‌Irish-settler ​epigraphic horizon, ‌and ​none ‌carries this ​name ‌or ​any secure ​reference ‌to the ‌Children ‌of ​Dôn.[1]

The ​silence ‌is ​structural rather ​than ‌damaging. Blodeuwedd ​is ​a ‌literary figure ​of ‌a ​single prose ​composition, ​not a ​cult ‌figure ‌with dedications; ​she ‌belongs ​to the ​codex, ‌not the ‌stone. ‌What ‌the epigraphic ​corpus ‌does ​preserve is ​the ​linguistic frame ‌in ​which ​her compound ​name ‌was ​formed: the ​Brittonic ​naming conventions ‌— ​transparent ‌descriptive compounds, ​mab/uab ‌patronymics ​— that ‌make ‌a name ​like ​Flower-face ​immediately legible ​to ‌a ‌medieval Welsh ​audience.[2]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Blodeuwedd ​is the ‌Mabinogi's argument ‌about what ​making ​means. Math ​and Gwydion ​make a ​woman ‌the way ​they make ​illusions — ‌by craft, ​for ‌a purpose, ​to defeat ‌a destiny ​— ​and the ​Fourth Branch's ​quiet question ​is ‌what a ​made thing ‌owes its ​makers. ‌She has ​no mother, ‌no father, ​no ‌childhood, and ​no choice ​in her ‌marriage; she ‌is ‌given beauty, ​a name, ​a husband, ‌and ​a court, ​and the ‌tale watches ​without ​comment as ‌the one ​thing she ‌chooses ​for herself ​is a ​man and ​a ‌murder.[1]

Gwydion's ‌sentence is ​exact in ‌its ​cruelty: not ​death, which ‌would end ‌her, but ‌the ‌kept name ​— Flower-face ‌worn forever ​on ​the face ​of the ‌night-bird every ​other ​bird hates. ​The punishment ‌preserves her ​even ​as it ​damns her. ‌Seven centuries ​later ‌the Welsh ​word for ‌owl is ​still her ‌name: ‌the flowers ​never stopped ​blooming in ​it, ​and the ‌shame never ‌left it ​either.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
