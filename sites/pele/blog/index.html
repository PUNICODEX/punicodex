The Canoe from Kahiki (Migration)

Pele came to Hawai'i from Kahiki — the ancestral homeland the Hawaiians left in the ​deep past — voyaging in a canoe with her brothers and sisters. Behind her she left a feud: her sister Nāmakaokaha'i, a sea goddess, held Pele to account for a seduction in the ‌old country, and the ocean pursued the canoe across the archipelago. At island after island Nāmaka caught up, and Pele's fires were quenched in steam; in the long versions the sea caught her ​at last and tore her apart, and only her bones, gathered by her brothers, were carried on to be made alive again. (Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology; Westervelt, Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes.) The migration myth ‌makes the goddess an exile: Hawai'i is not where Pele was born but where she finally outran the sea.

The Digging Stick Pā'oa (The Search)

From island to island Pele dug with her ​sacred digging stick Pā'oa, sinking deep pits in search of a fire-pit the sea's creatures could not kill. Kaua'i, O'ahu, the channels of Maui — at each, the water found her out and ‌drove her on, the steam of her drowning fires rising as the islands' mists. At last, on the great island of Hawai'i, she sank her pit into the flank of Kīlauea, and the ​sea could not reach it. There she built her house, Halema'uma'u — 'house of the ʻāma'u fern' — and there she has lived ever since, adding land to the island at every ‌eruption. (Westervelt, Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes; Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology.) The myth reads the archipelago's volcanology as a house-hunt: every crater on the chain is a rejected rental, and Hawai'i the only one whose ​landlord is deeper than the ocean.

Hi'iaka and the Chief of Kaua'i (The Epic)

Pele's youngest sister, Hi'iaka-i-ka-poli-o-Pele, was her favorite — kept in her bosom, raised as guardian of the hula. Gazing ‌in vision across the sea, Pele fell in love with Lohi'au, a chief of Kaua'i, and sent Hi'iaka to fetch him with power and a strict command: a set term, and do not ​embrace him. Hi'iaka crossed the islands, fought the mo'o guardians of the forests, and raised Lohi'au from the death of longing — then returned past her deadline to find that Pele, in jealous ​rage, had consumed Lohi'au in lava and turned Hi'iaka's beloved friend Hopoe to stone on the shore. Hi'iaka's fury shook Kīlauea itself; in most tellings the sisters reconcile, Lohi'au's spirit is brought back ‌from Milu, the underworld, and the epic closes with the hula schools confirmed in Pele's name. (Emerson, Pele and Hiiaka; Nogelmeier, The Epic Tale of Hi'iakaikapoliopele.) The tragedy turns on a command obeyed ‌too well: loyalty punished, love turned to ash — and the dance born as the peace treaty.
