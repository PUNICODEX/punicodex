01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Bragi (bragi) ​— Poetry, ‌Eloquence · ‌The Skald Among ​the Gods ​— is ​the Norse ​god of poetry ​and of ‌the well-turned ​word: husband of ​Iðunn, the ‌voice Snorri ​seats beside ‌Ægir to narrate ​the myths, ‌and the ​god Óðinn sends ​out to ​greet dead ​kings at the ​door of ‌Valhǫll.[1]

His ​attestation is ‌a voice rather ​than an ‌adventure. Snorri's ​catalogue of the ‌Æsir praises ​him as ‌renowned for wisdom ​and most ​of all ‌for eloquence ‌and skill with ‌words, and ​derives the ​word bragr — ‌'poetry', and ​'foremost' — ​from his ‌name; Grímnismál's litany ​of the ​world's best ‌things calls him ​the best ‌of skalds.[1][2] ​The Prose Edda's ​own frame ​makes him ​the gods' ‌storyteller, and the ‌court poems ​Hákonarmál and ‌Eiríksmál give him ​the solemn ​office of ‌welcoming the ‌slain into Óðinn's ‌hall.[3]

PuniCodex ‌restores the ​name as Bragi ‌— identical ​to the ​ASCII input, because ​normalized Old ‌Norse writes ​no diacritic ​in this name. ​The mechanical ‌rule therefore ​places it in ​Tier 2: ​nothing distinctive ‌is lost in ​transit, a ‌statement about ​orthography and ‌never about the ​god. No ‌domain is ‌claimed: the plain-ASCII ​bragi.com is ​registered to ​a third ​party outside the ‌collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[4]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ‌Bragi (nominative; genitive ​Braga). It ​is bound to ​the Old Norse ​poetic noun bragr, ​which carries two ‌senses at once: ​'poetry', and 'foremost, ​chief, the ‌best' — whence ​the lexicon's gloss ‌'Poet, first'.[1] Snorri ​runs the derivation ‌from the god ​outward: from Bragi's ​name bragr ​is called, and ​from it a ​man or woman ‌who surpasses others ​in eloquence is ‌a bragr-maðr or ​bragr-kona.[2] Modern etymologists ‌are less ​certain of the ‌direction, and the ​deeper question — ‌whether the god ​was made out ​of the noun, ‌or a ‌historical skald deified ‌into it — ​is registered rather ​than settled by ‌Simek and Lindow.[3]

​The ASCII form ​is not ‌a compromise here: ​normalized Old Norse ​Bragi is already ‌written in plain ​ASCII letters. There ‌is no diacritic, ​no thorn or ​eth, no ​vowel-length mark for ​the early domain-name ‌system to have ‌stripped. The name ​is classified Tier ‌2 by the ​project's mechanical ​rule — the ‌restoration preserves nothing ‌the ASCII form ‌loses — which ‌is a statement ​about orthography, never ‌about the ​god Grímnismál calls ​the best of ​skalds.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • ​b → B ​— Same, capitalized

​- r → ‌r — ​Same

  • a ​→ a — ​Same
  • g ‌→ g — ​Same
  • i ‌→ i — ​Same

The ‌project holds no ​domain for this ‌name: bragi.com is ‌registered to a ​third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is ​watched for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈbrɑ.ɣi/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Bra- — ‌Voiced bilabial stop ​[b], trilled ​[r], and ‌the short ​open back ‌[ɑ]; the ​syllable carries ‌the stress, ​as on ​every Old Norse ​first syllable

​- -gi ​— The ‌medial g ​softens between ‌vowels to ​the voiced ‌velar fricative [ɣ], ​a light ‌'gh' rather ​than a ‌stop, closing ​on short ​[i]

For ‌the modern ‌speaker, the ‌closest approximation is: ​'BRAH-ghi' — ​a short ‌open 'ah', ​the g ​softened to ‌a gentle ​fricative between ​the vowels.

‌Kindred and historical ​forms of ‌the name:

​- Old ​Norse — ​Bragi, genitive ​Braga; beside ‌the common ‌noun bragr, ​'poetry; foremost'

  • ‌Modern Icelandic ​— Bragi, ​the unbroken ‌living form ‌as a ‌male given ‌name
  • ​Norwegian — ‌Brage, as in ​the Brage ​Prize for ​literature
  • ‌Latinized — ​Brago, the ​form Saxo ​Grammaticus writes

‌Bragi is Tier ​2 by ​the mechanical ​rule: the ‌normalized Old ​Norse form ‌is already ​plain ASCII ‌— no ​diacritic and no ‌distinctive letter ‌for the ​ASCII alphabet ​to lose. ​Only the ​capital distinguishes ‌the restored ‌form from ​the input.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Bragi ​— Old ​Norse (North ‌Germanic), attested ​13th c. ​CE in ‌the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Poetic ​Edda and the ‌Codex Regius ​of the ​Prose Edda, ​recording material ​centuries older.[1] ​Before the ‌Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ‌names were ​carved in ‌the younger ​futhark; no ‌runic attestation ​of this theonym ‌survives, so ​the manuscript ​form is ‌the primary ‌witness.

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds step ​by step:

‌- The ​name is ​attested as ‌Bragi (nominative; ​genitive Braga), ​bound to ‌the poetic noun ​bragr, 'poetry; ‌foremost'.[2]

  • ​Old Norse ​orthography here ​writes no ​long vowel ‌and no ‌special letter: ​there is ‌no acute ​accent and ​no thorn ‌or eth ‌to restore.

‌- In the ‌Younger Futhark ​the name ‌would have ​been carved ​ᛒᚱᛅᚴᛁ (b-r-a-k-i, ​the ᚴ ‌rune writing ​g as ​well as ​k) — ‌a normalized ​reconstruction, not ​an attested ​carving.

  • The ‌Unicode restoration ​Bragi therefore ‌differs from ​the ASCII ‌input only ​in its ‌capital: the ‌name is ​plain ASCII ​in its ​medieval form, ​and Tier ‌2 by ‌the project's ​mechanical rule.[3]

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Bragi is ​the god ‌of poetry and ‌eloquence — ​and, characteristically for ​the North, ​of the offices ​eloquence performs: ​the telling of ‌the myths, ​the toasting of ​vows, and ‌the welcoming of ​the dead.[1]

‌### Poetry

Grímnismál ​44 calls ‌him the best ​of skalds, ​in the same ​breath that ​names Sleipnir best ​of horses ‌and Bifrǫst ​of bridges; Snorri ‌derives the ​very word bragr, ‌'poetry', from ​his name.[2]

‌Eloquence

Snorri's ​catalogue portrait praises ‌his wisdom ​and his skill ​with words ‌above all, and ‌coins the ‌compliment that survives ​him: the ​bragr-maðr and bragr-kona, ‌the man ​or woman of ​surpassing eloquence.[1]

‌### The Storyteller's ​Office

In ​Skáldskaparmál's frame, the ‌sea-god Ægir ​sits beside Bragi ‌at the ​feast in Ásgarðr, ​and the ​myths of the ​mead of ‌poetry and of ‌Iðunn's abduction ​are told in ‌Bragi's voice ​— the Prose ​Edda's own ‌image of where ‌tradition lives.[3]

‌### The Greeter ‌of Kings

​In Hákonarmál, Óðinn ‌sends Bragi ​and Hermóðr out ​to greet ​the slain Hákon ‌the Good ​as he ​approaches Valhǫll; in ​Eiríksmál, Bragi ‌is the voice ​that asks ​Óðinn why the ​bravest kings ‌must fall.[4]

​Husband of ‌Iðunn

Skáldskaparmál's first ​kenning for ‌him is 'husband ​of Iðunn' ‌— the god ‌of the ​word wedded to ​the goddess ​of the ever-young ​apples, poetry ‌married to what ‌keeps the ​gods alive.[3]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Bragi's iconography ​is literary ‌rather than ‌cultic — ​the corpus ​gives him ​no temple ​regalia, but ​the tradition ‌fixed a ​small set ​of attributes ‌around his ​voice:[1]

  • ‌The mead ​of poetry ‌— the ​blood-and-honey mead ​brewed from ​wise Kvasir, ​hoarded by ​the giant ‌Suttungr and stolen ​by Óðinn: ‌the myth ​of poetry's ‌origin that ​Bragi himself ‌narrates to ​Ægir in ‌Skáldskaparmál[2]
  • ​The bragarfull ​— the ‌'foremost cup' ‌drained with ‌binding vows ​at the ​great feasts, ‌named from ​the same ​word as ‌the god: ​the spoken ​word made ‌contractual over drink[3]

​- The ‌long beard ​— skegg-Bragi: ​Skáldskaparmál's kenning ​calls him ​the long-bearded ‌god, the ‌old skald's ​visible mark ‌of office[2]
​- The ​bench in ‌Ægir's hall ‌— the ‌storyteller's seat ‌at the ​feast, where ‌Bragi both ​bars Loki ​in Lokasenna ​and narrates ‌the myths in ​the Prose ​Edda's frame[4]
​- The ‌greeting at ​Valhǫll's door ​— the ​welcome spoken ‌over dead ​kings: praise ‌as the ​North's answer ‌to mortality[5]

​The harp ‌of the ‌familiar nineteenth-century ​images is ​a Romantic ​addition; the ​Eddas give ‌him no ‌instrument, only ​the voice.[1]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Bragi acts in ​almost nothing and speaks ‌in everything: his mythology ‌is a voice — ​the catalogue portrait, the ​storytelling frame, the flyting ​answer, and the welcome ​at the door of ​the dead.

The ‌Catalogue Portrait (Gylfaginning 26)

​Snorri numbers him among the ​Æsir with the formula ‌the tradition never varies: ​renowned for wisdom and ‌most of all for ​eloquence and skill with ‌words, knowing more of ​poetry than any other; ​from his name bragr ​is called, and his ​wife is Iðunn.[1] Grímnismál's litany ​seals it in verse ‌— Bragi is best ​of skalds.[2]

The ‌Gods' Storyteller (Skáldskaparmál)

When ​Ægir came to the ‌feast in Ásgarðr he ​was seated beside Bragi, ‌and the myths they ​talked over are the ones ‌the book exists to ​record: the truce-spittle that ​became Kvasir, the dwarfs' ‌brewing of his blood ‌with honey into the ‌mead that makes a ​skald, the mead's capture ​by Suttungr, and Óðinn's ‌theft of Óðrerir in ​three gulps as Bǫlverkr.[3] Bragi ​also tells how the ‌giant Þjazi, in eagle ​shape, carried off Iðunn ​and her apples of ‌youth, and how Loki ​won her back. When ‌the book later asks ​how Bragi himself shall ​be kennt, the answer ​is his identity in one ​line: husband of Iðunn, ‌first maker of poetry, ‌the long-bearded god, and ​son of Óðinn.[3]

‌The Flyting (Lokasenna 11–18)

​When Loki forced his ​way into Ægir's feast, ‌Bragi was the first ‌to refuse him a ‌seat; when Loki's flyting began, ‌he taunted the god ​of the word as ‌the most cowardly of ​the Æsir in war, ​shyest of the bow-shot. ​Bragi answered in kind, ‌and it was Iðunn ​who did the scene's ​true eloquent work, calming her ​husband before the quarrel ‌ran further.[4]

The ​Greeter of Dead Kings ​(Hákonarmál · Eiríksmál)

Eyvindr ​skáldaspillir's funeral poem for ‌Hákon the Good (c. ​961) gives Bragi his ‌solemn office: Óðinn sends ​'Hermóðr and Bragi' to ‌meet the slain king coming ​to Valhǫll. In the ‌anonymous Eiríksmál, woven for ‌Eiríkr Bloodaxe, Bragi asks ​Óðinn why he robbed ​the bravest king of ​victory — and receives ​the corpus's coldest answer: ‌the grey wolf gapes ‌ever at the dwellings ​of the gods.[5]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The honest ​comparisons for ‌Bragi are ‌typological, not genealogical. ​His closest ​structural kin ​in the neighbouring ​tradition is ​the Irish Ogma ‌of the ​Tuatha Dé ​Danann — the ‌champion of ​eloquence credited ‌with the Ogham ​script: two ‌Indo-European cultures ​that each enthroned ​the well-spoken ​word among their ​gods, with ​no borrowing ‌argued or needed.[1] ​The Greek ‌comparison is ​weaker: Bragi is ‌no Apollo ​— he ‌leads no choir ​and prophesies ‌nothing — and ​the North ​already assigned ‌the conquest of ‌the poetic ‌mead to ​Óðinn, leaving Bragi ​the distinct ‌office of ​the professional praiser.

​The deepest ‌debate is internal. ​Since the ​nineteenth century ‌scholars have asked ​whether the ‌god is ​the deified Bragi ​Boddason, the ​ninth-century Norwegian ​skald whose Ragnarsdrápa ‌Skáldskaparmál quotes ‌stanza by stanza; ​the handbooks ‌register the ​theory without settling ​it.[2] Simek ‌notes the ‌colder alternative — ‌that Bragi ‌may be ​a late literary ‌personification of ​bragr, poetry itself, ​with no ​old cult ‌behind him — ​and Lindow ​treats him ​as essentially a ‌figure of ​the poetic ​tradition he personifies.[2] ​Within the ‌corpus, closely related ​figures include ‌Óðinn (the ​mead's winner and, ‌per Snorri's ​kenning, his ‌father), Iðunn (his ‌wife), and ​Sága (the ​other divine figure ​bound to ​story and drink, ‌who shares ‌Óðinn's cups ​at Sǫkkvabekkr).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Bragi's ​afterlife lives ‌in the ‌language itself. ​Bragr ​remained the ​Old Norse ​word for ​poetry; ‌the bragr-maðr ​and bragr-kona ​kept his ‌name ​in everyday ‌compliment; and ​the bragarfull ‌— ​the foremost ​cup of ​the vow-toast ​that ​Ynglinga saga ‌sets at ​the great ‌inheritance ​feasts — ‌carried the ​word into ‌the ​drinking customs ‌of medieval ​Scandinavia.[1] Revived ​in ‌the sumbel ‌of modern ‌Heathen practice, ​the ​custom still ‌opens with ​a hail ​to ‌Bragi as ​patron of ​poets before ‌the ​boasting rounds ‌begin.

The ​name never ​died ​in the ​North: Bragi ‌remains a ‌living ​Icelandic given ‌name, Brage ​its Norwegian ​and ‌Swedish form, ‌and Norway's ‌premier literary ‌award, ​the Brage ‌Prize, has ​borne it ​since ​1992.[2] The ‌visual afterlife ​is later ​and ​thinner: the ‌white-bearded Bragi ​with a ​harp ​who appears ‌in nineteenth-century ​Scandinavian painting ‌and ​sculpture is ‌a Romantic ​invention, answering ‌the ‌period's hunger ​for a ​northern bard ​— ​the Eddas ‌knew him ‌by voice ​alone.[3]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No ​cult site, ‌votive deposit, ‌or named ​image of ​Bragi is ​known, and ​the place-name ​record offers ‌no secure ​theophoric site ​— the ‌silence that ​leads ‌the handbooks ​to weigh ‌him as ​a late ​literary personification ​rather than ​an old ​cult figure.[1] ‌The runic ​corpus knows ‌the name ​only ‌as an ​ordinary human ‌anthroponym: Bragi ​is well ‌attested as ​a Viking-Age ​man's name ‌in Scandinavian ‌inscriptions, which ‌is precisely ​the problem ​for cult-hunters, ‌since ​no carving ​can be ‌tied to ​the god.[2]

​The witnesses ‌are therefore ​the manuscripts. ‌Lokasenna and ​Grímnismál survive ​in the ​thirteenth-century Codex ​Regius ‌of the ‌Poetic Edda; ​Gylfaginning and ‌the Skáldskaparmál ​frame in ​the Codex ‌Regius and ‌sister manuscripts ‌of the ‌Prose Edda, ​with Skáldatal ‌in the ​Uppsala ​codex; Hákonarmál ​rides inside ‌the Heimskringla ​tradition and ​Eiríksmál inside ​Fagrskinna.[3] The ‌material corollary ​is negative ​but telling: ​where Þórr's ‌hammer-pendants survive ​by ‌the thousand, ​no object ‌in the ​ground bears ‌Bragi's mark ‌— his ​medium was ​never metal ​or stone, ​but the ‌trained voice ‌at the ​feast.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌knows Bragi ‌as a ​voice ​at the ​feast and ​a name ​in the ‌litany. ​Lokasenna 11–18 ​gives him ‌his one ​staged ‌scene: he ​is the ‌first of ​the Æsir ​to ​bar Loki ​from Ægir's ​drinking, the ‌first ​target of ‌the flyting ​that follows ‌— ​taunted as ‌the most ​war-shy of ‌the gods ​— ​and the ‌husband Iðunn ‌rises to ‌calm.[1] ​The poem ​keeps him ‌exactly where ​the tradition ​always ‌keeps him: ​at the ​bench, in ‌the ​war of ‌words.

Grímnismál ​44 supplies ​the ​verdict in ​the corpus's ‌most compressed ‌form. In ​Óðinn's ‌litany of ​the world's ​best things ‌— ‌Sleipnir best ‌of horses, ‌Bifrǫst of ​bridges, Bragi ‌of ​skalds — ​the god ​of poetry ‌takes ​his place ​among the ​superlatives that ‌structure ​the cosmos.[2] ​It is ​the Edda's ‌whole theology ​of ‌the name ​in four ‌words: poetry ​is ‌not a ‌pastime the ​gods tolerate ​but one ​of ​the excellences ‌the world ‌is made ​of.

Sources

  1. Lokasenna 11–18 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Grímnismál 44 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Bragi's ‌great witness ​— ​doubly, since ​it makes ​him both ​subject and ‌speaker. ​Gylfaginning 26 ​gives the ‌fixed portrait: ​renowned ‌for wisdom, ​pre-eminent in ‌eloquence and ​skill with ​words, ​knowing more ​of poetry ​than any; ‌from ​his name ‌bragr is ​called, whence ‌the ​bragr-maðr and ‌bragr-kona; his ​wife is ‌Iðunn.[1]

Skáldskaparmál ​then ​builds its ‌entire frame ‌around his ‌voice. ​Ægir at ​the feast ‌in Ásgarðr ​is seated ​beside ‌Bragi, and ​the treatise's ​foundational myths ‌are ​delivered as ‌Bragi's table-talk: ​the truce-spittle ​that ​became Kvasir, ​the brewing ‌of the ‌poetic mead, ​Suttungr's ‌hoarding and ​Óðinn's theft, ​and — ‌told ‌by the ‌husband himself ‌— Þjazi's ​abduction of ‌Iðunn ​and her ​recovery.[2] When ​the book ‌asks ​how Bragi ​shall be ​kennt, the ‌answer ​distils him: ​husband of ​Iðunn, first ‌maker of ​poetry, ‌the long-bearded ​god, son ‌of Óðinn.[2] ​No ‌other god ‌in the ​corpus is ​so literally ​the ​mouthpiece of ‌the book ‌that preserves ​him.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 26, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains no ‌invocation ​of the ​god Bragi. ​No ​runestone asks ​his favour, ‌no ​amulet bears ​his name, ‌and no ​inscription ‌yet found ​links a ‌dedicant ​to his ​cult — ​a ​silence he ​shares with ‌most ​of the ‌younger Æsir, ​and ‌one that ​weighs in ‌the ​debate over ‌whether he ​is ​an old ‌cult god ‌or a ‌literary ​personification of ​bragr.[1]

What ‌the ​inscriptions do ​preserve is ‌the ​name itself, ​as an ‌ordinary ​human anthroponym: ‌Bragi is ​well ​attested as ​a Viking-Age ​man's ‌name in ‌the Scandinavian ​runic ‌record, which ​is precisely ​the problem ‌— ‌none of ‌the carvings ‌can ​be tied ‌to the ​god ​rather than ​to a ‌farmer.[2] ​The theonym's ​bond with ​the ‌common noun ​bragr, 'poetry; ​foremost', ​suggests how ‌such a ​name ‌could pass ​between the ‌human name-pool ​and ‌the divine ‌register: a ​word ​for the ​best, worn ​first ‌by men ‌and then ​enthroned.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Bragi.
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. Bragi, bragr.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Bragi's ‌medieval afterlife belongs ‌to the ​court poems the ​kings' sagas ​preserve. Eyvindr skáldaspillir's ​Hákonarmál, composed for ​the funeral ‌of Hákon the ​Good around ​961 and quoted ‌in Hákonar ​saga góða, gives ‌him his ​solemn office: as ‌the slain ​king approaches with ​his host, ​Óðinn sends 'Hermóðr ​and Bragi' ​out to greet ‌him — the ​rider and ‌the word, dispatched ​together to ‌the door of ​Valhǫll.[1] The ‌anonymous Eiríksmál, woven ​for Eiríkr ‌Bloodaxe and preserved ​in Fagrskinna, ​makes Bragi the ‌questioner within ‌the hall: why ‌did Óðinn ​rob the bravest ​king of victory? ‌The answer ​— the grey ​wolf gapes ‌ever at the ​gods' dwellings ​— is the ‌corpus's coldest ​sentence, and it ‌is spoken ​to Bragi because ​asking why ​the great die ​is the ‌skald's oldest job.[2]

‌Ynglinga saga keeps ​his word ‌at the table: ​at the ​great inheritance feasts, ‌heirs drank ‌the bragarfull, the ‌foremost cup, ‌and spoke binding ​vows over ‌it — the ​toast that ​carried the god's ​name into ‌the ceremonial grammar ​of the Viking ​Age.[3] And ​Skáldatal, preserved with ‌the Uppsala ​codex of Snorri's ​Edda, records ​the name's other ‌bearer: Bragi ​Boddason the Old, ‌the ninth-century ​Norwegian skald of ‌Ragnarr loðbrók ​whose Ragnarsdrápa Skáldskaparmál ‌quotes — ‌the historical poet ​behind the standing ​theory that ​the god is ​a skald ‌the tradition could ‌not bear ​to bury.[4]

  1. Eyvindr Finnsson skáldaspillir, Hákonarmál, in Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Hákonar saga góða, ed. Bjarni Aðalbjarnarson, Íslenzk fornrit 26 (1941).
  2. Eiríksmál (anonymous), in Fagrskinna, ed. Bjarni Einarsson, Íslenzk fornrit 29 (1985).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglinga saga (the bragarfull), Íslenzk fornrit 26 (1941).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Bragi ​is the ‌god a ‌culture of ​oral ​memory had ​to invent. ​In a ​world ‌without archives, ​the skald ​was the ‌archive: ​the king's ‌deeds, the ​clan's honour, ‌the ​boundary between ​being remembered ​and being ​nothing ​all ran ‌through one ​trained voice ‌at ​the feast.[1] ‌The North ​looked at ‌that ​voice and ‌saw a ​god in ​it ‌— and ‌then put ‌the god ​to ​work at ‌the door ​of the ​dead, ‌because the ​only immortality ​the Edda ‌truly ​promises is ‌the welcome ​spoken over ​you ​when you ​arrive.

The ‌division of ‌labour ​is the ‌deepest thing ​the tradition ​says ‌about poetry. ‌Óðinn wins ‌the mead ‌— ​by disguise, ‌seduction, and ​theft — ​but ​Bragi pours ‌it. Inspiration ​is ordeal; ​eloquence ​is hospitality: ‌the craft ​of receiving ​greatness ​and giving ‌it words. ​The god ‌whose ​name means ‌'foremost' is ​the one ‌who ‌stands where ​the living ​greet the ​dead ​and finds ‌the sentence ‌both can ​bear.

Sources

  1. Eyvindr skáldaspillir, Hákonarmál, in Heimskringla, Hákonar saga góða; Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Bragi.
