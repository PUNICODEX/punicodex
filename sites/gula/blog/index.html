Bulluṭsa-rabi Sings Her ​Praise (The Great Hymn)

The most famous poem to Gula is the long hymn of the devotee Bulluṭsa-rabi ('O Great Lady, soothe my heart'), known from dozens of Old Babylonian and later ​copies. Line by line it outranks her against the other great goddesses — her power exceeds theirs, her command governs life and death, and even the gods themselves are her patients. The hymn ‌names her city, Isin, and calls her 'the great healer who revives the dead' — hyperbole that maps her onto no single myth but onto every sickbed. The poem survives in Foster's Before ​the Muses and the Pennsylvania Sumerian/Assyriological corpora. The poet's very name — Bulluṭsa-rabi, 'O Great Lady, soothe my heart' — casts him as a courtier, and the flattery is systematic: each rival ‌goddess is praised at length precisely so that Gula can surpass her. The Prayer of the Sufferer (The Sickbed)

Babylonian penitential psalms give Gula the words of the desperate. One such prayer, 'I kneel before ‌you, daughter of Anu... my affliction is grievous, my wound severe' (Foster, Before the Muses), has the patient catalog his sins before the goddess, begging her to 'look upon me and let your ​heart be soothed.' In Ludlul bēl nēmeqi ('I Will Praise the Lord of Wisdom'), the righteous sufferer's restoration comes through the diviner and exorcist whose rites Gula empowers. The theology is striking for ‌its psychology: illness is no pure accident but a rupture — with the gods, with one's city, with one's own past — so the cure must begin in confession before it can ​proceed to the drug. From Bau to Gula (The Lagash Ancestor)

Before she was Gula of Isin, the healing goddess was Bau of Lagash, daughter of An and wife ​of Ningirsu, celebrated in the inscriptions of Gudea (c. 2125 BCE) and in Sumerian hymns as the compassionate one who hears prayer. As Isin rose under the dynasties of the early second ​millennium, her local goddess Ninisina absorbed Bau's character and acquired the Akkadian epithet Gula, 'The Great One'; her consort became the storm-warrior Ninurta (Jacobsen, The Treasures of Darkness, 1976). Her names multiplied as ‌her cities changed hands: Ninkarrak guarded the northern caravan towns, while other districts knew her as Nintinugga — a theonymic heap that is the fossil record of Mesopotamian geopolitics. A Meditation

Gula embodies a proposition most ancient theologies only imply: that healing is ‌a form of greatness. Her very name is the Akkadian word for 'great' in the feminine — as if the culture had looked for the largest possible title and given it to the ​one who closes wounds. The Babylonian doctor, standing between the drug list and the incantation, addressed her before both, acknowledging that technique without mercy is not medicine. There is something striking in ​a warrior civilization's supreme goddess of care being the wife of its storm-god: the spear and the bandage married. To read her hymns is to watch a civilization think through suffering — not ‌explaining it away, as Job's comforters tried, but giving it a face that looks back, 'soothes the heart,' and sometimes says yes. That is a theology a sickbed can actually use.
