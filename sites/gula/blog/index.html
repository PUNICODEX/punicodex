Bulluṭsa-rabi Sings Her Praise ​(The Great Hymn)

The most famous poem to Gula is the long hymn of the devotee Bulluṭsa-rabi ('O Great Lady, soothe my heart'), known from dozens of Old Babylonian and later copies. ​Line by line it outranks her against the other great goddesses — her power exceeds theirs, her command governs life and death, and even the gods themselves are her patients. The hymn ‌names her city, Isin, and calls her 'the great healer who revives the dead' — hyperbole that maps her onto no single myth but onto every sickbed. The poem survives in Foster's ​Before the Muses and the Pennsylvania Sumerian/Assyriological corpora. The poet's very name — Bulluṭsa-rabi, 'O Great Lady, soothe my heart' — casts him as a courtier, and the flattery is systematic: each ‌rival goddess is praised at length precisely so that Gula can surpass her. From Bau to Gula (The Lagash Ancestor)

Before she was Gula of Isin, the healing goddess was Bau of ​Lagash, daughter of An and wife of Ningirsu, celebrated in the inscriptions of Gudea (c. 2125 BCE) and in Sumerian hymns as the compassionate one who hears prayer. As Isin rose under ​the dynasties of the early second millennium, her local goddess Ninisina absorbed Bau's character and acquired the Akkadian epithet Gula, 'The Great One'; her consort became the storm-warrior Ninurta (Jacobsen, The Treasures ‌of Darkness, 1976). Her names multiplied as her cities changed hands: Ninkarrak guarded the northern caravan towns, while other districts knew her as Nintinugga — a theonymic heap that is the fossil ‌record of Mesopotamian geopolitics.
