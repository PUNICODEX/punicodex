Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is ​/ho.de.ɾi/ — Modern Standard Japanese (Hepburn).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ho- — Voiceless glottal [h] plus open-mid back [o]; from 火 'fire'
  • -de- — Voiced alveolar stop [d] plus close-mid [e]
  • -ri — Alveolar ‌tap [ɾ] plus close front [i]; from 照 'shining'

Mythology

Hoderi's story in the Kojiki is a tale of sibling rivalry, the exchange of gifts, and the transfer ​of sovereignty from the sea to the land. He is the elder son, the firstborn of Ninigi, destined to be bypassed — and Japan's two oldest histories tell his falling-short with different temperaments, ​and the difference is itself instructive.

The Exchange of Gifts (Kojiki)

When Ninigi's sons grew up, he divided the world's livelihood between them: to Hoderi, the elder, he gave the fishhook that rules ​the sea; to Hoori, the younger, the bow and arrow that rule the mountains (Kojiki, age of the gods). Hoori proposed that they exchange — he would try the sea, Hoderi the ​chase — and Hoderi agreed. But the sea resisted the stranger: Hoori fished for days and caught nothing, and in a fury he flung the precious hook into the waves. Hoderi's response was ​absolute: he would sooner lose a kingdom than his hook, and he demanded its return on pain of their brotherhood itself. Cornered, Hoori walked into the sea to answer for the loss. The ‌exchange looks innocent but is the hinge of the whole cycle: a fisherman's tool, lost and owed, becomes the lever that moves sovereignty from elder to younger, from sea to land.

Hoori's ​Triumph (Kojiki)

Beneath the waves Hoori found not punishment but fortune: the palace of the sea god Watatsumi, and marriage to the god's daughter Toyotama-hime. From the sea's depths he sent the lost ‌hook back to land — recovered, in the Kojiki's wry detail, from the throat of a sea bream — bearing a curse that turned against its owner: whatever it touched would fail and ​wither. Struck by the curse, Hoderi saw his stores dwindle and his luck collapse, until he came barefoot to his younger brother, begging terms. Hoori took the submission and the precedence; Hoderi ‌gave him his daughter and his allegiance, and the elder line bent to the younger. The myth's meaning is economical and severe: legitimacy in the age of the gods does not follow birth ​order but the flow of fortune, and the sea, for all its plenty, could not hold the future that was gathering on the land.

Ancestor of the Hayato (Genealogy)

The Kojiki closes Hoderi's ‌line not with extinction but with a dignified office: his descendants are the Hayato, a people of southern Kyūshū remembered in early Japanese records as warriors and as ritual specialists at the imperial ​court. Historically the Hayato appear in the eighth-century chronicles as a military people whose sons served the throne — an echo, scholars have long suspected, of a pre-Yamato population of the south ‌absorbed into the imperial order on terms its own myth explains. The genealogy does double work: it gives the defeated elder brother a posterity, and it gives the victorious court a way to ​honor the people it absorbed, as ritualists rather than rebels. In Hoderi's house the myth means: the bypassed line is not erased but employed — the memory of the elder brother kept on ​the payroll of the younger's descendants.

The Tide Jewels of the Other History (Nihon Shoki)

The Nihon Shoki (720 CE) tells the same feud with different names and sharper weapons: the brothers ‌are called Umisachihiko, Sea-Luck Lad, and Yamasachihiko, Mountain-Luck Lad, and the sea god arms the younger with the two tide jewels — one that floods, one that ebbs. When the elder attacks, the ‌flooding jewel drowns him; when he begs, the ebbing jewel spares him, and he swears service to his brother's line, becoming — the genealogy agrees — ancestor of the Hayato. The Shoki's variant ‌notes are harsher still: in one alternate account the elder brother does not survive the feud at all. Two official histories, two temperaments: the Kojiki converts defeat into service, the Shoki leaves the ​sea prince gasping on the tideline. Read together they show the early court arguing with itself about what to do with the elder line — absorb it lovingly, or drown it and ​call it law.

Archaeology & Evidence

Hoderi has no independent archaeological attestation — no shrine, image, or inscription names him — and his importance is textual and genealogical, preserved in ‌the Kojiki's account of the imperial lineage and in the Nihon Shoki's parallel version. The historical echo of his myth is the documentary record of the Hayato themselves: the early chronicles (Shoku Nihongi ​and its predecessors) register Hayato delegations, military service, and ritual office at court from the eighth century, and southern Kyūshū place names and burial remains attest the ancient populations the genealogy claims ​as his children. The 'evidence' for Hoderi is thus a people remembered, not a stone inscribed.

Across Cultures

Hoderi is primarily a genealogical figure, and his afterlife is interpretive. Shinto scholarship has long read him as the mythic signature of the sea peoples and older lineages ​of southern Japan folded into the Yamato order — his 'Fire-Shining' name suggests a solar brilliance eclipsed by the mountain brother's steady fire. The myth's structure invites comparison with other younger-son-victories in the ​imperial line (Ninigi himself was passed over for rule of heaven in favor of the narrative's landward mission), making Hoderi one link in a chain where sovereignty repeatedly detours around seniority. Buddhist ​and Confucian readers of later centuries found in him a lesson in the submission of fortune to virtue; modern folklorists find an ethnographic memory. He absorbed no foreign cult — the traffic ran ‌the other way, his story naturalizing an absorbed people.

Cultural Legacy

Hoderi is little known outside scholarly and ritual contexts, but his story underlies one of the key ​narratives of Japanese origins: the transfer of legitimacy from sea to land, from elder to younger, and the inclusion of older peoples within the imperial frame. His name survives in Kojiki retellings, in ‌schoolbook summaries of the age of the gods, and in the quiet pride of communities in southern Kyūshū whose traditions remember the Hayato. For modern readers he is the necessary counterweight to the ​imperial success story — the elder brother whose loss makes the victory legible, and whose descendants' service reminds us that Japanese identity was assembled, not simply inherited.

A Meditation

Hoderi is the one who was passed over. Every national mythology crowns a winner; few pause over the brother who held the older claim. His story asks us to ‌notice the elder — the sea, the firstborn, the rival who does not win — and to see how myths of defeat are woven into national identity. The Kojiki is gentle with ‌him: he keeps his life, his daughter marries into the victor's house, his descendants hold honored office at the court that displaced him. Defeat, properly mythologized, becomes service; the bypassed line becomes the ​ritual memory that legitimizes the bypassing. Not every ancestor becomes emperor; some become memory — and memory, as the court understood, can be a more durable inheritance than the throne itself.
