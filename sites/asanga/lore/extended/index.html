Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Modern ASCII asanga Plain-ASCII fallback

The name Asanga reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the buddhist tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Asanga in Later Traditions

Yogācāra's fate was absorption: too useful to defeat, it was eaten by its rivals. In India, the great synthesis of the 8th century married Asanga's psychology to Nāgārjuna's emptiness — Śāntarakṣita's Madhyāmakālaṃkāra declared the two schools two doors into one house, a verdict all later Indian Buddhism accepted. In East Asia, Yogācāra merged with Huayan's cosmos and Tiantai's Lotus hermeneutics, surviving explicitly only in Japan's Hossō school while secretly structuring everything else. In Tibet, Asanga is 'the two brothers' half of every curriculum — the Abhisamayālaṃkāra is the master commentary on the Prajñāpāramitā that Nāgārjuna supposedly rescued from the sea. The modern meeting is stranger: William James and the Yogācārins now read each other — 'pure experience' and 'mind-only' are neighbors across a century, and the ālayavijñāna reappears in cognitive science as 'predictive processing.' The school named 'practice' became, in the end, the theory everyone practices against.

Modern Legacy

Asanga's legacy is institutional and conceptual at once. Nālandā, where his lineage taught for seven centuries, is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the name of India's modern flagship university; the Tibetan monastic curriculum still opens philosophy with his Abhisamayālaṃkāra, memorized line by line across every shedra from Lhasa to Mundgod. In East Asia the Faxiang/Hossō line, though small, preserved his texts when India lost them — Xuanzang's rescue of the manuscripts is itself one of the great stories of civilization's luggage. And the concepts migrated far beyond Buddhism: the store-consciousness anticipates the unconscious, the three natures prefigure constructivist accounts of perception, and 'mind-only' is quoted in contemporary philosophy of mind and machine-learning debates alike. A man whose name means 'without attachment' ended up attached to everything.

In the Earth

Asanga left no monument of his own, and his century left few datable ones — the 4th–5th-century Buddhist world of northern India is known mostly through the pilgrim accounts (Faxian, then Xuanzang) and the great monastic cities they describe. Nālandā, excavated by the Archaeological Survey of India from 1915 and now a UNESCO site, preserves the excavated grid of his school's eventual home: eleven monasteries and six temples in brick, the sarai and the stūpas. The Ajanta caves' later viharas (5th–6th c.) show the scholastic monasticism his movement filled — rows of cells, lecture halls, painted jātakas. His true artifacts are textual: the five Maitreya treatises survive in hundreds of Sanskrit fragments and complete Tibetan and Chinese translations, their colophons still arguing about who wrote them — which is, for a school about the construction of authorship, exactly right.

The Meditation

What Asanga means — beyond the dictionary

Asanga's school asks the question most traditions bury: not 'what is the world?' but 'who is building it right now?' His answer — the mind, constructing its world and mistaking the construction for the thing — was psychology three centuries before Augustine and fifteen before the laboratory. But his founding parable keeps the science honest: the vision came not from analysis but from a dying dog, and the first act of the school's founder was to bleed for a stranger's wounds.

There is a lesson in the pairing. Yogācāra's map of mind — seeds, stores, three natures — is as intricate as any in antiquity, yet the tradition insists it was dictated by compassion itself, in the form of Maitreya. Analysis without tenderness, Asanga's life says, is just another attachment — to being right. The name is the teaching: without attachment, even to the map. A psychologist who knew that the last obstacle is the theory of the obstacle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Asanga, Yogacara, Mind-Only, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Asanga?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Asanga is /ˈɐ.sɐŋ.ɡɐ/ — approximately 'UH-sung-guh' — short vowels, even stress; the ṅg is the nasal 'ng' of 'singer,' not 'finger.'.

02What does Asanga mean?

Asanga means Without attachment in the buddhist tradition.

03What are the symbols of Asanga?

Asanga is associated with The wounded dog (The dying dog whose maggots he moved with his tongue — the act of compassion that revealed Maitreya, and the founding parable of Yogācāra: insight comes through the heart, not the head), Maitreya's tower (Tuṣita heaven, where the five treatises were received — Yogācāra's scriptural charter is literally from the future), The mirror (The school's favorite image in later East Asian art — perception like a mirror: images arise, none stick; Asanga's name, 'without attachment,' is the mirror wiped clean), The storehouse (ālaya) (The ālayavijñāna, the warehouse consciousness where seeds of karma sleep and sprout — his school's most influential and most argued concept), The three natures (Imagined, dependent, perfected — Asanga's map of how the mind builds its world and how it sees through the building).

04Why restore Asanga in Unicode?

Asanga is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Asanga?

“Two Brothers, One Teaching” — Asanga's mythology is the hagiography of a psychologist: the vision that founded his school came through compassion for a dog, and his school's charter texts were, by tradition, dictated by the future Buddha — scholarship's verdict is that Asanga wrote them himself, which changes nothing about the parable.
