Two Brothers, One Teaching (The Brothers)

Asanga and his younger half-brother Vasubandhu — arguably the two most consequential Buddhist philosophers after Nāgārjuna — were born, the story runs ​(Xuanzang preserves it in his Great Tang Records of the Western Regions), to a brahmin of Kauśika lineage; their mother, the legend adds, resolved that one son should master the Mahāyāna and ‌the other its rival, the analytical Abhidharma of Kaśmīr. Vasubandhu became the encyclopedist of the Abhidharmakośa; Asanga wandered and meditated until he founded Yogācāra. Then the elder brother executed the gentlest ambush in ​the history of ideas: feigning a mortal illness, he had disciples read him a Mahāyāna sūtra and plant objections that only the Mahāyāna could answer — until the great critic recanted on ‌the spot and joined the school he had spent his career refuting. Vasubandhu's conversion doubled the school's firepower: the Viṃśikā and Trimśikā became its core. The myth explains a real fact: half of ​the Indian Mahāyāna was written by one family — brotherly love as the engine of a philosophical revolution.

Twelve Years, a Wounded Dog, and Maitreya (The Vision)

The founding vision of Yogācāra is ‌a story about compassion first. Asanga, the tradition says (Xuanzang; the Tibetan historians Bu ston and Tāranātha repeat it), spent twelve years in solitary retreat awaiting a vision of the future Buddha ​Maitreya — and saw nothing. Despairing on his way down the mountain, he met a dog dying in the road, its open wounds alive with maggots; to save both creature and worms he ‌cut flesh from his own thigh and reached to lift the maggots with his tongue. The dog vanished: it was Maitreya. The bodhisattva then carried Asanga bodily to Tuṣita heaven, where he received ​the five great treatises — Abhisamayālaṃkāra, Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra, Madhyāntavibhāga, Dharmadharmatāvibhāga, Ratnagotravibhāga. Modern scholarship — Frauwallner most forcefully — argues Asanga wrote them himself. The interpretive point survives either way: a philosophy of mind ‌founded by a man whose breakthrough came through the gut — the dying dog was the last rung of the ladder.

Yogācāra, the Practice of Mind-Only (The School)

What Asanga built — with ​Vasubandhu and their lineage — the tradition called Yogācāra, "practice of yoga," and its opponents called cittamātra, "mind-only." Its masterworks, the Mahāyāna-saṃgraha ("Compendium of the Mahāyāna") and the Abhidharmasamuccaya, set out a psychology ​of awakening: the store-consciousness (ālayavijñāna), a deep repository in which the seeds of deeds and habits sleep, waiting to sprout as the world; the three natures — the imagined (the snake), the ‌dependent (the rope), the perfected (the seeing); and the radical claim that the "external world" is a construction of consciousness, no more independent than a dream is independent of the dreamer. The Saṃdhinirmocana ‌Sūtra supplied the school's scriptural charter, with its doctrine of the three turnings of the wheel. The interpretive meaning: suffering was re-described from metaphysics into psychology — and liberation as a rewiring ‌of perception, perception seen at last as the whole stage.
