Mythology

Nut enters myth chiefly as the forbidden wife: the sky and the earth, she and Geb, were locked in an embrace Shu had ​to tear apart to create the world’s space. Separated, she spends eternity arched over him, swallowing the sun he receives back each dawn — the most elegant creation image Egypt produced.

The ​Embrace of Geb and Nut (Heliopolitan Cosmogony)

Shu and Tefnut, the first air and moisture, bore Geb the earth and Nut the sky — who loved so closely that no light or ​creature could pass between them. On Ra’s command, or by Shu’s own strength in the tellings, the air god forced them apart and holds them apart to this day: the atmosphere itself ‌is the wedge of creation.

The Birth Bricks of the Gods (Pyramid Texts)

The old utterances make Nut the great mother repeatedly: she who bore the gods on her five epagomenal days ​— the days added outside the 360-day year, won for her from the moon in the game of senet. Osiris, Horus, Seth, Isis and Nephthys were born on those five days, ‌the only time the sky goddess could labour.

She Who Gives Birth and Does Not Tire (Book of the Dead, spell 17)

Spell 17 invokes Nut as the sow who eats her ​piglets and the mother who daily bears the sun: ‘I am Nut, the great one who gives birth to the gods.’ The deceased appeals to her body as the ladder of ascent ‌— to be ‘a star imperishable’ among her children was the standard hope of the afterlife.

The Night Barque Within Her (Solar Theology)

In the solar books the sun god’s night journey ​runs through Nut’s interior from mouth to womb; her body is the Duat’s architecture. New Kingdom royal tombs — from Thutmose III to Ramesses VI — paint her figure along the sarcophagus ‌hall so that the king ascends through the goddess herself.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Nwt concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the ​name:

Archaeology & Evidence

Her finest monuments are ​architectural: the ceilings of the Ramesside tombs in the Valley of the Kings and the zodiac ceiling of Dendera, where her body frames the circumpolar stars. The Nut spell (BD 148) was ‌often written on stelae in the shape of the goddess herself, a tree-vault hybrid rising from the sycamore.

Across Cultures

Nut merges readily with Hathor as sky-cow and with the sycamore-goddess of offering scenes; in the late period she absorbs the funerary role of Nut-as-tree. ​Greek authors rendered her as a Titaness-figure comparable to Rhea; her swallowing-and-birthing of the sun invited comparison with the devouring Time of Orphic cosmogonies.

Within the Egyptian tradition, closely related names in ​the corpus include Ꜣb, Ꜣḫ, Ꜣmun, ꜥnḫ, ꜣnpw, and ꜥꜣpp.

Cultural Legacy

Her image — the blue woman bent from horizon to horizon — is the most reproduced Egyptian cosmological image after ‌the weighing of the heart. The five epagomenal days her myth explains still structure the Coptic calendar’s year-end; and ‘Nut, Nut, the sky is falling’ passed into Egyptian Arabic as a ​children’s rhyme.
