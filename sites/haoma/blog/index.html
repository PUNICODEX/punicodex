Haoma Who Was First Pressed (Yašt 9)

The ninth Yašt is the hymn of the plant made god: Haoma, green and with golden eyes, who was first pressed ​on the heights of Hara (Yašt 9.16, 19). The Avesta remembers him as both herb and yazata — the only deity of the Iranian pantheon who is simultaneously the offering itself, pounded ‌fresh on the pestle each night in the Yasna. His first-pressing on Hara Berezaiti, the Alborz, the axis of the world, is the same mountain where Mithra keeps his thousand-eared, thousand-eyed watch ​(Yašt 10.13) — sacrament and covenant share one summit. His color and eyes passed into iconography: the golden-green plant appears on Sasanian silver among the emblems of the consecrated banquet. Haoma collapses ‌the distance between altar and deity: the offering does not merely represent the god — it is the god, crushed to give life.

Zarathustra's Reproach and the Roll of the Righteous (Yasna ​9)

The most human scene in the Avesta opens the ninth chapter of the Yasna. Zarathuštra reproaches the plant: the karpans and the kavis — the sacrificial guilds he condemns — pressed ‌you, Haoma, for the stupefying draught, and the plant stands accused by its own prophet (Yasna 9.1–3). Haoma answers with a catalogue of the righteous who pressed him rightly: Yima, who won ​the sun; Thraētaona, who struck down the three-mouthed Aži Dahāka; Kərəsāspa, who slew the horned dragon Gandarəβa; and Vīštāspa, who first confessed the good religion (Yasna 9.3–8). The exchange is unique — ‌a prophet interrogating his own sacrament — and its verdict is exact: The scene gives the tradition its own charter myth of ritual reform, rare in the ancient world. the plant is ​holy; the abuse is in the pressing. Ritual ethics, not chemistry, decides what a sacrament does to the soul.

The Draught of Vīštāspa (Yašt 9)

For King Vīštāspa, Haoma was oracles ‌and victory. In Yašt 9 the king offers the plant the sacrifice of a hundred stallions, a hundred oxen, a hundred rams, and a hundred loads of grain — and the hymn ​says Haoma grants him what armies could not buy: the most fortunate of thoughts and the most victorious of weapons, with foreknowledge of the coming war against the Turanians and their daeva-worshipping ​king Arjāsp (Yašt 9.26–30). When Vīštāspa drank the pressed haoma, his spirit was illumined and his enemies' devices laid bare; the same hymn remembers the plant's gifts of swift horses and vanquishing ‌glory. The myth dignifies strategy as piety: the visionary draught makes foresight itself a weapon, and the patron king who drinks it fights with wisdom before he fights with armies.
