Born Between the Embrace (Genealogy)

Tangaroa was born into the darkness that lay between Ranginui, the Sky, and ‌Papatūānuku, the Earth, whose embrace left no room and no light for their children. With his brothers Tāne, Tāwhirimātea, Tūmatauenga, Rongo, and Haumia-tiketike he grew to manhood in that cramped twilight, and ​with them he debated how to escape: Tūmatauenga counseled killing the parents outright, Tāne proposed forcing them apart. When Tāne at last pushed Ranginui into the sky and light flooded the world, ‌Tangaroa looked at the dry land his brother had won and chose the other half of creation: he went down into the ocean, and took it for his own. (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, ​ch. 1; Smith, The Lore of the Whare-wānanga.) The myth reads the coastline as a family wound: the sea is not the land's opposite but its brother, still angry.

The Children Who ‌Split in Two (The Storm War)

Not all the brothers accepted the separation. Tāwhirimātea, the wind, remained with his father in the sky and made war on the others, and Tangaroa's domain ​was his first battlefield: the sea boiled under the storm. In the rout, Tangaroa's grandchildren — the children of his son Punga — split forever. Ikatere, father of the fish, fled into ‌the deep and cried 'swim, my children'; Tū-te-wehiwehi, father of the reptiles, scrambled ashore into Tāne's forests and cried 'hide, my children.' Hence the hostility the myth sees everywhere along the tideline: ​fish devour the lizards cast into the sea, and the sea never forgives the land. (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, ch. 1.) What looks like natural enmity is re-read as a family separated under ​fire — every fish a refugee, every lizard a deserter's child.

Hine-moana, Mother of All That Swim (The Ocean Wife)

In the traditions recorded by Elsdon Best, Tangaroa's wife is Hine-moana, 'ocean ‌woman' — not a separate power but the sea itself, personified as the mother of the fish tribes. Every species is their child, which is why the fisherman addresses the sea ‌as kin, and why the first fish of a catch was laid back into the water: a family does not eat its greeting. The canoes that crossed Hine-moana to reach Aotearoa were ‌themselves spoken of as her children, and the rituals of launching, fishing, and beaching observed her tapu; even the nets and lines were named and placated. (Best, Māori Religion and Mythology; Best, ​The Māori as He Was.) The theology is ecological before the word existed: the ocean is not a resource but a marriage, and the catch is a relative arriving for dinner.
