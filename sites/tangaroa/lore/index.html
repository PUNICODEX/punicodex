01

Mythology

Stories of Tangaroa

Tangaroa's stories are the Māori ocean chapter of the great separation cycle — and then the cycle widens: the same name, in other dialects, becomes the supreme creator of the whole Polynesian triangle. The Māori knew what the comparison would later prove.

Genealogy

Born Between the Embrace

Tangaroa was born into the darkness that lay between Ranginui, the Sky, and Papatūānuku, the Earth, whose embrace left no room and no light for their children. With his brothers Tāne, Tāwhirimātea, Tūmatauenga, Rongo, and Haumia-tiketike he grew to manhood in that cramped twilight, and with them he debated how to escape: Tūmatauenga counseled killing the parents outright, Tāne proposed forcing them apart. When Tāne at last pushed Ranginui into the sky and light flooded the world, Tangaroa looked at the dry land his brother had won and chose the other half of creation: he went down into the ocean, and took it for his own. (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, ch. 1; Smith, The Lore of the Whare-wānanga.) The myth reads the coastline as a family wound: the sea is not the land's opposite but its brother, still angry.

The Storm War

The Children Who Split in Two

Not all the brothers accepted the separation. Tāwhirimātea, the wind, remained with his father in the sky and made war on the others, and Tangaroa's domain was his first battlefield: the sea boiled under the storm. In the rout, Tangaroa's grandchildren — the children of his son Punga — split forever. Ikatere, father of the fish, fled into the deep and cried 'swim, my children'; Tū-te-wehiwehi, father of the reptiles, scrambled ashore into Tāne's forests and cried 'hide, my children.' Hence the hostility the myth sees everywhere along the tideline: fish devour the lizards cast into the sea, and the sea never forgives the land. (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, ch. 1.) What looks like natural enmity is re-read as a family separated under fire — every fish a refugee, every lizard a deserter's child.

The Ocean Wife

Hine-moana, Mother of All That Swim

In the traditions recorded by Elsdon Best, Tangaroa's wife is Hine-moana, 'ocean woman' — not a separate power but the sea itself, personified as the mother of the fish tribes. Every species is their child, which is why the fisherman addresses the sea as kin, and why the first fish of a catch was laid back into the water: a family does not eat its greeting. The canoes that crossed Hine-moana to reach Aotearoa were themselves spoken of as her children, and the rituals of launching, fishing, and beaching observed her tapu; even the nets and lines were named and placated. (Best, Māori Religion and Mythology; Best, The Māori as He Was.) The theology is ecological before the word existed: the ocean is not a resource but a marriage, and the catch is a relative arriving for dinner.

Pan-Polynesian

The Same God in Other Islands

Travel west and Tangaroa grows greater. In Tahiti the name becomes Ta'aroa, the self-existent creator from whose body the world was hatched (Henry, Ancient Tahiti). In Tonga and Samoa it is Tangaloa, the supreme god whose messenger, the plover Tuli, dove to the sea-bottom and brought up the sand of the first islands (Craig, Dictionary of Polynesian Mythology). In Hawai'i it is Kanaloa, companion of Kāne in the company of gods who made the first man, and lord of the ocean's dark deep (Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology). The Māori sea-god is thus the eastern branch of the oldest divine name in Polynesia — which is why the nineteenth-century comparatists made him their first case study. One proto-god, four biographies: the triangle itself emerges as a single story told in dialects.

02

Tangaroa

Ocean

Tangaroa is the sea given will: lord of ocean, fish, and all that swims, son of the Sky Father and Earth Mother, and the brother who fled to the deep when his parents were torn apart. Every fish is his child, every canoe his guest, and every storm over the water is family business.

Lord of Ocean

He took the sea for his domain when his parents were separated, vowing in the recitations never to return to the land.

Father of Fishes

With Hine-moana, the ocean woman, he is ancestor of every finned and swimming creature the nets take.

The Exiled Brother

Tāwhirimātea's war drove him from the coast; his grandchildren scattered to sea and shore, splitting into fish and lizard.

One Name, Many Islands

Ta'aroa in Tahiti, Tangaloa in Tonga and Samoa, Kanaloa in Hawai'i — one ocean god under four island names.

Sacred Symbols

The fish-hook (matau) The carved hook with which his descendants take his children from the sea — every catch a petition, every offering a repayment.
The whale and the shark His largest and most feared children; the whale (tohorā) and the shark (mangō) carry his mana in bulk.
The canoe (waka) The vessel that crosses his domain; canoes were tapu and addressed as kin, because the sea is a relative who must be asked.
The tide Tangaroa-whakamau-tai, 'Tangaroa who fixes the tides' — one of his names, and the rhythm by which fishermen read him.
The lizard (ngarara) His grandchildren who hid ashore: the lizards, fish that chose the land — kin, yet forever on the wrong side of the beach.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Tangaroa

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Tangaroa is the standard Polynesian romanisation, documented in academic sources — “God of the sea (from Proto-Polynesian *Taŋaroa)”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual polynesian names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

tangaroa

The plain tangaroa form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Tangaroa

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Tangaroa. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Tangaroa.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Tangaroa and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Tangaroa was spoken

/ta.ŋa.ˈɾo.a/ Māori Reconstruction
ta- Voiceless alveolar [t] plus pure open [a]; Māori t is unaspirated, with none of the puff English puts on 'top.'
-nga- The velar nasal [ŋ] — ng as in 'singer,' never as in 'finger' — one sound, though two letters.
-roa Tapped alveolar [ɾ] plus the diphthong [oa] ('long, extensive'); the diphthong draws the stress to this syllable.

Modern Approximation

'tah-ngah-ROH-ah' — say ng as in 'singer,' tap the r, and let the last two vowels glide together.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Tangaroa is the Māori continuation of Proto-Polynesian *Taŋaroa — the same name the Tahitians lengthen into Ta'aroa the creator and the Tongans keep as Tangaloa. No vowel length distinguishes the Māori form, so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII spelling loses: Tier 2, a display fact only. The ng is not two letters but one sound, the velar nasal English lacks.

Extended Lore

Tangaroa is the sea as a relative — the most radical idea in the Māori cosmos. A god who is a brother, a domain that is a marriage, a catch that is a visit: the whole grammar of the ocean is familial, and the modern world's grammar of the ocean is exactly the opposite. When Māori negotiators in the 1980s and 90s said the sea is an ancestor, they were not inventing an environmentalism of convenience; they were quoting him. The name Tangaroa carries a complete theory of the deep: that the largest thing on the planet is a person with children, a temper, and a claim on our manners.
