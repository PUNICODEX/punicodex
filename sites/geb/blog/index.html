Mythology

Geb’s mythology is the deep structure under the Osiris cycle: the separation of earth and sky that creates the world’s ​space, and the divine tribunal over which he presides when Horus and Seth dispute the succession. His jealousy of Osiris — in one strand he lies with Nut and fathers the ​five gods on the forbidden days — is the genealogical knot of the whole pantheon.

Parted from Nut (Heliopolitan Cosmogony)

Born of Shu and Tefnut, Geb and Nut loved without space between ​them until Shu, on Ra’s command, wrenched them apart — and the world’s breathable distance dates from that separation. Geb’s green fertility and Nut’s starry vault remain arched apart for eternity, ‌the air god’s arms the only thing between them.

The Five Children on the Epagomenal Days (Pyramid Texts)

Though the birth of the five gods belongs to Nut’s myth, the Pyramid Texts ​address Geb as their father and as the mountain of the west on which the dead king ascends. ‘You are Geb, the heir of the gods,’ the utterances tell the king — ‌the earth is the ultimate estate of succession.

The Tribunal of Geb (Contendings of Horus and Seth)

In the New Kingdom narrative of the divine lawsuit, the gods weary of the ​eighty-year dispute between Horus and Seth and appeal to Geb, who as earth and eldest remaining power hears the case and awards the Two Lands to Horus, assigning Seth to the desert ‌and the thunder — the judicial myth behind the division of the living king from the dangerous margins.

The Earth Receives the Dead (Book of the Dead)

Spell 125’s negative confession ​addresses the earth directly — ‘I have not polluted the soil’ — because the soil is Geb’s body and the dead enter it as guests. The grave itself, the shaft and chamber, ‌is a doorway into the god; to be buried was literally to be taken into Geb.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Gb concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, ​each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The Earth God — The soil, the mines, the buried treasure: everything underfoot is his body and his property.
  • The Cackler — His sacred ‌goose gives him the epithet “the Cackler”; his cry was the world’s first sound in some cosmogonies.
  • Father of the Ennead — By Nut he fathered Osiris, Isis, Seth, Nephthys, Horus the ​Elder — the gods whose quarrels drive Egyptian myth.
  • Judge of the Divine Tribunal — He presides over the council of gods in the succession myth of Horus and Seth.

Across Cultures

The Greeks identified Geb with their own earth — Cronos in the common equation, which made mythographic ​sense of his fatherhood of the divine generation. Through Plutarch this mapping circulated widely, though Egyptian theology itself kept him younger and more fertile than the aged Titan. Roman-period underworld iconography occasionally ​fuses his image with that of the infernal judge.

Kindred figures in the PuniCodex cross-tradition index include Anāhitā, Auðhumla, Ọbalúayé, Bꜣstt, Cernunnos, and Cōātlīcue, each linked through earth / mother / ‌fertility.

Cultural Legacy

Geb is less famous than his children but structurally indispensable: every genealogy of the Egyptian gods runs through him. His laughter-as-earthquake remains one of the most charming etiological fragments ​of Egyptian religion, quoted in every handbook; and his goose still cackles on temple walls at Kom Ombo and Esna.
