​Overview

Mythology

The mythology of Dangun is preserved in Korean oral tradition, shamanic ritual texts, and classical sources such as the Samguk Yusa. The narratives are not merely stories; they encode social ‌values, ritual obligations, and relationships between humans, nature, and the sacred. Some details vary by region and by shamanic lineage.

The Bear and the Tiger (Samguk Yusa)

Hwanung tested a bear and a ​tiger by locking them in a cave with only garlic and mugwort. The tiger gave up, but the bear endured one hundred days and became a woman named Ungnyeo. Hwanung then took ‌her as his wife, and she bore Dangun.

The Founding of Gojoseon (Foundation myth)

Dangun established the capital at Asadal and ruled over Gojoseon for one thousand five hundred years. The date ​traditionally given for the founding, 3 October 2333 BCE, is celebrated as National Foundation Day.

Dangun Becomes Mountain Spirit (Apotheosis)

At the end of his reign Dangun left the affairs of state ‌and withdrew into the mountains, where he became a sansin, a mountain deity who continues to protect the land and its people.

Hwanung Comes Down to Sin-si (The Descent)

The Samguk Yusa opens ​the tale in heaven: the supreme lord Hwanin ruled the sky, and his son Hwanung, gazing down upon the world, begged leave to govern it and bring order to humankind. Hwanin looked ‌and saw that the land was worth the blessing; granting his son the three heavenly seals and three thousand followers, he sent him down to the foot of the sandalwood tree, where ​Hwanung established the holy city of Sin-si, the ‘city of god’. There the heavenly prince taught the hundred arts: he bound the people by law, showed them how to sow the five grains, ‌how to cure sickness with herbs, how to take husbands and wives by rite and to send the dead away by rule. The sandalwood — 檀 (dan), the tree that gives the ​dynasty its first syllable — stands at the center of the settlement as an axis where heaven’s bureaucracy touches the soil of earth. The myth reads like a charter: Korean civilization begins ​not with conquest or migration but with a delegation of heavenly order, administered patiently from a single sacred tree.

The Bear Who Endured (The Trial)

When a bear and a tiger living ‌together prayed to Hwanung that they might become human, the heavenly prince set them an ordeal: eat only garlic and mugwort, and shut yourselves away from sunlight for one hundred days. The tiger ‌grew restless and broke the fast before the days were out, but the bear endured to the end and was transformed into a woman — Ungnyeo, the ‘bear-woman’. She prayed beneath the ‌sandalwood for a child, and Hwanung, moved by her devotion, took her as his wife; from their union was born Dangun, the sandalwood prince. The Samguk Yusa tells the episode with the ​economy of a parable: the stronger animal fails, the meeker succeeds, and a nation descends from patience rather than power. Later Korean tradition never tired of the moral — that the people’s ​character was shaped in a dark cave, by an animal’s willingness to endure what flesh finds unbearable, and to come out of it changed.

Asadal and the Fifteen Hundred Years (The Founding)

Dangun ‌grew and in his day founded the state of Gojoseon, ‘Joseon of old’, establishing its capital at Asadal — a name later tradition ties to the sandalwood tree of his father’s descent ​and to a peak in the Taebaek range. The Samguk Yusa credits him with a reign of one thousand five hundred years, a fabulous span that marks his kingship as belonging to ​mythic time rather than history; the Samguk Sagi, more sober, enters the founding under 2333 BCE in the Dangi calendar that Korean tradition still counts from. The two accounts agree on the essence: ‌Dangun gave his people law, agriculture, and ritual order, the three instruments by which a settlement becomes a state. When the sage Jizi (Gija) led five thousand followers east from the Zhou ​collapse, tradition says Dangun did not resist but yielded, withdrew to Jangdanggyeong, and let the new line rule — the mythic model of a succession accomplished by withdrawal rather than war, legitimacy ​transferred by grace.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Dangun concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The sandalwood tree — Dang 檀 ‌means sandalwood; the tree marks the sacred descent place of Hwanung.
  • Bear and tiger — The two animals who underwent Hwanung's trial; the bear's perseverance became the origin of the Korean people.
  • Garlic ​and mugwort — The austere foods of transformation, marking the passage from animal to human and from wilderness to culture.
  • Mountain peak — The place where Dangun withdrew and continues to dwell ‌as a protective mountain spirit.

Across Cultures

The Dangun myth has been interpreted through many lenses: as ​a nativist charter myth, as a Confucian parable of virtuous rule, as a Buddhist tale of miraculous birth, and, in the modern era, as a national foundation narrative. North and South Korea both ​claim Dangun as a common ancestor, though their political interpretations differ. The myth also resonates with broader northeast Asian stories of bear ancestors and celestial founders.

Within the Korean tradition, closely related ‌names in the corpus include Baekdusan, Chilsong, Halmoni, Hwanin, Hwanung, and Jowangshin.

Cultural Legacy

Dangun is perhaps the most potent symbol of Korean national identity. His tomb near Pyongyang, the annual Gaecheonjeol ‌ceremony, and countless school textbooks, monuments, and cultural productions keep the figure present in public life. The story is also invoked by overseas Korean communities and by inter-Korean dialogue that seeks a ‌shared mythic past. In popular culture Dangun appears in films, video games, and webtoons, often reimagined as a heroic founder.

A Meditation

Dangun is named for a tree, and the naming is the meaning. The sandalwood marks ​the place where heaven’s order was pegged into Korean soil; the ‘lord’ (君, gun) marks the moment an ancestor became an office. Between the two syllables sits the whole Korean claim: that ​the people descend from a disciplined animal and a patient god, and that their first king ruled so long his reign stops being a span and becomes a climate.

The restoration of ​the capitalized name is thus more than typographic courtesy. An ancestor who lives in altars, holidays, and divided nations cannot be flattened to lowercase without losing something real. Dangun is the kind ‌of name that is a charter — and charters, unlike labels, deserve their capitals.
