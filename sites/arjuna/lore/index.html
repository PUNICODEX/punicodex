01

Mythology

Stories of Arjuna

Arjuna’s mythology is the epic’s spine: the feats of the forest-years, the disguises of the incognito year, the divine weapons won from Śiva and Indra, and the chariot-dialogue that turned a battle’s morning into the world’s most read scripture. He is the hero who wins everything and is saved, in the end, by having once doubted.

Ādi Parvan

The Pāṇḍavas’ Birth

When Pāṇḍu was cursed to die at love’s consummation, Kuntī used the boon of the mantra: Dharma fathered Yudhiṣṭhira, Vāyu Bhīma, and Indra himself Arjuna — born, the epic says, with Indra’s marks. The third son was trained by Droṇa, favoured by the master above all pupils, and marked from birth as the one who would carry the war.

Vana Parvan

The Kirātārjunīya

In the forest, Arjuna did austerities for the divine weapons; Śiva came as a kirāta, a hill-tribesman, and they fought over a boar’s carcass until the god revealed himself and gave the Pāśupata — the shaft that ends worlds. Kālidāsa made the combat his greatest mahākāvya, the Kirātārjunīya.

Bhagavadgītā

The Chariot and the Crisis

Seeing his teachers and kinsmen ranked against him, Arjuna lowered his bow: ‘I will not fight.’ Krishna’s answer — eighteen chapters on duty, devotion, the self, and action without attachment — is the Bhagavadgītā, the most read and most translated of Sanskrit texts. The war resumed; but the dialogue outgrew it entirely.

Aśvamedhika

The Horse and the End

After the war, Yudhiṣṭhira’s horse-sacrifice wandered the subcontinent under Arjuna’s guard — his last great circuit. In the Svargārohaṇika, the Pāṇḍavas walk to heaven’s gate; Arjuna falls first among them, his sin named precisely: the vanity of having believed himself the greatest of archers.

02

The Third Pāṇḍava, the Great Archer

The Warrior Who Doubted, and the Gītā’s Listener

Arjuna is the Mahābhārata’s central hero: the archer without equal, winner of Draupadī’s svayaṃvara, friend and charioteer-client of Krishna, and the man whose crisis on the battlefield became the occasion of the Bhagavadgītā — the moment world philosophy was spoken from a chariot between two armies.

The Gandiva

The bow of Brahmā, won from Agni at Khaṇḍava; its twang alone routs armies.

The Svayaṃvara

He alone shot the eye of the rotating fish at Draupadī’s bride-choice, looking only at its reflection.

The Gītā’s Chariot

Between the armies he faltered — and Krishna answered with eighteen chapters that became a scripture.

The Tīrtha Pilgrimage

His year of exile became the Tīrthayātrā — the epic’s encyclopedia of India’s sacred geography.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

अर्जुन

The name in its original Sanskrit form. Arjuna (अर्जुन) is attested in the source tradition — “The third of the Pāṇḍava princes, a son of Indra and Kuntī; the great archer of the Mahābhārata, to whom Kṛṣṇa speaks the Bhagavad-gītā.”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

arjuna

The plain arjuna form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Arjuna

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Arjuna. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Arjuna.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Arjuna and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Arjuna was spoken

/ˈɑːrdʒʊnə/ Sanskrit (IAST: Arjuna)
Ar- The j is the palatal “j” of “jump”; the name is related to arjuna, “white, silver”.
-ju- Short u as in “put”; the compound flows without stress in the classical cadence.
-na The final a is a light schwa; the name means “the white one”, “the silvery”.

Modern Approximation

“AHR-joo-nuh”
