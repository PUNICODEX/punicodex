Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈɑːrdʒʊnə/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Arjuna).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ar- — The j is the ​palatal “j” of “jump”; the name is related to arjuna, “white, silver”.
  • -ju- — Short u as in “put”; the compound flows without stress in the classical cadence.
  • ​-na — The final a is a light schwa; the name means “the white one”, “the silvery”.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: “AHR-joo-nuh”

Mythology

Arjuna’s mythology is the epic’s spine: the feats of the forest-years, the disguises of ‌the incognito year, the divine weapons won from Śiva and Indra, and the chariot-dialogue that turned a battle’s morning into the world’s most read scripture. He is the hero ​who wins everything and is saved, in the end, by having once doubted.

The Pāṇḍavas’ Birth (Ādi Parvan)

When Pāṇḍu was cursed to die at love’s consummation, Kuntī ‌used the boon of the mantra: Dharma fathered Yudhiṣṭhira, Vāyu Bhīma, and Indra himself Arjuna — born, the epic says, with Indra’s marks. The third son was trained by ​Droṇa, favoured by the master above all pupils, and marked from birth as the one who would carry the war.

The Kirātārjunīya (Vana Parvan)

In the forest, Arjuna did ‌austerities for the divine weapons; Śiva came as a kirāta, a hill-tribesman, and they fought over a boar’s carcass until the god revealed himself and gave the Pāśupata ​— the shaft that ends worlds. Kālidāsa made the combat his greatest mahākāvya, the Kirātārjunīya.

The Chariot and the Crisis (Bhagavadgītā)

Seeing his teachers and kinsmen ranked against him, ‌Arjuna lowered his bow: ‘I will not fight.’ Krishna’s answer — eighteen chapters on duty, devotion, the self, and action without attachment — is the Bhagavadgītā, the most ​read and most translated of Sanskrit texts. The war resumed; but the dialogue outgrew it entirely.

The Horse and the End (Aśvamedhika)

After the war, Yudhiṣṭhira’s horse-sacrifice wandered the ‌subcontinent under Arjuna’s guard — his last great circuit. In the Svargārohaṇika, the Pāṇḍavas walk to heaven’s gate; Arjuna falls first among them, his sin named precisely: the vanity ​of having believed himself the greatest of archers.

Across Cultures

Arjuna is the Nara of the Nara-Nārāyaṇa ​pair — the human half of Vishnu’s eternal companion — making his friendship with Krishna a theology in dialogue-form. In Javanese and Balinese tradition he becomes the refined knight ​of the wayang cycle; and the Gītā made him, doubting, the everyman of all later Indian philosophy.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include ‌Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Bhakti, and Bhūdevī.

Cultural Legacy

The Gītā’s chariot is the world’s most reproduced scene of instruction; the phrase ‘Kurukshetra’ entered the political vocabulary of every ​Indian language as the name of any field where duty and doubt collide. His name crowned South Indian kings (Arjunavarman) and the great tank of Rajput legend; and ‌‘Arjuna award’ is India’s highest honour for a sportsperson — the archer’s prize.
