The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᛁᚱ

The name in its original Norse form. Eir (ᛁᚱ) is attested in the source tradition — “Help, mercy”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

eir

The plain eir form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Eir

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Eir. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Eir.com → eir.com

The non-ASCII characters in Eir are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Eir.

Pronunciation

How Eir was spoken

/ˈɛir/ Old Norse Reconstruction
Ei- The Old Norse diphthong ei, [ɛi]: a glide opening at the short front vowel of 'bed' and closing toward 'ee' — one syllable, never two.
-r The trilled nominative ending [r], lightly sounded, as in modern Icelandic Eir.

Modern Approximation

'AYR' — one syllable: glide from the e of 'bed' into 'ee', and close with a light trilled r; modern Icelanders say it nearly the same way.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Eir, third of the ásynjur in Snorri's catalogue; the common noun eir means 'help, mercy, clemency'.
  • Younger Futhark ᛅᛁᚱ (a-i-r) — the ᛁ rune writes both e and i; a normalized rendering, not an attested carving.
  • Modern Icelandic Eir, an unbroken living given name; the verb eira, 'to spare, show mercy', keeps the noun's family alive.

The Accent / Script Rule

Eir is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying it. Three letters and a capital; the goddess's standing in the sources is entirely beside the orthographic point.

05

Healing, Mercy, Medical Skill

The Best Physician of the Ásynjur

Eir is the Norse tradition's one physician: third in Snorri's catalogue of the ásynjur, defined by a single superlative — læknir beztr, 'the best of healers' — and named again among the healing maidens of Lyfjaberg in Fjölsvinnsmál. Her name is the common noun for mercy itself.

Lyfjaberg

The 'mountain of healing' where Menglöð sits with her maidens — lyf is the Old Norse word for medicinal herbs and remedies.

The Nine Maidens

Hlíf, Hlífþursa, Þjóðvarta, Björt, Blíð, Blíðr, Fríð, Eir, and Aurboða — the company Fjölsviðr names at Menglöð's knees.

The Holy Place

Fjölsvinnsmál's promise: the maidens protect those who make offering at the sacred stead, and no sickness shall drag them down.

The Superlative

Læknir beztr — 'the best physician': the only office Snorri gives her, and the only medical title in the ásynjur catalogue.

Sacred Symbols

Lyfjaberg The mountain of remedies — healing made landscape, the high place where help is found
The nine maidens Protection, brightness, joy, mercy: the named company whose gifts are the inventory of recovery
The altar at the holy place The covenant of Fjölsvinnsmál — sacrifice answered with deliverance from sickness
Menglöð's knee The healing maidens sit at the necklace-glad one's knees — mercy attendant upon splendour
The name itself Eir, 'help, mercy': the remedy and the plea for it in a single word
06

Mythology

Stories of Eir

Eir has the thinnest dossier of any goddess in the Edda — one superlative in Snorri's catalogue and one stanza-list in the Poetic Edda — and the office both witnesses agree on: she heals. Nothing else about her was ever written down.

Gylfaginning 35

The Best Physician

Snorri's catalogue of the ásynjur runs like a court roll: Frigg is highest; second is Sága at Søkkvabekkr; and then, without genealogy, marriage, or adventure, the third entry: 'Eir is the third: she is the best physician' — Eir er þriðja: hon er læknir beztr. That is the whole notice. No hall is named, no parentage given, no deed narrated. In a catalogue that finds something to say about every goddess — Fulla's headband, Gefjun's plough, Syn's door-keeping — Eir alone is defined by a profession and a superlative, and the profession is medicine.

Fjölsvinnsmál 38-40

The Maidens of Lyfjaberg

The Poetic Edda keeps her second witness in the strangest of its love-quests. Svipdagr, come to win Menglöð, must answer the gate-ward Fjölsviðr riddle for riddle; among his questions is this: what are the maids called who sit at Menglöð's knees in harmony together? Fjölsviðr names nine: Hlíf the protectress, Hlífþursa, Þjóðvarta, Björt the bright, Blíð and Blíðr the blithe, Fríð the fair, Eir, and Aurboða. Then comes the promise that makes the list matter: these maidens will give help if men make offering at the holy place, and no sickness so cruel will seize the sons of men that they cannot free them from it. Menglöð's seat is Lyfjaberg, the mountain of healing — and Eir, 'mercy', stands named in its company.

Skáldskaparmál þulur

Valkyrie and Kenning

The name has a quieter afterlife in the poets' apparatus. The þulur — the metrical name-lists preserved with the Prose Edda — count an Eir among the valkyries, and the skalds used eir as a base-word in kennings for women, as the handbooks register: 'Eir of the ale' or 'Eir of rings' is simply a woman, any goddess's name being lawful tender in the kenning system. Whether the valkyrie Eir is the physician of Gylfaginning under another aspect or a second bearer of the name, the sources do not say; Simek registers the double attestation and leaves the door open. What the kennings prove is that the name lived in the working memory of the poets.

Scholarly discussion

The Menglöð Question

Who is the lady of Lyfjaberg whom Eir attends? Menglöð — 'the one gladdened by necklaces' — bears a name that points straight at Freyja, owner of the Brísingamen, and much scholarship has taken Menglöð as a byname or hypostasis of the Vanadís rather than a separate figure; the two poems of Svipdagsmál as a whole have often been read as a fragmentary Freyja-myth. The identification is attractive and unprovable, and the cautious handbooks present it as exactly that. If it holds, then the North's one physician sits in Freyja's own retinue — mercy among the attendants of love; if it does not, Lyfjaberg keeps its own mistress, and Eir keeps hers.

Extended Lore

Eir is the goddess made of a single word, and the word is mercy. The Edda gives her no story because her name is already the story: eir is what the wounded ask for and what the healer gives — help, clemency, the staying of a hand. Snorri places her third among the goddesses, ahead of figures with far longer dossiers, as if the catalogue itself understood that a pantheon without a physician would be incomplete. Fjölsvinnsmál completes the portrait by giving her a company and a mountain: mercy does not work alone but sits named among protection, brightness, gladness, and peace — the full inventory of what recovery feels like from the inside. To say her name in Old Norse was already to ask for the one thing medicine and prayer both promise.
