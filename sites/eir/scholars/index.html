01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Eir (eir) ​— Healing, ‌Mercy, Medical ‌Skill · ​the Best ​Physician — is ​the Norse ​pantheon's one ​explicit deity ‌of medicine: ​third in Snorri ​Sturluson's catalogue ‌of the ​ásynjur, defined ‌by a ​single superlative — ‌læknir beztr, ​'the best ​physician' — ​and named ​once more in ​the Poetic ‌Edda among ​the nine ‌healing maidens who ​sit at ‌Menglöð's knees ​on Lyfjaberg, ‌the mountain ​of remedies.[1][2]

Her ‌name is ​the Old ​Norse common ‌noun eir ‌'help, mercy, clemency' ‌— the ​remedy and ​the plea ‌for it ​in a single ​word. No ‌genealogy, marriage, ​hall, or ​adventure is ‌recorded for her; ​the two-sentence ‌notice in ​Gylfaginning 35 ​and the ​name-list of Fjölsvinnsmál ​38–40 are ‌her entire ‌dossier, and ​the office ‌both witnesses agree ​on is ​healing.[1][2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the ‌name as ‌Eir — identical ‌to the ​ASCII input ‌apart from ​the capital, ​because normalized Old ​Norse writes ‌no diacritic ​in this ​name. The mechanical ​rule therefore ‌places it ​in Tier ​2: nothing ​distinctive is lost ‌in transit, ​a statement ‌about orthography ​and never ‌about the goddess. ​The temple ‌is domain-less: ‌the plain-ASCII ​eir.com is ​registered to a ​third party ​outside the ‌collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35 (the catalogue of the ásynjur), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. eir.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested in ‌normalized Old ​Norse as ​Eir (the ​final -r ​is the ​nominative ending). ‌It is ​the common ​noun eir ‌(feminine) 'help, ​mercy, clemency, ‌peace' — ​the word ‌a sufferer ​asks for ​and a ​healer grants.[1] ​The dictionaries ​distinguish it ‌from the ​homonym eir ‌(neuter) 'copper, ​bronze'; the ‌goddess's name ​belongs to ‌the mercy-word, ​and its ‌deeper etymology ​is unsettled ​— the ‌standard lexica ‌gloss it ‌and leave ​the origin ​open.[1][2]

Because ‌the normalized ​form carries ​no diacritic ‌and no ​distinctive letter, ​the ASCII ‌form eir ​loses nothing: ‌this is ​a technological ​non-event rather ​than a ​compromise, and ‌the name ‌is classified ​Tier 2 ‌by the ​mechanical rule. ​The restoration ‌Eir is ‌a matter ‌of canonical ‌reference — ​the capital ‌and the ​scholarly context ​— not ​of rescued ‌orthography.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • ​e → ‌E — ​Same, capitalized

​- i ​→ i ‌— Same
​- r ‌→ r ​— Same

‌The project ​holds no ‌domain for ‌this name: ​the plain-ASCII ​eir.com is ​registered outside ​the collection ‌and is ‌watched for ​release.

Sources

  1. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. eir (f. 'help, mercy'; distinguished from eir n. 'copper').
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. eir, eira.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed pronunciation ‌of the ‌name is ​/ˈɛir/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- Ei- ‌— The ​Old ​Norse diphthong ‌ei, [ɛi]: ​a glide ‌opening at ​the short ‌front vowel ​of English ​'bed' and ​closing toward ​'ee'; ​one syllable, ‌never two
​- -r ‌— The ​trilled nominative ‌ending [r], ​lightly sounded, ‌as in ​modern ‌Icelandic

For ​the modern ​speaker, the ‌closest approximation ‌is: 'AYR' ‌— one ​syllable, gliding ​from 'eh' ‌into 'ee', ​closed ​with a ‌light trill.

​Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • ‌Old Norse ​— Eir, ​third of ​the ​ásynjur; the ‌common noun ‌eir 'help, ​mercy'
  • ‌Younger Futhark ​— ᛅᛁᚱ ​(a-i-r), a ‌normalized runic ‌rendering, not ‌an ‌attested carving

​- Modern ‌Icelandic — ​Eir, an ​unbroken living ​given name; ‌the verb ​eira 'to ​spare, ​show mercy' ‌keeps the ​word-family in ​daily use[2]

​Eir is ‌Tier 2 ​because the ‌restoration preserves ​nothing the ‌ASCII ​form loses: ‌three plain ‌letters and ​a capital. ​The god-rank ​of the ​name's bearer ‌is beside ‌the orthographic ​point.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. eir, eira.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ​manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Eir — ​Old Norse ‌(North Germanic), ​attested in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the Prose ‌Edda (c. ​1300) and ‌its sister ​codices, and ​in the ​seventeenth-century paper ​manuscripts that ​alone preserve ‌Fjölsvinnsmál.[1][2] ​Before the ‌Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ‌names were ​carved in ‌the Younger ​Futhark; no ‌runic attestation ​of this ​theonym survives, ‌so the ‌manuscript form ‌is the ​primary witness.

​The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ​step by ​step:

  • ‌The name ​is the ​feminine common ‌noun ​eir 'help, ‌mercy, clemency', ​with the ​nominative singular ​ending -r.

​- Old ‌Norse orthography ‌writes no ​diacritic in ‌this name: ​the vowel ​is the ‌diphthong ei, ‌plain in ‌the manuscripts.
‌- In ​the Younger ‌Futhark the ​name would ​be carved ​ᛅᛁᚱ (a-i-r), ‌the ​ᛁ rune ​writing both ​e and ‌i — ​a normalized ​reconstruction, not ​an attested ‌inscription.

  • ​The Unicode ‌restoration Eir ​is therefore ‌identical to ​the ASCII ‌input apart ‌from the ​capital: nothing ​was lost ​in transit, ​which is ‌the definition ‌of Tier ​2.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. The Poetic Edda, trans. Carolyne Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014), introduction to Grógaldr and Fjölsvinnsmál.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Eir ​is the ‌only figure ‌in the ​Norse ​corpus whose ​stated office ​is medicine: ​Snorri defines ‌her ​by her ​craft and ‌a superlative, ​and ‌the Poetic ​Edda places ‌her in ​a company ​whose ​business is ​deliverance from ​sickness.[1][2]

‌Healing

​Gylfaginning 35 ‌gives her ​whole identity ‌in ​one clause: ‌hon er ​læknir beztr, ‌'she is ​the ​best physician' ‌— the ‌only medical ‌title ​in the ​catalogue of ‌the ásynjur.

​### Mercy

​Her ‌name is ​the common ​noun eir ‌'help, ​mercy, clemency': ‌the remedy ​and the ​plea ​for it ​in a ‌single word.

‌### Medical ​Skill

‌Fjölsvinnsmál names ​her among ​the nine ‌maidens ‌of Lyfjaberg ‌— the ‌mountain whose ​name is ‌built ​on lyf, ​'medicinal herb, ​remedy' — ‌who ​free from ​sickness those ​who honour ‌the ​holy place.[2]

​### The ​Ásynjur Household

‌She stands ​third ‌in Snorri's ​court-roll of ‌the goddesses, ​after ‌Frigg and ‌Sága and ​ahead of ​Fulla — ​a ​rank that ‌far outruns ‌her recorded ​deeds.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40, trans. Larrington (2014); Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Lyfjaberg.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Eir's ​iconography is ‌the ‌iconography ​of her ​two ​attestations — ​the ​corpus gives ‌her ​no ​regalia, but ‌her ​surroundings fixed ‌a ​small set ‌of ​associations:[1][2]

​- Lyfjaberg ​— ​The 'mountain ​of ‌healing' ​where Menglöð ‌sits ​with her ‌maidens: ​lyf is ‌the ​Old ‌Norse word ​for ​medicinal herbs ‌and ‌remedies

  • ‌The ​nine ​maidens — ‌Hlíf ​('protection'), Hlífþursa, ​Þjóðvarta, ‌Björt ​('bright'), Blíð ​and ‌Blíðr ('blithe'), ​Fríð ‌('fair, peace'), ​Eir, ​and ​Aurboða: the ​named ‌company whose ‌gifts ​read ‌like the ​inventory ​of recovery

‌- ‌The holy ‌place ‌— ​Fjölsvinnsmál's covenant: ‌the ​maidens give ​help ​to those ‌who ​make ​offering at ​the ‌sacred stead, ​and ​no ​sickness shall ‌drag ​such worshippers ‌down
​- The ‌superlative ​— ‌Læknir beztr, ‌'the ​best physician': ​the ​only epithet ​the ‌tradition ‌ever gave ​her

Sources

  1. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40, trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Eir's mythology ​is two notices and ‌a name-list — and ‌a coherent portrait ​anyway, because every witness ​agrees on the office.

​### The Catalogue ​Notice (Gylfaginning 35)

Snorri's ​roll of the ásynjur ‌opens with Frigg, ​continues with Sága at ​Søkkvabekkr, and then gives ‌the third entry in ​full: 'Eir is ‌the third: she is ​the best physician.' No ‌parentage, no hall, ​no deed follows. In ​a catalogue that finds ​something to say ​about every goddess — ​Fulla's headband, Gefjun's plough, ‌Syn's door-keeping — ​Eir alone is defined ‌by a profession, and ​the profession is ‌medicine.[1]

The Maidens ​of Lyfjaberg (Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40)

‌Her second witness ​is embedded in the ‌Edda's strangest love-quest. Svipdagr, ​come to win ​Menglöð, must trade riddles ‌with the gate-ward Fjölsviðr, ‌and among his questions ‌is this: what ​are the maids called ​who sit at Menglöð's ‌knees in harmony ​together? Fjölsviðr names nine ​— Hlíf, Hlífþursa, Þjóðvarta, ‌Björt, Blíð, Blíðr, ​Fríð, Eir, and Aurboða ​— and adds the ‌promise that gives ​the list its weight: ‌these maidens will help ​those who make ​offering at the holy ​place, and no sickness ​so cruel will ‌seize men that they ‌cannot free them from ​it. Menglöð's seat ‌is Lyfjaberg, the mountain ​of remedies.[2]

The ​Menglöð Question

Who is ‌the lady Eir ‌attends? Menglöð — 'the ‌one gladdened by necklaces' ‌— bears a ​name that points at ‌Freyja, owner of the ​Brísingamen, and much ​scholarship has read Menglöð ​as a byname or ‌hypostasis of the ​Vanadís; the cautious handbooks ​present the identification as ​attractive and unprovable.[3] ‌If it holds, the ​North's one physician sits ​in Freyja's own ​retinue — mercy among ‌the attendants of love.

​### Valkyrie and ‌Kenning

The þulur preserved ​with the Prose Edda ‌count an Eir among ​the valkyries, and ‌the skalds used eir ‌as a base-word in ​kennings for women; ​whether the valkyrie is ​the physician under another ​aspect or a ‌second bearer of the ‌name, the sources do ​not say.[4]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40, trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Menglöð; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, s.v. Menglöð, Freyja.
  4. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Eir.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

No interpretatio ​romana ever ‌found Eir ‌a Latin ​name: the ​classical and medieval ​writers who ​mapped the ​northern gods ‌left her unmentioned, ​and no ​equation with ‌a Greco-Roman ​healing deity ‌appears in any ​medieval source. ‌Her real ​company is ​native. The ​Germanic world knew ​healing as ​a craft ‌guarded by ​female figures and ‌spoken charms: ​the Second ‌Merseburg Charm ​— the ‌one surviving Old ​High German ‌healing spell ​with divine ​names — binds ‌Wodan to ‌the mending ‌of a ​wrenched foot ​in a formula ‌with Norse ​echoes, and ​the Edda's ‌own Oddrúnargrátr ​gives a mortal ​woman the ‌role of ​birth-helper.[1] Eir ‌is the divine ​end of ​that continuum: ​the household ​of healing ‌maidens on Lyfjaberg ‌raised to ​the rank ‌of ásynja. ​Modern comparisons ​with Greek healing ‌figures such ‌as Hygieia ‌are analogies ‌of office, not ​borrowings — ‌the name ​eir is ​transparently Norse, ​the common noun ‌for mercy, ​and the ​tradition grew ​its physician out ‌of its ​own vocabulary ​of help.[2]

​Within the ‌corpus, her closest ​kin by ‌office are ​Freyja (whose ‌Menglöð-aspect she ​may attend) and ‌Frigg (at ‌the head ​of the ​catalogue where she ​stands third); ​no other ‌Norse figure ‌is defined ​by medicine.

Sources

  1. The Second Merseburg Charm, in Braune, Wilhelm, and Ernst A. Ebbinghaus, Althochdeutsches Lesebuch, 17th ed. (Tübingen: Niemeyer, 1994); Oddrúnargrátr (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Eir.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Eir's ​afterlife is ‌quiet and ‌remarkably ​alive. The ​name never ​died ​in the ​North: Eir ‌remains ​a living ​Icelandic given ‌name, ​and its ‌word-family survives ​in ‌the everyday ​Icelandic verb ​eira, ​'to spare, ​to show ​mercy'.[1] ‌Modern Heathen ​and Ásatrú ‌practice, ​which keeps ‌few of ​the ‌lesser ásynjur ​in regular ‌use, ​has made ​room for ‌her ‌precisely because ‌her office ​is ​the one ‌every household ​needs ​— she ‌is invoked ​in ​healing rites ‌as the ​physician Snorri ‌named.

​Popular culture ​reached her ​mainly ​through the ‌valkyrie list: ‌an ​Eir numbered ‌among the ​valkyries ​appears in ‌modern games ‌and ‌retellings, usually ‌in the ​healer's ‌role that ​Gylfaginning and ​the ​þulur between ‌them suggest. ​Her ​scholarly afterlife ​is disproportionate ‌to ​her dossier: ​the two-sentence ​goddess ‌of Gylfaginning ​35 is ‌cited ​in every ‌handbook of ​Norse ‌mythology as ‌the standing ​proof ​that the ​northern pantheon ​contained ‌an explicit ‌deity of ​medicine.[2]

Sources

  1. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. eira.
  2. Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Eir; Lindow, Norse Mythology: A Guide (2001), s.v. Eir.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Eir ​is an ‌entirely textual ‌goddess: ​no runestone ​invokes her, ​no ​votive deposit ​or place-name ‌can ​be securely ​claimed for ‌her, ​and no ‌image is ​identified ‌as hers.[1] ​Her witnesses ​are ​manuscripts, and ​they hang ​by ‌threads of ​different thickness. ‌The ​Gylfaginning notice ‌survives with ​the ‌Prose Edda ​— above ‌all ​the Codex ​Regius (GKS ‌2367 ‌4to, c. ‌1300) and ​its ​sister codices.[2]

‌Fjölsvinnsmál hangs ​thinner: ​the poem ‌is not ​in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the Poetic ‌Edda ​at all ​but is ​preserved ​only in ‌seventeenth-century paper ‌manuscripts, ​and it ‌was nineteenth-century ​editors ​who first ‌joined it ‌with ‌Grógaldr as ‌the two ​halves ‌of Svipdagsmál. ​The late ​transmission ​has made ‌some scholars ​cautious ​about the ​poem's age, ‌but ​its language ​and metre ​are ‌accepted as ​medieval, and ‌its ​Lyfjaberg catalogue ‌remains the ​only ‌narrative context ‌in which ​the ​physician of ​the ásynjur ​is ‌ever seen ‌at her ​post.[3]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌knows Eir ‌once, and ​the once ​is enough ​to give ​her a ​landscape. In ‌Fjölsvinnsmál — ​the second ​half of ‌the Svipdagsmál ​pair — ‌the wooer ​Svipdagr ‌trades questions ​with the ​gate-ward Fjölsviðr, ​and one ​exchange is ​hers: what ‌are the ​maids called ‌who sit ​at Menglöð's ‌knees in ​harmony together? ‌Fjölsviðr names ​nine, ‌and Eir ​stands eighth: ​Hlíf, Hlífþursa, ‌Þjóðvarta, Björt, ‌Blíð, Blíðr, ‌Fríð, Eir, ​and Aurboða.[1] ​The next ‌stanza gives ​the company ​its charter: ‌they will ​help those ​who make ‌offering ​at the ‌holy place, ​and no ​sickness so ​cruel will ​seize the ‌sons of ‌men that ​the maidens ‌cannot free ​them from ​it.

The ‌setting does ‌the rest ‌of ‌the work. ​Menglöð's seat ‌is Lyfjaberg ​— the ​mountain built ​on lyf, ‌the Old ​Norse word ​for medicinal ​herbs and ‌remedies — ​and the ​nine names ​read ‌as a ​healer's inventory: ‌protection, brightness, ​gladness, peace, ‌mercy.[2] The ​poem never ‌stages Eir ‌doing anything; ​like Snorri, ​it is ​content to ​place her ‌exactly where ‌a physician ​belongs.

Sources

  1. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. lyf; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, s.v. Lyfjaberg.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is ‌Eir's charter. ​Gylfaginning 35 ​gives ​her entire ​identity ​in one ‌clause of ​the ​ásynjur catalogue ‌— ​'Eir is ‌the third: ​she ‌is the ​best ​physician' — ​and the ​placement ​is itself ‌a ​datum: she ‌outranks ​every goddess ‌after Frigg ​and ‌Sága in ​a ‌roll where ​rank plainly ​matters.[1] ‌Snorri, who ‌finds ‌a hall, ​a husband, ​or ‌a deed ​for ​nearly every ‌other entry, ​offers ​nothing more; ‌the ​superlative stands ‌alone, ​the only ​medical office ​anywhere ​in his ‌mythology.

‌The Prose ​Edda's second ‌contribution ​is the ​apparatus. ‌The þulur ‌transmitted with ‌Skáldskaparmál ‌count an ​Eir ‌among the ​valkyries, and ​the ​skaldic kenning ‌system ​treated her ​name ​as lawful ‌base-word for ​woman-kennings ​— proof, ​as ‌Simek notes, ​that the ‌name ​lived in ‌the ​working memory ‌of the ‌poets ​whether or ​not ​the physician ​and the ‌valkyrie ‌were one ​figure.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Eir.

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no ​attestation of ​the ​goddess ​Eir. No ​runestone ‌invokes ​her, no ​amulet ‌bears her ​name, ‌and ​no inscription ‌links ​a ​dedicant to ​her ​cult ​— a ‌silence ​she shares ‌with ​most ‌of the ​lesser ‌ásynjur ​and one ‌that ​marks ​her as ‌a ‌figure of ‌the ​literary-mythological ​record rather ‌than ​of ​named cult ‌practice.[1]

​What ​the runic ‌tradition ​can offer ‌is ​the ​rendering. In ​the ​Younger ‌Futhark the ‌name ​would ‌have been ​carved ​ᛅᛁᚱ (a-i-r): ‌the ‌ᛁ ‌rune writes ‌both ​e ‌and i, ​and ​ᚱ ​carries the ‌final ​nominative -r. ​No ​such ‌carving is ​known ​— ​the form ‌is ​a ‌normalized reconstruction, ​and ‌the primary ​witness ‌remains ‌the manuscript ​spelling ​of ​the Eddas, ​which ‌is ‌itself plain ​ASCII.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Eir.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Eir has ‌no ​saga narrative: ​no ​þáttr, no ​conversion anecdote, ​no ‌euhemerized king-list ​carries ​her. Her ‌medieval afterlife ​outside ‌the mythographic ​corpus ‌runs through ​the skaldic ​kenning ​system, where ​eir ​served as ‌a base-word ​for ‌women — ​'Eir ‌of the ​ale', 'Eir ‌of ​rings' — ‌any ​goddess's name ​being lawful ‌tender ‌in the ‌poets' ​algebra.[1] The ​usage is ‌mechanical ​rather than ​devotional, ‌but it ​is evidence ​of ‌exactly the ​kind ‌PuniCodex exists ​to record: ​the ​name was ​current, ‌pronounceable, and ‌ready to ​hand.

‌The word's ​independent ​life is ‌worth one ‌further ‌note. Old ‌Norse ​prose used ‌eir as ​an ​ordinary noun ​of ‌mercy and ​the derived ​verb ​eira 'to ‌spare, ​show mercy' ​wherever clemency ​was ‌asked or ​granted; ‌the goddess's ​name and ‌the ​everyday plea ‌are ‌the same ​word, and ​the ​sagas keep ​the ‌word if ‌not the ​goddess.[2]

Sources

  1. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Eir.
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. eir, eira.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Eir ​is the ‌goddess ‌made of ​a ​single word, ​and ​the word ​is ‌mercy. The ​Edda ​gives her ‌no ​story because ‌her ​name is ‌already ​the story: ​eir ​is what ​the ​wounded ask ‌for and ​what ‌the healer ​gives ‌— help, ​clemency, ‌the staying ​of ‌a hand.[1]

​Snorri ​places her ‌third ‌among the ‌goddesses, ​ahead of ​figures ‌with far ​longer ​dossiers, as ‌if ​the catalogue ​itself ‌understood that ​a ‌pantheon without ​a ​physician would ​be ​incomplete. Fjölsvinnsmál ‌completes ‌the portrait ​by ‌giving her ​a ​company and ‌a ‌mountain: mercy ‌does ‌not work ​alone ‌but sits ​named ​among protection, ​brightness, ‌gladness, and ​peace — ​the ​full inventory ‌of ​what recovery ​feels ​like from ‌the ​inside.[2] To ‌say ​her name ‌in ​Old Norse ‌was ‌already to ​ask ​for the ​one ​thing medicine ‌and ‌prayer both ​promise.

Sources

  1. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. eir.
  2. Fjölsvinnsmál 38–40, trans. Larrington (2014).
