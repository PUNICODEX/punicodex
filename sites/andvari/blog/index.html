The hidden history behind Andvari

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar; this one rescues a story from a footnote. Andvari is the dwarf who owned the most consequential treasure in Norse mythology — the hoard of the waterfall Andvarafors and the ring Andvaranaut, whose curse runs through four generations of the Völsung cycle — and yet his name needs no rescue at all: Old Norse wrote it in unbroken ASCII. There is nothing to restore in the spelling and everything to restore in the telling. This post walks the full record — name, runes, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the careful one has no domain in the collection, because plain-ASCII andvari.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Andvari (andvari) — Dwarf, Guardian of Gold · Careful one — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Dwarf, Guardian of Gold". The name's traditional gloss is "Careful one", and the dictionary confirms the dwarf bears a speaking name: the common noun andvari means care, solicitude, watchfulness.

Andvari is the dwarf who lived in the waterfall Andvarafors in a pike's shape, sweeping his hoard about him with his fins. When the Æsir owed Hreiðmarr a wergild for his shape-changing son Ótr, Loki borrowed Rán's net, dragged the falls, and stripped the dwarf of everything down to the last hidden ring — and Andvari answered with the curse that governs the rest of the cycle: the gold would be the death of two brothers, strife to eight of the kin-line, and joy to no one. Fáfnir's dragon, Sigurðr's sword, and the hoard sunk in the Rhine are all that sentence working itself out.

PuniCodex restores the name as Andvari — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the figure. No domain is claimed: andvari.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old Norse as Andvari — a weak masculine noun with no added nominative ending, so the name ends in plain -i. Cleasby–Vigfusson record the dwarf and the common noun under the same headword: andvari, 'care, solicitude'. The dwarf's name is his character — the careful one, the watcher — which is the myth's quiet irony, because the story exists to show care failing: the most watchful keeper in the corpus is caught in an afternoon, by a borrowed net, in his own water.

The ring takes its name from him in the same dictionary: Andvara-naut, 'Andvari's jewel' — a name coined by the loss, since the corpus never uses it until after Loki has taken it. Owner and ring are bound in the lexicon itself.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᛅᚾᛏᚢᛅᚱᛁ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which the name would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs a-n-t-u-a-r-i.

That detail matters in the usual way: the runic row underspecifies what the manuscripts fix. The ᛏ rune writes both t and d, and ᚢ (úr) writes v — so the carving of the name would not, by itself, tell you whether the middle consonants were voiced. No Viking-Age carving of the name is known — the rendering is a normalized reconstruction, and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Eddas and Völsunga saga, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈɑnd.vɑ.rɪ/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: AHND-vah-ri — three even syllables, stress on the first, the v soft as in 'very', the r trilled, the ending a short 'ih'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Andvari, the dwarf of Andvarafors; also the common noun andvari, 'care, solicitude'
  • Younger Futhark — ᛅᚾᛏᚢᛅᚱᛁ, the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Reginsmál's prose — Gustr, 'Gust', the other name the text gives the dwarf in the curse itself
  • Modern Icelandic — Andvari, revived as the title of the learned journal of the Icelandic Literary Society, founded 1874

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. Both a's are short and open. And the final -i is the weak-declension ending: the name carries no added nominative -r, unlike the gods' names around it.

Mythology

Andvari's mythology is one afternoon's extortion and a four-generation echo. The corpus tells the catching three times — Reginsmál's prose, Snorri's Skáldskaparmál, and Völsunga saga — with unusual agreement, and then spends the rest of the Völsung cycle proving the curse.

The Otter's Ransom (Reginsmál prose · Skáldskaparmál)

Óðinn, Loki, and Hœnir came to a waterfall and saw an otter on the bank eating a salmon, its eyes shut. Loki killed it with a stone and boasted of a double catch — but the otter was Ótr, shape-changing son of the farmer Hreiðmarr. That night Hreiðmarr and his sons Fáfnir and Reginn seized the gods and set the wergild by the skin itself: the otter's hide filled with red gold, then covered with gold until not a hair showed. Óðinn sent Loki down to Svartálfaheimr to find the gold where it lay — in the keeping of a dwarf at a waterfall.

The Net of Rán (Völsunga saga 14)

Loki went to Rán, the sea-queen who drowns sailors, and borrowed her net. At Andvarafors he cast it and caught the pike-shaped dwarf, and named the price: all the gold in the waterfall, or Andvari's head. The dwarf paid the hoard over — but kept one small ring hidden in his hand, pleading that with it he could breed gold again. Loki took that too: one coin left would be one too many.

The Curse on the Ring (Reginsmál prose · Völsunga saga 14)

Stripped bare, Andvari spoke. Reginsmál's prose preserves the sentence as the tradition's own words: the gold that Gustr had owned would be the death of two brothers and bring strife to eight of the kin-line, and no man would have joy of his wealth. The ring took its name from the loss — Andvaranaut, 'Andvari's jewel' — and even the last coin went into the ransom, covering the final hair of the otter-skin.

Fáfnir's Bed (Völsunga saga 14–20 · Fáfnismál)

The curse began at once. Hreiðmarr refused his sons a share; Fáfnir killed his father sleeping, took the hoard to the heath called Gnitaheiðr, and brooded on it until he became the worst of serpents. Reginn, cheated, fostered Sigurðr of the Völsungs, reforged the sword Gram, and egged him to the heath. Sigurðr stabbed up into the dragon's belly from a pit, killed Reginn on the nuthatches' warning, and loaded the hoard — Andvaranaut and all — on Grani's back. The third owner of the ring was the greatest hero of the North, and the curse did not care.

The Gold in the Rhine (Völsunga saga 24–31 · Atlakviða)

From Sigurðr the treasure passed as dowry and blood-price into the house of the Gjúkungar. When King Atli lured Gunnar and Högni to his hall for the hoard, the brothers did the last careful thing anyone does with Andvari's gold: they sank it in the Rhine before riding to their deaths — Högni's heart cut out, Gunnar harping with his toes in the snake-pit until an adder found his heart. Atlakviða closes the account with the river itself as the treasure's last keeper: the Rhine shall hold the strife-metal of the Niflungar. Two brothers dead, eight of the line at strife, no man the happier — the prophecy's arithmetic, complete.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Andvari is the iconography of a single robbery — the corpus fixed around his waterfall a set of objects no retelling can omit:

  • Andvaranaut — the ring taken last and cursed first, the treasure that kills every hand it passes through
  • Rán's net — the sea-queen's drowning-net, borrowed for an afternoon to do the Æsir's debt-collecting
  • Andvarafors — the waterfall named for its keeper, a hoard-hall that flows and cannot be locked
  • The pike-shape — the disguise of the careful: a form made for guarding, useless against a net
  • The otter's skin — the measure of his loss: gold counted out hair by hair until the hoard was gone

Archaeology & Evidence

Andvari himself is a purely literary figure: no runestone, votive deposit, or named image records him. But the cycle his gold sets in motion is the best-attested narrative in Viking-Age art. The Ramsund carving in Södermanland (Sö 101, c. 1030) cuts the whole Fáfnir episode into a rock face — Sigurðr thrusting up through the serpent's body, the slain Reginn with his smith's tools, Grani loaded with the treasure, and the birds whose speech the dragon-heart taught. The Gök stone (Sö 327) weaves the same scenes through a runic inscription — the serpent's body carries the rune-band itself, so the dragon Andvari's gold created literally holds the text. Related scenes reach as far west as the Isle of Man and the Halton cross in Lancashire. The manuscript witnesses are thin in the usual way: Reginsmál stands in the Codex Regius, Snorri's account in the codices of the Prose Edda, and Völsunga saga in the paper manuscript NKS 1824 b 4to (c. 1400), the saga's only complete medieval copy.

Realm & Domain

Andvari's domain is Dwarf, Guardian of Gold — and the sources give each term its full weight.

The Dwarf

Reginsmál's prose introduces him by kind before name: 'Andvari was the name of a dwarf'. He stands among the dvergar of Svartálfaheimr, the smiths and hoarders opposite the Æsir in every treasure-story.

Guardian of Gold

He kept his hoard by swimming it: in a pike's shape he swept through the falls with the gold about him, a guardian whose watchfulness is his very name. The pike-form is camouflage, not nature — a shape made for keeping, not fighting.

The Waterfall-Keeper

The falls bear his name: Andvarafors, 'Andvari's waterfall' — which is why it took a net, not a siege, to rob him.

Speaker of the Curse

His last domain is speech. The careful one's final act of care was to make sure the gold would care for no one else either.

Across Cultures

Andvari needs no foreign counterpart: the cursed-hoard complex is native Germanic, and the corpus supplies its own sharpest contrast in another dwarf-ring. Draupnir, forged freely by Brokkr and Sindri for the gods, drips eight new rings every ninth night — the given ring multiplies; the taken ring kills. Between them the two rings map the North's whole ethics of treasure. Continental heroic tradition keeps the same stratum: the hoard of the Nibelungenlied, won by Siegfried, sunk in the Rhine, and fatal to its holders, is the southern telling of the Andvaranaut complex — one inheritance, not a borrowing.

Cultural Legacy

Andvari's afterlife is one of the largest in mythology, worn under another name. Wagner's Alberich in Das Rheingold — the dwarf of the water's depth, robbed of his gold, who curses the ring and watches it destroy owner after owner through four operas — is built substantially on the Andvari of Reginsmál and Völsunga saga; the curse-motif that drives the entire Ring cycle is the sentence spoken at Andvarafors. William Morris's Sigurd the Volsung (1876) kept the dwarf's own name before English readers. In Iceland the name was revived with no curse attached: Andvari is the title of the learned journal of the Icelandic Literary Society, founded in 1874 — the careful one finally keeping something that multiplies without killing. And modern fantasy's treasury of cursed gold runs downhill from his waterfall.

A Meditation

His name is care itself — and the myth is merciless to it. The most careful keeper in the corpus is caught in an afternoon, by a borrowed net, in his own water, and stripped to the last hidden ring. Care could not save him, because care guards against theft, and what came for him was law: a wergild, a skin to be covered hair by hair. What he had left was speech, and he used it like a dwarf — precisely, vindictively, with perfect aim. The curse is the one thing he made that nobody could take, because it was already given. Everything after is arithmetic, and the myth lets the numbers run for four generations to teach the lesson the North never tired of: gold taken by force does not change owners; it changes victims.

The Unicode Restoration

Andvari is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Norse Andvari carries none of these. The manuscripts write seven plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the dwarf; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.
