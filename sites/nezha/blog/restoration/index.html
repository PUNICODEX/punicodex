Nézhā: the stories first, then the spelling

You cannot weigh an accent without knowing what it carries. Here is what Nézhā has been carrying.

The Stories That Carry the Name

The Flesh Ball. Lady Yīn carries her third child for three years and six months. When the birth finally comes, what emerges is not an infant but a ball of flesh, and Li Jing, garrison commander of Chéntānguān, strikes at it with his sword, certain it is a demon. The ball splits open — and out steps a boy already able to walk and speak, a divine embryo sent down as the disciple of the immortal Taiyi Zhenren.

The Dragon King's Third Son. Bathing in the Nine-Bend River where it meets the East Sea, the boy dips his Red Armillary Sash in the water — and the sash, an instrument of cosmic order, churns the sea until the palace of the Dragon King Āo Guǎng trembles on its foundations. When the Dragon King's third son, Āo Bǐng, rises to punish the offender, Nézhā kills him. A child has bloodied the bureaucracy of the waters, and the Dragon King of the East Sea will have justice from heaven itself.

Flesh to Father, Bone to Mother. To spare his parents the Dragon King's vengeance, Nézhā takes his own life — and frames the deed as an accounting: he returns his flesh to his father and his bones to his mother, paying back the body they gave him so that his crime dies with him and no debt remains to be collected from his house. It is the most shocking sentence in Chinese popular mythology, filial piety pressed so hard it becomes separation. The motif is old — Chan Buddhist texts of the Song dynasty already speak of Nézhā rending flesh and bone to repay his parents.

And Here Is What the Spelling Preserves

Against those stories, the attestation: 哪吒, as Fēngshén Yǎnyì (Investiture of the Gods) and Wu Cheng'en, Xīyóujì (Journey to the West) preserve it. The name means "Nezha (from Sanskrit Nalakuvara)".

The restoration keeps 2 features the ASCII form nezha cannot show:

  • e → é — Stress on e: where the voice peaks.
  • a → ā — Long vowel: how long the vowel is held.

A storyteller pronouncing the name would restore every one of them by instinct. The file only writes down what the voice already knows.

The Name Travels

Nézhā is himself a product of transmission: the name enters Chinese through Buddhism as a transcription of Sanskrit Nalakūvara, a son of the guardian king Vaiśravaṇa, and the earliest Chinese forms (那吒, read closer to Nata) preserve that foreign sound. As the figure was naturalized, the genealogy was rewritten — his father became the historical-flavored general Li Jing, who absorbed Vaiśravaṇa's iconography wholesale and became the pagoda-bearing Heavenly King (托塔天王) of the Chinese celestial court.

The Address It Earned

The temple of Nézhā stands at /nezha/ — lore, scholars, patterns — because a name carrying this much story deserves more than a parking page. What survives here cannot be shown in ASCII: a feature of the original that the fallback alphabet flattens. That is the Tier-1 test, and this name passes it without argument.
