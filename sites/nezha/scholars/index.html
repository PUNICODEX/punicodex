01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Nézhā (nezha) ​— Youth, War, ‌Rebellion · Nezha (from ‌Sanskrit Nalakuvara) — ​belongs to the ​Chinese tradition, where ​it is catalogued under ​the domain "Youth, ​War, Rebellion". The ‌name means "Nezha (from ​Sanskrit Nalakuvara)"[1].

Nézhā ​is the child ‌who would not ​stay born. Third son ‌of the garrison ​commander Li Jing, ‌he came into ​the world after three ​and a half ​years of pregnancy ​— not as an ​infant but as ‌a ball of ​flesh, which his ‌father split with a ​sword to reveal ‌a walking, talking ​boy. The immortal ‌Taiyi Zhenren took him ​as a disciple ‌and armed him ​as no child has ​ever been armed: ‌the Universe Ring, ‌the Red Armillary ‌Sash, the Fire-Tipped Spear, ​and the Wind ​Fire Wheels on ‌which he rides ​the sky.

What follows ​is one of ‌the most harrowing ​arcs in Chinese myth: ​a boy who ‌kills the Dragon ​King's son, returns ‌his flesh to his ​father and his ​bones to his ​mother to spare ​them heaven's vengeance, and ‌is rebuilt from ‌lotus roots and ​blossoms — a body ‌owed to no ​one. From there ​he becomes the ‌vanguard of Jiāng Zǐyá's ‌Zhou army in ‌the Investiture of ‌the Gods and, ​in Journey to the ‌West, the celestial ​marshal sent to ​battle Sūn Wùkōng.[2]

PuniCodex ​restores the name ‌as Nézhā and ​serves its temple ​at nézhā.com. The restoration ​preserves at least ‌one distinctive feature ​— a diacritic ​or a distinctive letter ​— that the ‌ASCII form loses, ​and exactly one historically ‌valid Unicode restoration ​exists, which places ‌the name in ​Tier 1. The plain ‌ASCII form nezha ‌survives as a ​modern convenience imposed ​by the early domain-name ​system; the restoration, ​not the fallback, ‌is the form the ‌project defends as ​philologically complete[3].

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested in ‌Chinese ​characters as ​哪吒. Etymologically ​it ​means "Nezha ​(from Sanskrit ‌Nalakuvara)"[1].

​The ASCII ​form nezha ‌survives only ​because ‌the early ​domain-name system ‌could ​not carry ​diacritics; it ​is ​a technological ​compromise, not ‌an ​ancient spelling. ‌The Unicode ​restoration ‌Nézhā recovers ​both the ‌stress ​accent and ‌the vowel ​length ​of the ‌original directly ‌in the ‌address ​bar. The ​restoration preserves ‌at ​least one ​distinctive feature ‌— ​a diacritic ​or a ‌distinctive ​letter — ‌that the ​ASCII ​form loses, ​and exactly ​one ‌historically valid ‌Unicode restoration ​exists, ‌which places ​the name ​in Tier ‌1.

‌The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

‌- ​n → ‌N — ​Same, ​capitalized

  • ​e → ‌é ​— Stress ​on e

​- ‌z → ​z — ​Same
​- h ‌→ h ​— ‌Same

  • ​a → ‌ā — ​Long ‌vowel

The ‌project holds ​the ​domain nézhā.com ​(xn--nzh-bma6j.com) as ​the ‌canonical home ‌of this ​name[2].

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of the name ‌is /nɤ˧˥ ʈʂa˥/ ‌— Modern Standard ​Mandarin (Pinyin).[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • Né- ​— Voiced alveolar nasal ​[n] plus the ​unrounded close-mid back vowel ‌[ɤ], with Tone ​2 (rising, ˧˥). ​哪 carries this reading ‌only in the ​theophoric name; its ordinary ‌interrogative reading is ​nǎ (Tone 3).
  • ‌-zhā — Voiceless ​unaspirated retroflex affricate ​[ʈʂ] plus open central ​vowel [a], with ​Tone 1 (high level, ​˥). Pinyin 'zh' ‌denotes the retroflex ​series, not the English ‌'zh' of 'measure'.

​For the modern speaker, ‌the closest approximation ​is: NUH-jah — the ‌first syllable rises ​like a question, ‌the second stays high ​and level; keep ​the 'zh' retroflex, tongue ‌curled back, closer ‌to 'dr' than to ‌'j'.

Kindred and ​historical forms of ​the name:

  • Traditional ‌— 哪吒
  • ​Simplified — 哪吒
  • ​Wade-Giles — No²-cha¹

‌- Older transcriptions ​— 那吒 Nàzhā / ​Nata, the earlier ‌Buddhist transcription of Sanskrit ​Nalakūvara

  • Religious ‌title — 中壇元帥 Zhōngtán ​Yuánshuài (Marshal of ​the Central Altar)

​- Related terms — ​乾坤圈 Qiánkūnquān (Universe ‌Ring); 混天綾 Hùntiānlíng (Red ‌Armillary Sash); 風火輪 ​Fēnghuǒlún (Wind Fire ‌Wheels)

哪吒 is the ​settled transcription of ​a name that entered ‌Chinese through Buddhist ‌transmission from Sanskrit Nalakūvara, ‌son of the ‌guardian king Vaiśravaṇa. ​The Modern Standard Mandarin ‌reading is Nézhā: ​吒 zhā (Tone 1) ​is the value ​recorded in the Unihan ‌Database (Unicode Consortium, ​kMandarin) and standard ​dictionaries, while 哪 carries ​here its special ‌proper-name reading né (Tone ​2), preserved only ​in this name ​and recorded as such ‌in the standard ​dictionaries (Xiandai Hanyu Cidian, ‌Hanyu Da Cidian); ​its ordinary interrogative reading ‌is nǎ (Tone ​3), the value ‌given under kMandarin in ‌the Unihan Database. ​Because the name is ​a medieval transcription ​of a foreign theonym ​rather than a ‌native Chinese word, ‌Old Chinese reconstruction does ​not apply.

  1. Fēngshén Yǎnyì (Investiture of the Gods). ↗

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌written in ‌Chinese characters ​as ​哪吒. This ​original script ​is the ​form ‌against which ​the ASCII ​fallback ‌nezha and ​the PuniCodex ‌restoration Nézhā ​are ‌measured: the ​restoration preserves ​both its ​pitch ​accent and ​its vowel ‌quantity of ​the ‌written form, ​so that ‌a reader ​typing ‌the modern ​address still ‌speaks the ​ancient ​name.[1]

The ‌original script ‌is ‌哪吒, identical ​in traditional ​and simplified ‌Chinese. ​Pinyin Nézhā ​combines the ‌special proper-name ​reading ​né (Tone ‌2) of ​哪 — ‌preserved ​only in ​this name, ​the ordinary ​interrogative ‌reading being ‌nǎ — ​with 吒 ‌zhā ​(Tone 1), ​as recorded ‌in ‌the standard ‌dictionaries and ‌the Unihan ​Database. ‌The name ​is a ​medieval transcription ​of ‌Sanskrit Nalakūvara, ​so no ​Old Chinese ​reconstruction ‌applies. The ​tone-marked restoration ​Nézhā maps ​directly ‌to the ​registrable domain ‌form: the ​display ‌keeps both ​diacritics (é ‌and ‌ā, both ​registrable IDN ​characters), and ​no ​apostrophe is ‌required anywhere ‌in the ​name.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Nézhā is ​the child who would ‌not stay born. ‌Third son of the ​garrison commander Li Jing, ​he came into ​the world after three ​and a half ​years of pregnancy — ‌not as an ​infant but as a ​ball of flesh, ‌which his father split ​with a sword to ‌reveal a walking, ​talking boy. The immortal ‌Taiyi Zhenren took ​him as a disciple ​and armed him ​as no child has ​ever been armed: ​the Universe Ring, the ‌Red Armillary Sash, the ​Fire-Tipped Spear, and ‌the Wind Fire Wheels ​on which he ‌rides the sky.

What ​follows is one ‌of the most harrowing ​arcs in Chinese ‌myth: a boy who ​kills the Dragon King's ​son, returns his ‌flesh to his father ‌and his bones ‌to his mother to ​spare them heaven's ​vengeance, and is rebuilt ‌from lotus roots ​and blossoms — a ​body owed to no ‌one. From there ​he becomes the vanguard ​of Jiāng Zǐyá's ‌Zhou army in the ​Investiture of the ‌Gods and, in Journey ​to the West, ​the celestial marshal sent ​to battle Sūn Wùkōng.[1]

​### The Universe ‌Ring

Qiánkūnquān, the golden ‌ring of heaven ​and earth, first of ‌the weapons Taiyi ​Zhenren bestowed — a ​cosmic bracelet that ‌strikes with the weight ‌of the world it ‌names.

The ‌Eastern Sea

Bathing in ​the sea, Nézhā ‌stirred the waters with ​his sash until ​the Dragon King's palace ​shook — the ‌act that killed Āo ​Bǐng and set the ​cycle of debt ​and sacrifice in motion.

‌### The Lotus ​Rebirth

His soul rebuilt ​from lotus roots ​and blossoms by Taiyi ‌Zhenren, Nézhā returns ​in a body owing ‌flesh to no parent ​— purity, renewal, ‌and freedom from the ​debt of birth.

‌### The Wind Fire ‌Wheels

Fēnghuǒlún, the ​paired wheels of wind ​and flame beneath ​his feet — the ​mount that carries the ‌celestial vanguard faster ‌than storm across the ​three realms.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

The Nézhā cycle ​is the great Chinese myth ‌of the filial rebel — ‌a story in which the ​most radical act of disobedience and ​the most extreme act of ​filial piety turn out to ​be the same act. Its ​canonical form was fixed in ‌the Ming dynasty, above all ​in the Investiture of the ​Gods.[1]

The Flesh Ball (Birth)

‌Lady Yīn carries her third ​child for three years and ‌six months. When the birth ​finally comes, what emerges is ‌not an infant but a ​ball of flesh, and Li ​Jing, garrison commander of Chéntānguān, strikes ​at it with his sword, ​certain it is a demon. ​The ball splits open — ‌and out steps a boy ​already able to walk and ‌speak, a divine embryo sent ​down as the disciple of ‌the immortal Taiyi Zhenren. The wrongness ​of the birth is the ‌point: Nézhā enters the world ​already owing nothing to ordinary ‌nature.[2]

The Dragon King's ​Third Son (East Sea)

Bathing ​in the Nine-Bend River where ‌it meets the East Sea, the ‌boy dips his Red Armillary ‌Sash in the water — ​and the sash, an instrument ​of cosmic order, churns the ‌sea until the palace of ​the Dragon King Āo Guǎng ​trembles on its foundations. When the ‌Dragon King's third son, Āo ​Bǐng, rises to punish the ​offender, Nézhā kills him. A ‌child has bloodied the bureaucracy ​of the waters, and the ‌Dragon King of the East ​Sea will have justice from ​heaven itself.

Flesh to Father, ​Bone to Mother (Sacrifice)

To ​spare his parents the Dragon ‌King's vengeance, Nézhā takes his ‌own life — and frames ​the deed as an accounting: ‌he returns his flesh to ​his father and his bones to ​his mother, paying back the ‌body they gave him so ‌that his crime dies with ‌him and no debt remains ‌to be collected from his ​house. It is the most ‌shocking sentence in Chinese popular mythology, ​filial piety pressed so hard ​it becomes separation. The motif ​is old — Chan Buddhist ‌texts of the Song dynasty ​already speak of Nézhā rending ​flesh and bone to repay ​his parents.

The Lotus ‌Incarnation (Rebirth)

His wandering soul is ​gathered by Taiyi Zhenren, who ​rebuilds him from lotus roots ​for limbs and lotus blossoms ‌for flesh — the lotus ​rebirth. Remade, Nézhā becomes what ‌the old body never could ​be: a warrior free of the ‌debt of birth. In the ​Investiture of the Gods he ‌rides his Wind Fire Wheels ‌as vanguard of Jiāng Zǐyá's ​Zhou army in the overthrow ​of the Shang; in Journey ​to the West he serves heaven ​as a celestial marshal, sent ‌down to do battle with ‌Sūn Wùkōng — one rebel ​conscripted against another.

  2. Nezha (encyclopedia entry). ↗

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Nézhā ​is himself ‌a product ‌of transmission: ​the name ​enters Chinese ​through Buddhism ​as a ​transcription of ‌Sanskrit Nalakūvara, ​a son ​of the ‌guardian king ​Vaiśravaṇa, and ‌the earliest ​Chinese forms ‌(那吒, read ​closer to ​Nata) preserve ​that foreign ​sound. As ​the figure ‌was naturalized, ​the genealogy ‌was rewritten ​— his ‌father became ​the historical-flavored ‌general Li ​Jing, who ‌absorbed Vaiśravaṇa's ​iconography wholesale ​and became ‌the pagoda-bearing ‌Heavenly King ‌(托塔天王) of ​the Chinese ​celestial court. ‌Taoism then ​claimed the ​child outright: ‌under the ​title Zhōngtán ​Yuánshuài, Marshal ‌of the ​Central Altar, ‌Nézhā became ​one of ​the most ​vividly worshipped ​protectors in ‌the Chinese ‌ritual pantheon. ​The figure ‌traveled with ​the Chinese ​world — ‌to Korea, ‌Vietnam, and ‌Japan (where ‌he is ​Nataku) — ‌but it ​is in ​the Sinitic ​ritual tradition, ‌and above ​all in ​the temples ​and processions ‌of Taiwan ​and the ​southeastern diaspora, ​that the ‌lotus-born marshal ​remains a ‌living god ​rather than ‌a literary ​memory.[1]

Within ‌the Chinese ‌tradition, closely ​related names ​in the ​corpus include ​Bāguà, Cháng'é, ‌Fúxī, Guāndì, ‌Guānyīn, and ​Hòuyì.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

No ​Chinese deity ‌has ‌had ​a stronger ​twenty-first ​century. Nézhā's ​cult ​thrives ‌in Taiwan ​and ​the ‌diaspora, where ​the ‌Marshal of ​the ‌Central ​Altar dances ​in ​temple processions ​— ​in ‌recent decades ​to ‌electronic ​music, the ‌'techno ​Nézhā' of ‌modern ​festival ‌culture. On ​screen, ​the 2019 ‌animated ‌feature ‌Ne Zha ​became ​the ‌highest-grossing animated ​film ​in Chinese ‌history, ​its ​defiant reading ‌of ​the myth ‌— ​fate ​is a ​rumor ​the child ‌refuses ‌— ​resonating with ‌a ​generation; ​Ne Zha ‌2 ‌(2025) went ‌further, ‌breaking ​global box-office ‌records ​for animation. ​The ​rebel ‌child who ​returned ​his ​body to ‌his ​parents has ​become, ​arguably, ‌the most ​popular ‌Chinese deity ​of ‌the ​modern era: ‌an ‌emblem ​of youth ​that ​will not ​be ‌told ‌what it ​owes.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Nézhā ​is a ‌textual rather ‌than ​an archaeological ​figure, and ​his ​attestation history ​tracks the ‌Buddhist ​transmission itself. ​The name ‌first appears ​in ‌Tang-dynasty Buddhist ​translations as ‌那吒 ​(Nata), a ​transcription of ​Sanskrit ​Nalakūvara, son ​of the ‌guardian ​king Vaiśravaṇa, ‌whose cult ​flourished ‌in Tang ​China and ‌whose ​iconography — ‌armor, spear, ​and ​reliquary pagoda ‌— later ‌passed to ‌Nézhā's ​literary father, ​Li Jing. ‌By ​the Song ​dynasty the ‌flesh-and-bone-returning ​motif was ​current in ‌Chan ​literature, and ‌Yuan-era drama ​gave ​the child ​a stage ​life. ‌The canonical ‌narrative was ​fixed ‌in the ​Ming novels: ​the Investiture ‌of ‌the Gods ‌supplied the ‌full ​cycle from ‌flesh ball ​to ​lotus rebirth, ​and Journey ‌to ​the West ​installed him ​as ‌a marshal ​of the ​celestial ​court. Material ‌attestation is ​chiefly ‌cultic — ​temple statuary ‌of the ​Marshal ‌of the ‌Central Altar, ​a ​wheel-riding, ring-bearing ​youth, across ​southeastern ‌China, Taiwan, ‌and the ​diaspora.[1]

Classical Texts

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Nézhā's ​literary ‌file is ‌late ​but ​enormous: the ​cycle ​was ​fixed in ‌the ​Ming ​vernacular novels. ‌The ​Fēngshén ‌Yǎnyì (Investiture ​of ‌the ​Gods) gives ​the ​fullest ​account — ​the ‌flesh-ball ​birth, the ‌killing ​of ‌the Dragon ​King's ‌third ​son Āo ‌Bǐng, ​the ​self-sacrifice that ‌'returns ‌flesh ‌to father ​and ​bones ‌to mother', ​and ​the ‌lotus-body resurrection ​by ​the ‌immortal Taiyi ​Zhenren.[1] ‌The ​Xīyóujì (Journey ​to ​the ​West) knows ‌him ‌as ​a seasoned ‌celestial ​marshal ​who battles ‌Sūn ‌Wùkōng ‌on heaven's ‌behalf. ​The ‌oldest kernel ​of ​the ​sacrificial motif ‌appears ​earlier ​still, in ​the ‌Song-dynasty ​Chan record ​Wǔdēng ​Huìyuán, ‌where Nézhā ​returns ‌his ​flesh and ‌bones ​to ‌his parents ‌and ​then ​preaches the ​dharma ​to ‌them from ‌a ​lotus.[2]

Daoist Sources

Buddhist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​name ‌itself ‌is ​a ​Buddhist ​import: ​Nézhā ​transcribes ‌Nalakūbara ​(also ​Nalakūvara), ‌third ​son ‌of ​the ‌yakṣa-king ​Vaiśravaṇa, ​carried ​into ​Chinese ​with ‌the ​Tang-dynasty ‌esoteric ​translations ‌of ​Vaiśravaṇa ‌ritual ​manuals ‌associated ​with ​Amoghavajra.[1] ‌The ‌Indian ‌guardian's ​son ​was ‌gradually ​naturalized ​— ‌given ​a ​Chinese ‌garrison-commander father, ​Li ‌Jing, ​who ​inherited ​Vaiśravaṇa's ​pagoda ‌— ‌until ​the ‌foreign ​prince ​became ‌the ‌most ‌Chinese ‌of ​rebel ‌children. ​The ​Wǔdēng ​Huìyuán ‌preserves ​the ​transitional ​figure: ‌a ​Nézhā ​who ​sheds ‌his ​borrowed ‌body ​and ‌preaches ​to ‌his ‌parents, ​a ​Buddhist ​sermon ​wearing ‌a ‌Chinese ​myth.[2]

Calligraphy & Script

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

哪吒 ​is ‌a ‌transcription fossil: ​both ​graphs ​were ​pressed ​into ‌service ​to write ​a ‌foreign ​name ‌and ​mean ‌almost ​nothing else. ​哪 ​(né) ​pairs ​the ‌mouth ​radical ‌口 — ​the ‌scribal ​convention ‌marking ​a ‌syllable to ​be ​pronounced, ‌not ‌parsed ‌— ​with ​the phonetic ‌那; ​吒 ​(zhā) ‌likewise ​couples ​口 ‌with the ​phonetic ‌乇.[1] ​The ​mouth-radical ​flag ​is ‌the same ‌device ​Buddhist ‌translators ​used ​for ‌Sanskrit ‌syllables across ‌the ‌canon, ​so ‌the ​deity's ​name still ​shows ‌its ​passport: ​two ​characters ‌that ​exist, essentially, ​to ​say ‌'Nézhā'. ​The ‌Japanese ​reading ‌Nata and ​the ‌older ‌form ​那吒 ​record ​the ​same loan ‌one ‌dialect ​apart.[2]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Nézhā ​teaches that ‌the deepest ‌bond and ​the deepest ​freedom can ​be purchased ​with the ​same ‌act. He ​does not ​simply disobey ‌his parents, ​as the ‌Western rebel ​does; he ‌pays ​them back ​— flesh ​for flesh, ​bone for ​bone — ‌and discovers ​that what ‌remains ​after the ‌debt is ​settled is ‌not nothing ​but everything: ‌a self ​made of ​lotus, ‌owing its ‌shape to ‌no one's ​expectation. The ​myth refuses ‌the easy ​lesson. His ​sacrifice ‌is genuinely ​filial and ​genuinely annihilating; ‌his rebirth ​is genuinely ‌free and ​genuinely owed ​to ​a master's ​compassion.

To ‌name a ‌domain Nézhā ​is to ‌claim that ​paradox: the ​right ‌to begin ‌again without ‌denying where ‌you came ​from. The ‌lotus does ​not pretend ​it ​was never ‌in the ​mud. It ​rises out ​of it, ‌blooms above ​the water, ​and ​carries the ‌memory of ​the depths ‌in every ​petal — ‌the child ​of the ‌East ‌Sea who ​became the ​vanguard of ​heaven, still ​riding the ‌wind on ‌wheels of ​fire.[1]
