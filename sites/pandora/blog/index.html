Mythology

The stories of ​Pandōra circulate within the Greek corpus, where the figure embodies first woman, box of evils.

Primary Attestation (Sources)

Name and Meaning (Etymology)

The name means "All-gifted" and reflects the figure's role in the tradition. Pandṓra is the first woman in Hesiodic cosmogony, fashioned by the gods as a ​punishment for Prometheus's theft of fire. She opens the jar (pithos) and releases evils into the world, retaining only Hope. The story explains the origin of human suffering while framing womanhood within a ​pessimistic theological economics. The jar—often mistranslated as a box in later European retellings—was a storage vessel, and Hope's retention inside it has been debated since antiquity.

A Beautiful Evil, Built by Committee (The Making)

‌After Promētheus stole fire, Zeus ordered Hephaistos to 'mix earth with water, and to put in it the voice and strength of humankind,' shaping 'a beautiful evil' in the likeness of a modest ​maiden (Hesiod, Theogony 570-584). The gods then worked her over like a dedication: Athēnâ dressed her in silver-white robes and set a veil on her head; the Horae crowned her with spring flowers; ‌Aphrodítē 'shed grace upon her head and cruel longing and cares that weary the limbs'; Hermēs gave her 'a bitch's mind and a thievish character' and the gift of winning speech (Works and ​Days 60-80). Nothing in her is her own — she is a composite artifact, each Olympian's contribution a function. The myth makes the first woman a manufactured object, which is exactly the bitterness Hesiod ‌wants us to taste: Pandōra is a tool disguised as a bride.

Sent to Epimētheus (The Sending)

Zeus named her Pandōra — 'because all the Olympians gave her a gift, a bane to ​industrious men' (Hesiod, Works and Days 80-82) — and had Hermēs take her to earth with a great storage-jar. Promētheus had already warned his brother Epimētheus never to accept a gift from Olympian ‌Zeus; the Works and Days compresses the tragedy into a single line: Epimētheus 'took the evil, and only when he had it perceived the evil' (Op. 88-89). Apollodorus (Library 1.7.2) retells the same ​scene. The whole machinery of the myth depends on this one moment of ignored foresight: forethought is chained in the Caucasus, afterthought answers the door. From her, Hesiod adds in the Theogony (590-612), ‌descends 'the race of women' — a passage later Greeks found as uncomfortable as we do.

All the Evils Take Wing (The Jar)

Hesiod continues with terrible speed (Works and Days 90-105): 'the woman ​took off the great lid of the jar with her hands and scattered all these evils, bringing sorry troubles upon mortals. Only Hope remained there, inside, in the unbreakable home, under the lip ​of the jar, and did not fly out.' Before this, men lived 'free from ills... without the evil diseases that bring death'; now the jar's winged sorrows fill sea and land — 'for ‌the earth is full of evils, and the sea is full.' Hope stayed, Hesiod adds in a dark aside, 'by the plans of aegis-bearing Zeus,' leaving every reader to decide whether hope is ‌the one mercy left to mortals or the final bait sealed in with them. The vessel, again, was a pithos — a household storage jar; the tradition's later 'box' is Erasmus' sixteenth-century mistranslation, ‌not Hesiod's.

Pandōra is shown as a beautiful woman ​holding a large jar or pithos, often accompanied by Hephaistos, Athena, Hermes, and Eros. The lid of the jar is her defining attribute: lifted, it releases evils; replaced, it traps only Elpis (Hope).

‌The scene was popular on Attic vases and later became a moral allegory in Renaissance art.

  • The storage-jar (pithos) — The great earthenware vessel from which every mortal ill escaped — a household ​jar, which is precisely Hesiod's point: evil enters the world through the home
  • Clay — She was kneaded from earth and water by Hephaistos — a divine artifact built of human material
  • The floral ​crown — The Horae crowned her with spring flowers, and Athēnâ veiled her — beauty as the wrapping of the trap (Works and Days 72-76)
  • The girdle — Aphrodítē's contribution of longing and ‌care — desire sewn into the gift so that it cannot be refused

Epithets & Cult Titles

Pandōra's name is itself an epithet: Πανδώρα (Pandōra, "all-gifted"), because every Olympian gave her a gift. ​In some contexts she is called Ἀνησιδώρα (Anēsidōra, "sender-up of gifts"), a title that distinguishes her gifts from the evils released from her jar.

Both names express the ambiguity of the first woman: ​she is bane and blessing inseparably united.

The Homeric Hymns

The myth of Pandōra is told not in the Homeric Hymns but in the proem of Hesiod's Works and Days and in the ‌Theogony, both of which stand in the hymnic tradition. Zeus commands Hephaistos to mould the first woman as punishment for Prometheus's theft of fire, and the gods heap gifts upon her.

Hesiod's account is ​the foundational "hymn" of Pandora, explaining why toil and disease fill the human world.

Oracle Sites & Sanctuaries

Pandōra had no major oracular sanctuary, but her myth is tied to Thessaly, where Prometheus ‌shaped mankind from clay, and to the Acropolis of Athens, where a cult of Pandora was associated with Athena. The jar itself became a ritual object symbolizing the containment of misfortune.

Her absence ​from oracular sites underscores that she is a cosmic figure rather than a local goddess.
