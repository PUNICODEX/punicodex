01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ullr (ullr) ​— Glory, ‌splendor · Archery, ‌Hunting, Skiing ​— is the ​Norse god ​of the bow, ​the ski, ​and the ‌duel: son of ​Sif, stepson ​of Thor, lord ‌of Ýdalir, ​the Yew-Dales, 'such ‌a good ​bowman and so ‌fast on ​skis that no ​one can ​compete with him', ​and the ​god it is ‌good to ​call on ‌in single combat.[1]

​His dossier ‌is a paradox ​the handbooks ‌never tire of: ​among the ‌least narrated gods ​in the ​literature — a ‌stanza and ‌a half of ‌eddic verse, ​one sentence of ​Snorri, a ‌list of ​kennings — and ​among the ‌best attested in ​the ground, ​with scores of ‌theophoric place-names ​across Norway and ‌Sweden where ​Denmark and Iceland ​have none.[2] ​Atlakviða keeps his ​name on ‌the oath-ring ‌of the temple; ​Saxo Grammaticus ‌keeps the rumor ​that for ​ten years, as ‌Ollerus, he ‌bore Odin's name ‌and ruled ‌in his place.[3]

​PuniCodex restores ‌the name as ​Ullr — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, ‌because normalized ​Old Norse writes ​no diacritic ​in this name. ‌The mechanical ​rule therefore places ​the name ​in Tier 2: ‌nothing distinctive ​is lost in ‌transit, a ​statement about orthography ‌and never ​about the god. ‌The temple ‌is domain-less: ​the plain-ASCII ullr.com ​is registered ​to a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[4]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old ‌Norse as ​Ullr, and unlike ​many names ​in the ​northern onomasticon ​its etymology is ‌secure: it ​descends from ​Proto-Germanic *wulþuz, ‌'glory, splendor' — ​Gothic wulþus ‌'glory', Old ​English wuldor, ‌the word behind ​'Wuldorfæder', the ​Glory-Father of ​the biblical ​poems.[1] The sound ​changes are ‌regular: initial ​w was ‌lost before the ​rounded vowel, ‌the cluster ​lþ assimilated ‌to geminate ll, ​and the ‌old nominative ​ending became ​-r. The god's ‌name is ‌thus the ‌abstract noun ​made personal: 'Glory' ​itself.

The ‌ASCII form ​is not ​a fallback here ‌but the ​form itself: ​normalized Old ‌Norse writes four ​plain Latin ‌letters and ​a capital. ​The Unicode restoration ​Ullr therefore ​preserves nothing ‌the ASCII ‌alphabet loses, and ​the mechanical ‌rule classifies ​the name ​Tier 2 — ‌an orthographic ‌fact, not ‌a judgment ‌on the god.

​The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

​- u ​→ U — ​Same, capitalized
‌- l ​→ l ​— Same

  • ​l → ‌l — ​Same
  • ​r → r ​— Same

‌The project ​holds no ‌domain for this ​name. The ‌plain-ASCII ullr.com ​is registered ‌to a third ‌party outside ​the collection ​and is ​watched for release; ​this temple ‌claims nothing ‌it does ​not hold.[2]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈulːr/ — Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- U- — ​Short close ​back rounded [u], ‌held brief, closer ​to the ​vowel of English ‌'put' than of ​'pool'; every ‌Old Norse word ​stresses the first ‌syllable, here ​the only syllable
​- -ll- ​— The geminate ​lateral [lː], held ​audibly as ‌in modern Icelandic ​Ullur; the double ‌letter is ​the one articulatory ‌event the spelling ​records

  • ‌-r — The ​nominative masculine ending ‌from older ​*-z, a tapped ​or trilled [r] ‌closing the ‌syllable; in later ‌Icelandic it ​fused with the ​l, but the ‌reconstructed form ​keeps it sounded

​For the modern ‌speaker, the ​closest approximation is: ​'ULL-r' — one ‌firm beat, ​the vowel short ‌and rounded as ​in 'pull', ​the double l ​held a fraction ​long, the ‌final r sounded.

‌Kindred and historical ​forms of ‌the name:

  • ​Old Norse ​— Ullr, son ‌of Sif, stepson ‌of Thor

‌- Younger Futhark ‌— ᚢᛚᛚᚱ (u-l-l-r), ​the Viking-Age ‌runic rendering

  • ​Proto-Germanic — *wulþuz ​'glory, splendor'; ​Gothic wulþus, Old ‌English wuldor[2]
  • ​Latin — ​Ollerus, Saxo Grammaticus's ​Latinization in the ‌Gesta Danorum[3]

​- Modern Scandinavian ​— Ull ​(Norwegian and Swedish), ‌living above all ​in the ‌place-names: Ullensaker, Ullern, ​Ullevi, Ullensvang

Ullr ‌is Tier ​2 because the ‌restoration preserves nothing ‌the ASCII ​form loses: no ​diacritic, no distinctive ​letter, no ​vowel length. English ‌and Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Ullr — ​Old Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ‌13th c. ​CE in the ​Codex Regius ‌of the ​Poetic Edda ‌and the codices ​of Snorri's ‌Edda, recording ​material centuries older.[1] ​Before the ​Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse names ​were carved ‌in the ​Younger Futhark; ‌no runic attestation ​of this ‌theonym survives ​from Viking-Age Scandinavia, ‌so the ​manuscript form ‌is the primary ​witness. The ​runic rendering ‌is ᚢᛚᛚᚱ ‌(u-l-l-r).

The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ​step by ​step:

  • The ‌name is ​a simplex, ​not a ‌compound: four letters, ​one syllable, ​a geminate ‌ll the manuscripts ​write double.

‌- Old ​Norse orthography needs ​no diacritic ​here: the ​vowel is ‌short u, and ‌no distinctive ​letter (þ, ‌ð, æ, ǫ) ​occurs in ​the name.
‌- The ‌Unicode restoration Ullr ‌is therefore ‌identical to ​the ASCII form ‌— the ​restoration is ​a matter of ​canonical reference, ‌not rescued ​orthography.

  • ​The name's etymology ​is secure: ‌Proto-Germanic *wulþuz ​'glory, splendor' (Gothic ​wulþus, Old ​English wuldor), ‌with regular loss ​of initial ‌w before ​the rounded ‌vowel and assimilation ​of lþ ‌to ll.[2]

‌- The same ​name-element appears ​on the ​third-century Thorsberg ​chape (owlþuþewaz), the ‌earliest runic ‌evidence for ​its antiquity.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. Ullr; Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Ullr, Thorsberg chape.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ullr is ​catalogued under ‌Archery, Hunting, Skiing ‌— the ​rare case where ​the lexicon's ​gloss is a ​direct quotation ​of the sources' ‌own list: ​the bow, the ​hunt, the ‌snowshoe are ​precisely what Snorri ‌and the ​skalds assign him, ‌and the ​hall the poems ​give him ​is named for ​the bow-wood ​itself.[1]

Archery

‌'He is ​such a good ‌bowman... that ​no one can ‌compete with ​him': Snorri's first ‌office, confirmed ​by the skaldic ‌heiti boga-Áss, ​the Bow God, ​and by ‌the address ‌Grímnismál gives him ‌— Ýdalir, ​the Yew-Dales, named ​for the ‌tree that made ​the North's ​bows.[1]

Hunting

‌Veiði-Áss, the ​Hunting God, stands ​in Skáldskaparmál's ‌list of his ​names — ‌the bow's working ​purpose, the ​winter pursuit on ​snowshoes through ​the northern forest.[2]

‌### Skiing

‌He is 'so ​fast on ‌skis that no ​one can ​compete with ‌him', and the ‌skalds knew ‌him as öndur-Áss, ‌the Snowshoe ​God — the ‌oldest divine ​patron of travel ​over snow, ​and the source ‌of his ​modern Scandinavian afterlife ​as the ​god of skiers.[1]

‌### Single ​Combat

'It is ​good to ​call on him ‌in single ​combat', Snorri adds ‌— the ​duel, einvígi, ‌where one man's ​skill and ‌one god's favor ‌decide. With ​the oath-ring of ​Atlakviða, the ​attribute hints at ​a god ‌of formal, sworn ‌contest: combat ​under law.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 31, trans. Faulkes (1987); Grímnismál 5 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for Ullr), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Ullr is ‌functional rather ​than ​regal — ​the corpus ​fixes ​around him ‌the equipment ​of the ​winter ‌hunter and ​the tokens ‌of the ​sworn ‌word:[1]

  • ​The yew ​bow ​— the ​weapon of ​Ýdalir timber: ‌archery ​as divine ‌craft, the ​hall's name ‌as ​the god's ‌own advertisement[1]

​- ‌The snowshoes ​— öndurr, ​the ski ‌or ‌snowshoe: winter ‌travel raised ​to godhead; ​the ‌kenning öndur-Áss, ​Snowshoe God[1]
​- ‌The shield, ​'Ullr's ship' ​— the ‌skaldic ​kenning that ‌makes his ​shield a ​vessel: ​a fragment ​of a ‌lost ‌voyage myth[2]
​- The ‌oath-ring — ​the ​holy ring ‌of Atlakviða ‌by which ‌the ‌deepest oaths ​were sworn: ‌Ullr ​as guarantor ​of sworn ​speech[3]

  • ‌Ýdalir ​— the ​Yew-Dales, his ​hall in ‌Grímnismál's ​survey of ​heaven: one ​of ‌the few ​divine addresses ‌the poems ​give[1]

‌- The ​yew tree ‌— Taxus ‌baccata, ​the bow-wood ​of the ​North: ​the living ‌emblem of ‌his cult ​landscape[1]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ullr's mythology is ​a stanza and a half of ‌eddic verse, one sentence of ‌Snorri, a list of kennings, and ​a Danish fable — among ​the thinnest narrative dossiers of any ​god with so deep a ​footprint.

The Yew-Dales (Grímnismál 5)

​In Grímnismál's numbered survey of ‌the gods' dwellings, the fifth hall ​belongs to Ullr: 'Yew-Dales is ​the name of the place where ‌Ullr has made himself a ​hall.' The poem gives him no ‌lineage and no adventure — ​only an address, but an address ‌that declares his office: the ​yew is the bow-wood of the ​North. The poem touches him ​once more at its close, when ​the boy at the fire ​is blessed: 'the favor of Ullr ‌and of all the gods ​shall he have' — Ullr's ‌name first among the gods whose ​goodwill is invoked.[1]

The ‌Snowshoe God (Gylfaginning 31)

Snorri's whole ​account is one packed sentence: ‌Ullr, son of Sif and stepson ​of Thor, is such a ‌good bowman and so fast on ​skis that no one can ​compete with him; he is fair ‌of face, has a warrior's ‌accomplishments, and it is good to ‌call on him in single ​combat. Skáldskaparmál keeps the poets' shorthand ​— son of Sif, kinsman ‌of Thor, the Snowshoe God, the ​Bow God, the Hunting God, ​the Shield God — and the ‌skalds' name for the shield, ​'Ullr's ship', whose story Snorri no ​longer knows: somewhere behind the ‌verse, a lost myth had the ​god travelling on his shield ‌as on a vessel.[2]

The ​Ring of Ullr (Atlakviða 30)

​When Atli has betrayed the Burgundian ​kings, Guðrún reminds him of ​the oaths he swore long before ‌— by the sun and ‌the sun-hall, by Sigtyr's mount, and ​'by the ring of Ullr'. ‌The oath-ring, the holy arm-ring of ​the temple on which the ​deepest vows were sworn, is here ‌named as Ullr's own, and ‌nothing else in the corpus explains ‌the attribution: a bow-god with ‌a cult-ring suggests a god of ​the assembly and the sworn ‌word, a legal weight the myths ​no longer spell out.[3]

​Ollerus on the Throne (Saxo, Gesta ​Danorum III)

Saxo Grammaticus, euhemerizing ‌the gods as ancient kings ​and wizards, keeps the one adventure ​the Icelanders forgot. When Othinus ​was exiled for dishonoring the cult, ‌the gods put Ollerus — ​the Latin form of Ullr — ​in his place: for ten ​years he bore Odin's name and ‌rites and ruled at Uppsala, ​until the true Odin returned and ‌drove him out; he fled ​to Sweden and was slain by ‌the Danes as he tried ​to rebuild his glory. Saxo adds ‌the rumor that Ollerus was ‌such a cunning wizard he could ​cross the seas on a ​bone marked with terrible spells — ​the winter-god's ski or skate ​refracted through a Danish chronicler's Latin. ‌A god who can wear ‌Odin's name for a decade is ​no minor figure.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Ullr's true ​comparison class ‌is not ‌another mythology's ​gods but ​his own ​name's reach. ​Inside the ​corpus his ‌nearest kin ​is Skaði, ​the other ski-going ‌hunter of ​the northern ‌corpus; the ​skalds' pairing ‌of bow, ​snowshoe, and ​shield makes ​him the ​Æsir's winter ​counterpart to ‌the giant-bred huntress. ​His mythic ‌position — ​son of ‌Sif, stepson ​of Thor ‌— seats ​him in ‌the one ​household of ​the gods ‌Snorri trusted with ‌a family ‌tree, and ​Saxo's Ollerus, ​ruling in ‌Óðinn's place ​at Uppsala, ​keeps a ‌memory that ​this 'minor' ​god once ‌carried major authority.[1]

​Beyond the ‌corpus, the ​old etymological ​tie to ​Proto-Germanic *wulþuz, ​'glory' — ‌Gothic wulþus, ‌Old English ​wuldor — ‌led generations ​of scholars to ​see in ‌Ullr a ‌faded sky- ‌or splendor-god, ‌and nineteenth-century ​comparatists reached ‌for bow-bearing ​Apollo and ​other Indo-European ​hunters. Current ‌handbooks are cooler: ​Simek and ​Lindow emphasize ​what can ‌actually be ​shown — ​a cult ​dense in ‌Norway and ​Sweden, absent ‌from Denmark ​and Iceland, a ‌ring-oath and ​a hall, ‌and a ‌name meaning ​'Glory': the ​profile of ​a god ​whose importance ‌peaked before ‌the sources ​were written.[2]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Ullr's ​afterlife is ‌the snow ‌itself. When ​Scandinavia ​reinvented skiing ​in the ​nineteenth ​century as ‌sport and ​national emblem, ​the ‌old snowshoe ​god was ‌waiting: Norwegian ​ski ‌culture embraced ​Ull as ​its ​patron, and ​his name ​has crowned ‌winter ​festivals, ski ‌clubs, and ​slopes ever ‌since ​— the ‌only Norse ​god ‌whose modern ​cult is ​conducted on ‌mountains ‌with equipment.[1]

‌The name ​lives on ​as ‌a Scandinavian ​given name ​and, ‌far more ​densely, in ​the map: ‌Ullensaker, ​Ullern, Ullensvang, ‌Ullevi and ​their kin ​keep ​his sanctuaries' ​names long ‌after ‌the sanctuaries ​fell. Modern ‌Heathen and ​Ásatrú ​communities call ‌on him ‌for winter ‌sport ‌and for ​the sworn ‌word, ​reading the ​oath-ring of ​Atlakviða as ‌seriously ​as the ​bow. And ​in the ‌scholarly ​reception he ​stands as ​the ‌textbook case ​of a ‌great god ​the ‌literature lost ​— the ‌figure every ‌handbook ​uses to ​prove that ​the ​Edda's silence ‌is not ‌a cult's ​measure.[2]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Ullr's archaeology ​is written ‌across two ‌countries rather than ​buried in ​any one ​of them: ​no image and ​no votive ‌hoard can ​be securely assigned ​to him, ‌but the ​Scandinavian place-name record ‌is the ​densest of ‌any god outside ​the great ​three.[1] Norway ​keeps Ullensaker ​('Ullr's field'), Ullern, ​Ullensvang and ‌the lost ​Ullarhof; Sweden keeps ‌Ullevi and ​Lilla Ullevi ‌('Ullr's sanctuary'), Ulleråker ​('Ullr's field'), ‌Ullvi and ​their kin — ‌a cult ​landscape spread ​across south-eastern ‌Norway and eastern ‌middle Sweden, ‌with not ​one secure Ullr-name ​in Denmark ‌or Iceland.

​At Lilla Ullevi ​in Bro ‌parish, Uppland, ​excavations in the ​2000s uncovered ‌an Iron ​Age cult ‌building rich in ​amulet rings ​— a ​rare look at ​the kind ‌of site ‌the name advertises, ​though the ‌identification with ​the god rests ​on the ‌place-name alone.[1] ‌The oldest ‌possible attestation is ‌runic and ​continental: the ‌third-century Thorsberg shield-chape ​from Schleswig ​reads owlþuþewaz, ​whose first element ‌is the ​*wulþu- 'glory' ​that became the ​name Ullr ‌— whether ​theonym or ​personal name is ​disputed, but ‌it proves ​the name's antiquity.[2] ‌Later antiquarian ​tradition supplied ‌the familiar image: ​seventeenth- and ‌eighteenth-century Icelandic ‌paper manuscripts picture ​Ullr on ​skis with ​his bow ​— an early ‌modern reconstruction, ‌not a ​medieval witness.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never stages ‌Ullr — ​no poem ​gives him ​a line ​of dialogue, ​an errand, ‌or an ​enemy — ​yet it ‌keeps him ​in three ‌places, and ​each is ‌telling. Grímnismál ​5 numbers ​Ýdalir among ​heaven's first ​dwellings: 'Yew-Dales ​is the ‌name of ​the place ‌where Ullr ​has made ‌himself a ​hall' — ‌an address ​before Freyr's ‌Alfheim in ​the poem's ​own ordering. ‌Grímnismál 42 ‌closes the ‌poem's blessings ​with his ​name in ‌front: 'the ​favor of ​Ullr and ‌of all ​the gods ​shall he ‌have, ​whoever first ‌reaches the ​fire' — ​Ullar hylli, ​Ullr's favor, ​standing at ‌the head ‌of the ​catalogue of ‌divine goodwill.[1]

​And in ​Atlakviða, the ‌grimmest of ‌the heroic ‌lays, Guðrún ‌recalls the ​oaths Atli ‌swore and ​breaks: by ​the sun ​and the ‌sun-hall, by ​Sigtyr's mount ​— and ​'by the ‌ring of ​Ullr'. In ​a poem ​with no ‌other interest ​in the ‌gods, the ​temple oath-ring ‌is named ​as Ullr's ‌own: the ‌deepest sworn ​word in ​the heroic ​corpus rests ​on the ‌quiet god ‌of the ​yew-dales.[2]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 5 and 42 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Atlakviða 30 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Ullr's whole ‌Icelandic narrative ​witness, and ​it is ​exactly one ​sentence long. ​Gylfaginning 31: ‌'One is ​called Ullr, ​son of ‌Sif and ​stepson of ‌Thor. He ​is such ‌a good ​bowman and ​so fast ​on skis ​that no ​one can ‌compete with ​him. He ‌is also ​fair of ‌face and ​has a ‌warrior's accomplishments. ​It is good ‌to call ​on him ​in single ‌combat.' Snorri ‌compresses a ‌cult into ​a catalogue ​— parentage, ‌two sports, ​a beauty, ​and a ‌use.[1]

Skáldskaparmál ​then preserves ​what the ‌poets did ​with him. ‌Ullr may ​be called ​son of ​Sif, kinsman ​of Thor, ‌the Snowshoe ‌God, the ​Bow God, ‌the Hunting ​God, the ​Shield God; ‌and the ‌shield itself ‌may be called ‌'Ullr's ship' ​— a ‌kenning Snorri ​records without ​its story, ​the fragment ‌of a ​lost myth ​in which ​the god ‌travelled on ​his shield ​as on ​a vessel. ‌The words ​survived the ‌tale, which ​is the ‌whole shape ​of Ullr's ‌record: equipment ‌without narrative, ​names without ​myths, a ​god the ​specialists kept ‌long after ‌the stories ​were forgotten.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 31, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for Ullr and for the shield), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​Scandinavian runestone ‌invokes Ullr: ‌no ​inscription asks ​his favor, ​bears ​his theonym, ​or links ‌a ​dedicant to ​his cult ‌— ​a silence ‌he shares ​with ‌most of ​the gods, ​since ​the monumental ​runic corpus ​names ‌Óðinn, Þórr, ​and precious ‌few ​others.[1]

What ‌the runic ​record ‌does offer ​is older ‌and ​continental. The ​third-century Thorsberg ‌shield-chape ‌from Schleswig, ‌one of ​the ​earliest runic ‌inscriptions of ​all, ​reads owlþuþewaz ‌— whose ​first ​element, *wulþu- ‌'glory', is ​exactly the ‌Proto-Germanic ​noun that ​became the ​name ​Ullr. Whether ‌the compound ‌is ​the theonym ‌itself or ​a ​personal name ‌built on ‌it ‌is disputed; ‌either way, ​the ‌inscription proves ​the name-element ​was ​old, southern, ‌and martial ​centuries ​before the ​Eddas were ‌written.[2] ​In the ​Younger Futhark ​the ‌Viking-Age rendering ​would have ‌been ​ᚢᛚᛚᚱ (u-l-l-r) ‌— the ​short-twig ‌row underspecifying ‌nothing in ​this ​name, though ​no such ​carving ‌of the ‌theonym is ​known.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ullr.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Ullr, Thorsberg chape.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Ullr's ‌medieval afterlife ‌is Danish. ​Saxo Grammaticus, writing ​the Gesta ​Danorum before ​Snorri's Edda ​reached its final ‌form, euhemerizes ​him as ​Ollerus: when ‌Othinus was ​exiled for dishonoring ‌the cult, ​the gods ‌put Ollerus ​in his place, ​and for ​ten years ​he bore ​Odin's name and ‌rites and ​ruled at ‌Uppsala. On ​the true Odin's ‌return he ​was driven ‌out, fled ​to Sweden, and ‌was slain ​by the ​Danes as ‌he tried ‌to rebuild his ‌glory. Saxo ​adds the ​rumor — ‌fama est — ​that Ollerus ​was such ‌a cunning ​wizard that he ​crossed the ‌seas on ​a bone ‌marked with terrible ​spells, a ​detail long ​read as ​the snowshoe god's ‌ski or ‌skate seen ​through a ‌chronicler's Latin.[1]

The ​two traditions ​never reconcile, ‌and the ‌mismatch is ‌the evidence. Snorri ‌knows a ​sportsman of ‌one sentence; ​Saxo knows a ​usurper who ​wore Odin's ‌crown for ​a decade. A ​fable in ​which Ullr ‌can pass ​for the highest ​god is ​hard to ‌invent for ​a deity nobody ‌worshipped — ​it reads ‌as the ​last echo ‌of an age ‌when the ​god of ​the yew-dales ​stood nearer the ​center of ‌heaven than ‌the Icelandic ​record admits.[2]

Sources

  1. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 3, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ullr; Turville-Petre, E. O. G., Myth and Religion of the North (London: Weidenfeld & Nicolson, 1964).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Every ​tradition in ‌the lexicon ‌has its ​quiet god, ​the one ​the myths ​barely use ​and cannot ‌forget. Ullr ​is the ​North's. Grímnismál ‌names his ​hall among ‌the first ​of heaven's ‌addresses and ​puts his ​favor first ​among the ​gods' blessings; ​the poets ‌swear by ​his ring; ‌the map ​of two ‌countries is ​salted with ‌his holy ​places ‌— and ​Snorri, who ​can spend ‌pages on ‌a fishing ‌trip, gives ​him one ​sentence.[1]

The ‌discrepancy is ​itself the ​record. Whatever ‌Ullr was ​at the ​height of ‌his worship ​— bow-god, ‌sky-god, lord ​of the ​sworn oath, ​the Glory ​his name ‌still means ‌— it ​belonged to ‌an older ​layer of ​the religion ‌than the ‌Eddas preserve, ‌and ‌by the ​thirteenth century ‌only the ​specialists kept ​the tokens: ​a stanza, ‌a kenning, ​a rumor ​from Denmark ​that he ‌once sat ​in Odin's ​seat. Ullr ​teaches the ‌reader of ​myth a ‌hard and ​useful lesson: ‌the silence ​of a ‌source is ‌evidence about ​the source, ​never about ​the god. ​The yew-dales ‌are still ‌on the ​map.[2]

  2. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 3, trans. Peter Fisher (1979); Turville-Petre, E. O. G., Myth and Religion of the North (1964).
