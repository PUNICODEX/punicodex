Mythology

Stories of Kārttikeya

Kārttikeya's myths are the war-god's perfect résumé: born of fire and stars for one battle, victorious at seven days old, and never outgrown by the cults that made him their own. The Sanskrit epics and Kālidāsa give the north's version; the Tamil Sangam and the Kanda Purāṇam give the south his second, greater career.

Mahabharata · Kumārasambhava

The Son of Fire and Stars

The demon Tārakāsura had ravaged heaven for a thousand years under a boon: only a son of Śiva could kill him — and Śiva sat in ascetic mourning for his dead wife Satī, unmoved by gods, weapons, or time. The gods dispatched Kāma, god of desire, to shoot the ascetic; Śiva burned Kāma to ash with his third eye — and the seed of the awakened god was too hot for any bearer. Agni swallowed it and could not hold it; Gaṅgā took it and laid it in a thicket of reeds (śaravana); there the seed became six infants, found and suckled by the six Kṛttikā stars, the Pleiades (Mahābhārata, Vana Parvan's account of Skanda's birth; Kālidāsa, Kumārasambhava 13–15). Pārvatī pressed the six babies into one child with six heads — Ṣaṇmukha. Born of fire, carried by water, raised by stars, and fused by a mother's hands: no single parent could hold him, because every element conspired to make him.

The Purāṇic cycle

Seven Days Old, Commander of the Gods

While he was yet a week old, the gods elected the child senāpati — commander-in-chief of the armies of heaven — and his mother gave him the spear, the vel, that is his name and his body in every temple (the Mahābhārata's Skanda cycle; the Purāṇic accounts; Kālidāsa closes the Kumārasambhava with the gods' acclamation, canto 17). At the head of the gods' host he assaulted Tārakāsura's mountain fortress. The demon who had beaten Indra's thunderbolt and every weapon of the pantheon fell to a week-old general with his mother's spear; the thousand-year war ended in a single engagement. The tradition means the arithmetic exactly: the war the adults could not win, the infant finished, because force without freshness is only habit. The war-god who was never young enough to be underestimated is the pantheon's permanent argument that readiness is a decision, not an age.

Sangam literature

South to Murugan

His second great myth is his own migration. The Tamil Sangam anthology praises Murukan — 'the Beautiful' — in the Tirumurukāṟṟuppaṭai, one of the earliest long poems in the language, which maps his six hill-abodes (the later Arupadai Veedu) and already knows him as lord of love as well as war. In the south he wooed and won two wives: Devayānai, daughter of Indra, given to him for the Tāraka victory — the court marriage; and Vaḷḷi, the huntress of the hills, won by disguise and persistence in the telling preserved by the Kanda Purāṇam — the mountain marriage. The fuller cycle makes the theology explicit: the Sanskrit war-god and the Tamil hill-god are one, and his household itself marries the two civilizations. The commander who conquered Tāraka's fortress with one spear conquered a whole second civilization without drawing it — conquest by being loved, the one victory his father never managed.

The Palani legends

Thaipusam and the Kavadi

His living myth is the most extreme devotion in Hinduism. The temple legends of Palani tell its charter: Śiva ordered the asura Idumban to carry two hills — Śivagiri and Śaktigiri — slung on a shoulder-pole like a milkman's yoke, from Kailasa to the far south. Resting at Palani, Idumban found the hills suddenly occupied by a child who would not move them back; he fought the boy, fell, and was restored to life and made the guardian of the hill — and the pole-and-burden he carried became the kavadi his devotees still bear. At Thaipusam, at the Kṛttikā-star full moon, millions carry milk-pots and wooden kavadis, some pulling chariots with hooks in their skin, some piercing cheeks and tongues with small spears — the body offered to match the god's gift. From Palani to Sri Lanka's Kataragama to Malaysia's Batu Caves, the war-god's festival is among the largest religious gatherings on earth, and its charter is a demon taught that what the god asks you to carry, he will help you carry.

02

The Commander of the Gods

War, Youth, the Spear, and the Six-Faced One

Kārttikeya is India's god of war at his most beautiful: the six-faced son of Śiva, raised by six star-mothers, who took the spear at seven days old and destroyed the demon no god could touch — and who rides his peacock still through the temples of two nations that call him their own.

The Vel

His spear — the lance of victory over the unconquerable, given by his mother and sharp at seven days.

The Six Mothers

The Kṛttikās — the Pleiades who found him in the reed-thicket and nursed him, giving him his name.

Six Faces

Ṣaṇmukha: the six-faced god who sees every direction at once — the commander's total field of view.

The Peacock

His mount — the war-bird that eats serpents, whose dance is also a weapon.

Sacred Symbols

The vel (spear) Focused force — the weapon that ends what the assembled gods could not
The six faces Total sight — the commander who misses nothing on any battlefield
The peacock Pride harnessed to war — beauty made battle-standard
The Kṛttikās, the Pleiades Foster-motherhood raised to the heavens — the stars that named him
The rooster on his banner The war-crow announcing dawn — vigilance that never sleeps

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

कार्त्तिकेय

The name in its original Sanskrit form. Kārttikeya (कार्त्तिकेय) is attested in the source tradition — “Son of Krittika”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

kartikeya

Reduced to plain kartikeya, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Kārttikeya

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Kārttikeya restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Kārttikeya.com → xn--krttikeya-5bb.com

The non-ASCII characters in Kārttikeya are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Kārttikeya.

Pronunciation

How Kārttikeya was spoken

/kaːr.t̪i.keː.jɐ/ Reconstructed Sanskrit

Modern Approximation

KAHR-tih-kay-yah — the first vowel long, e always long in Sanskrit.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Sanskrit kārttikeya — IAST, with the long ā and the e unmarked (e and o are inherently long in Sanskrit and IAST never macrons them). The restoration Kārttikeya is the correct IAST form; Kārttikēya is a hypercorrection that mistakes the ISO 15919 convention for IAST.

Extended Lore

Kārttikeya is the god of arriving exactly as needed. Six mothers, one spear, seven days: nothing in his story wastes a moment. He asks the commander's question, which is also every late-starter's and every young team's: what are you waiting to grow up for — and what would you take on, if you knew you were already enough?
