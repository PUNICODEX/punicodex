Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script 后羿 Hòuyì — "The divine archer who shot down nine suns"
Unicode Restoration Hòuyì Restored stress, length, and script
Modern ASCII houyi Plain-ASCII fallback

后羿 names the divine archer: 后 is an archaic title meaning 'lord, sovereign' (not the later sense 'empress', for which 皇后 is used), and 羿 is the archer's given name. The Modern Standard Mandarin reading is Hòuyì (both syllables Tone 4), as recorded in the Unihan Database (Unicode Consortium, kMandarin) and standard Mandarin dictionaries. Baxter & Sagart (2014) reconstruct the Old Chinese reading of 羿 as *N-ŋ(r)ik-s. The tone-marked Pinyin restoration Hòuyì preserves the citation falling tone of both syllables; in connected speech the contour may be compressed, but the lexical tones remain Tone 4.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Hòuyì is the divine archer of Chinese myth, the hero who stood between heaven's excess and the earth's survival. In the age of the sage-emperor Yáo, the ten suns — three-legged crows, sons of Dìjùn and the sun-mother Xīhé — rose all at once instead of taking their appointed turns. Rivers dried, crops failed, and monsters crawled out of the scorched wilderness. Hòuyì drew his great bow and shot nine suns from the sky, sparing one so that day and night, sowing and harvest, could keep their order.

His story is a hinge between worlds: the same bow that cleared the earth of monsters also won him the elixir of immortality from Xīwángmǔ, the Queen Mother of the West — the elixir his wife Chángé would ultimately take, fleeing to the moon. The archer who saved the earth lost heaven, and Chinese myth has never stopped telling both halves of that story.

Hòuyì in Later Traditions

Hòuyì's myth traveled with Chinese civilization itself. In Japan the story of the archer and the ten suns was known through Chinese texts and pictorial art, where it informed depictions of solar catastrophe alongside native Amaterasu lore; Korea received the cycle through the same classical canon, and its own founding archer figure Jumong — whose very name is glossed as 'skilled archer' — shows how deeply the archer-hero type resonated on the peninsula. Vietnamese tradition likewise absorbed the ten-suns narrative through Chinese literature. Within China, Taoist tradition folded Yì into its cosmology of solar regulation and immortality elixirs, while the Queen Mother of the West, his benefactor, rose to become one of the highest deities of the Taoist pantheon. The myth also furnished Buddhist and popular writers with a ready vocabulary for speaking of cosmic excess and karmic reversal, though Yì himself remained a figure of the native classical tradition rather than a Buddhist convert. Scholars have long noted the parallel with other great archers — Apollo's plague-bringing arrows in Greece, Rama's bow in India — but the Chinese version is distinctive: the archer's target is not a rival god but disorder itself.

Modern Legacy

Hòuyì never left the Chinese imagination. The shooting of the suns is among the first myths Chinese children learn, retold in primers, animated films, and television dramas, and the Chángé sequel anchors the Mid-Autumn Festival, when families gaze at the moon where the archer's wife is said to dwell. Modern China has written the pair onto the sky itself: the national lunar exploration program is named Chángé, and its missions are spoken of as the moon goddess finally receiving visitors — a deliberate bridal of myth and engineering. The idiom of 'shooting the suns' survives as a metaphor for cutting down excess and correcting what has grown monstrous. In art, from Han dynasty tomb reliefs to contemporary illustration, the archer drawing his bow against a wheel of suns remains one of the most recognizable images in the Chinese visual canon.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Hòuyì in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Hòuyì, Archery, Suns, Heroism, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Hòuyì?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Hòuyì is /xoʊ̯˥˩.i˥˩/ — approximately HOH-yee — two crisp syllables, each spoken with a sharp falling tone, like a decisive command: 'HOE! YEE!'.

02What does Hòuyì mean?

Hòuyì means The divine archer who shot down nine suns in the chinese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Hòuyì?

Hòuyì is associated with The great bow and arrows (Heaven-sent precision; the power to correct cosmic disorder without destroying the cosmos), The three-legged crow (The true form of each sun; nine were shot down, and the tenth still crosses the sky), The Fusang tree (The cosmic mulberry at the eastern edge of the world where the ten suns roosted between journeys), The elixir of immortality (Xīwángmǔ's reward and the pivot of loss — the gift that carried Chángé to the moon), The spared sun (Measured judgment; the hero preserves the order he restores rather than abolishing it).

04Why restore Hòuyì in Unicode?

Plain ASCII houyi strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Hòuyì?

In the reign of Emperor Yáo, the ten suns — three-legged crows roosting in the Fusang tree, sons of Dìjùn and the sun-mother Xīhé — abandoned their orderly rotation and rose together. The earth burned: rivers dried, crops withered, and the people starved. Yáo called on Hòuyì, the divine archer, who climbed to the heights and loosed his arrows. One by one nine suns fell from the sky in their crow forms, and the tenth — spared — resumed its solitary, lawful journey. The myth is told across the Shān Hǎi Jīng tradition and given its fullest early form in the Huáinánzǐ.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Hòuyì and related cults.
  • Hòuyì's deeds are textual before they are pictorial. The earliest witnesses are Warring States literature: the Shān Hǎi Jīng preserves the ten-suns framework and the suns' parentage, the Chǔ Cí poem Tiān Wèn (Heavenly Questions, c. 4th–3rd century BCE) interrogates Yì's shooting of the suns and his tangled fate, and the Mèngzǐ records the Féngméng betrayal. The fullest surviving narrative is the Huáinánzǐ (139 BCE), which sets the archer in Emperor Yáo's reign and catalogs the slain monsters. In Han dynasty art the cycle became a staple of funerary iconography: tomb reliefs and painted stones at sites such as the Wu Liang shrines (Shandong, 2nd century CE) show the archer drawing against crow-bearing suns, while Mawangdui funerary art (168 BCE) depicts the solar crow and the moon's toad-and-hare company that would gather around Chángé. Han mirrors and later pictorial stones continued the imagery, fixing the bow, the crows, and the moon as the archer's permanent attributes.
