The hidden history behind Don

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Don is the opposite case, and in a strange way the most poignant one: the matriarch of the most magical family in Welsh literature, mother of Gwydion the enchanter and Arianrhod of the star-castle, attached to a name so plain that the medieval manuscripts wrote it in three unbroken ASCII letters. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain — because the woman herself is missing from her own story. She never speaks, never acts, never appears; she exists only in the names of her children. This post walks the full record — name, sound, family, sources, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the mother of the Welsh gods has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII don.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Don (don) — Mother of Gods · Goddess — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Mother of Gods". The name's traditional gloss is "Goddess", though its etymology is genuinely disputed.

Don is the matriarch of the Mabinogi's northern divine house: sister of Math fab Mathonwy, lord of Gwynedd, and mother of the kindred Welsh tradition called Plant Dôn, the Children of Dôn — Gwydion the enchanter, Gilfaethwy his brother, Arianrhod ferch Dôn, Gofannon the smith, and Amaethon the ploughman. She is the only major figure of the Four Branches who never speaks, acts, or appears: the whole of the Fourth Branch unfolds among her children and her brother, and she is present only in the matronymics they carry — uab Dôn, son of Dôn; uerch Dôn, daughter of Dôn.

PuniCodex restores the name as Don — identical to the ASCII input, because the Middle Welsh manuscripts write the name in plain Latin letters. Modern Welsh scholarship writes Dôn with a circumflex, but that mark is a modern length-sign, not a letter the manuscripts carry. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess.

The Name

The name is attested in Middle Welsh as Don, matriarch of the northern divine house, in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi and in Culhwch ac Olwen — always in the matronymic: Gwydion uab Dôn, Gilfaethwy uab Dôn, Arianrhod uerch Dôn, Gofannon uab Dôn, Amaethon uab Dôn. The genealogical tradition supplies her own lineage — Dôn daughter of Mathonwy — which makes her sister to Math fab Mathonwy and explains her children's standing at his court; the later genealogies give her Beli Mawr for a husband, a marriage the Mabinogi itself never mentions.

The etymology behind the lexicon's gloss "Goddess" is the standard one, and it is disputed. The traditional derivation runs to Common Celtic dēwā, 'goddess' — the very word the Welsh language still keeps in Dyfrdwy, its name for the River Dee; the competing reading takes the name as a river-name of the Danube–Don type. The comparison with Irish Danu, mother in name of the Túatha Dé Danann, has been a commonplace of Celtic studies since the nineteenth century, but no Welsh text equates the names, and the modern handbooks register the parallel without endorsing the identification.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Middle Welsh manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Don — attested in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1375–1420), the two great codices of the Mabinogi, recording material composed centuries earlier.

The scholarly rendering proceeds step by step. Middle Welsh writes the name in three plain Latin letters; the manuscripts carry no mark of any kind on the vowel. Modern Welsh orthography writes Dôn with a circumflex, but that mark is a modern length-sign for the long vowel /oː/, not a letter any manuscript carries — so the temple form follows the manuscript spelling. The Unicode restoration Don therefore changes nothing but the capital; the name stands in Tier 2 by the mechanical rule, and the plain-ASCII spelling is the philologically complete form.

Pronunciation

The pronunciation of the name is /doːn/ — Modern Welsh.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • D- — Voiced alveolar stop [d], fully voiced as in English 'door'
  • -ô- — Long close-mid back rounded [oː], the vowel modern Welsh orthography marks with the circumflex; a pure sustained 'oh', never the diphthong of English 'doe'
  • -n — Final alveolar nasal [n], closing the single syllable cleanly

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'DOHN' — one syllable, one long pure 'oh' held steady, like English 'dawn' with the vowel kept clean.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Middle Welsh — Don, the manuscript form, written without any length mark
  • Modern Welsh — Dôn, the same name with the modern circumflex
  • Welsh river-name — Dyfrdwy, the River Dee, from *Dēwā 'the goddess'
  • Irish comparison — Danu (or Dana), the standing handbook comparison, never an equation

Mythology

Don has no myth of her own — no tale, no speech, no deed the sources record. What she has is a family, and the family is the mythology.

The Sister-Sons at Math's Court (Math fab Mathonwy)

The Fourth Branch opens at the court of Math fab Mathonwy, lord of Gwynedd — who cannot live unless his feet rest in a virgin's lap, except in time of war — and introduces its great men by their mother's name: Gwydion and Gilfaethwy, sons of Don, Math's sister-sons. Gilfaethwy desires Goewin, the maiden of the foothold; Gwydion engineers a war against Pryderi of Dyfed with conjured stallions, hounds, and shields that vanish after a day; and while the king is in the field, the brothers force Goewin. Math's justice turns the brothers through three years of animal shapes — stag and hind, boar and sow, wolf and she-wolf — and takes them back into honour when the debt is paid. Through all of it the mother never appears: she is present exactly as a name inside other names.

Arianrhod ferch Dôn and the Wand (Math fab Mathonwy)

When Math needs a new footholder, Gwydion proposes his sister, Arianrhod daughter of Don. Stepping over the wand to prove her virginity, she drops a yellow-haired boy — Dylan, who makes at once for the sea — and 'something small' that Gwydion hides in a chest: the boy who becomes Lleu. Shamed, Arianrhod lays the three tynghedau on her son — no name until she names him, no arms until she arms him, no wife of any race then on earth — and the rest of the branch is Gwydion undoing each destiny by craft, down to the making of Blodeuwedd from flowers.

The Ploughman and the Smith (Culhwch ac Olwen)

The oldest Arthurian tale names the anoethau, the impossible tasks set by Ysbaddaden Bencawr, and two sons of Don stand inside the list: Amaethon fab Dôn, without whom the giant's ground cannot be tilled, and Gofannon fab Dôn the smith, who must be brought to the work of the headlands. Agriculture and smithcraft, the two oldest arts, belong to the matriarch's sons.

The Futile Battle (Welsh Triads; Kat Godeu)

The triad of the Three Futile Battles of the Island of Britain gives one to the house of Don: Cad Goddeu, the Battle of the Trees, fought on account of a bitch, a roebuck, and a lapwing that Amathaon fab Dôn brought away from Annwn. The Book of Taliesin's poem Kat Godeu remembers the same war from the other side of the family — Gwydion enchanting the forest into an army against Arawn. One son's theft begins the quarrel; another son's enchantment fights it.

Plant Dôn (the genealogical tradition)

The Welsh tradition organized its divine world by the mother's name: Plant Dôn beside Plant Llŷr. The genealogies make Don a daughter of Mathonwy — sister to Math — and later ones supply the husband the tales omit, Beli Mawr. The Mabinogi gives her neither husband nor speech: the only major figure of the Four Branches who never appears, and the only one whose name all the others carry.

Symbols & Iconography

Don has no regalia of her own — the sources give her none. Her symbolic record is carried by her house:

  • The matronymic (uab Dôn) — 'Son of Dôn': the mother-name standing at the head of every child's name, her whole presence in the text
  • Math's wand — Her brother's instrument of testing and transformation, laid across her children's fates
  • Plough and anvil — Amaethon's plough and Gofannon's smithy, the crafts of cultivation and making
  • The Dee (Dyfrdwy) — The river the Welsh still call 'the goddess', the name's closest living relative in the landscape
  • The star-castles — Caer Arianrhod and Caer Gwydion, her daughter's and her son's halls written into the Welsh night sky by later tradition

Archaeology & Evidence

Don is a textual figure: no temple, votive deposit, or inscription names her, and the early inscribed stones of Wales record personal names in funerary formulae, not theonyms. Her evidence is manuscript evidence. The Fourth Branch survives complete in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1375–1420), with earlier fragments in Peniarth 6; Culhwch ac Olwen is preserved in the same two codices; the Book of Taliesin (early fourteenth century) holds Kat Godeu. The tales' date of composition is debated, most often placed in the later eleventh or twelfth century.

The geography of her house is real — Gwynedd and Arfon in the north-west of Wales, where Math's court and Arianrhod's castle stand in the tale — but no sanctuary of hers is known. The closest thing to a material trace of the name is a river: Dyfrdwy, the Welsh name of the Dee, continues Common Celtic *Dēwā, 'the goddess', and keeps the matriarch's name-element flowing through the landscape to this day.

Realm & Domain

Don's domain is Mother of Gods — a domain that is a genealogy, and the genealogy is the whole record.

The Sister-Sons

Gwydion and Gilfaethwy, sons of Don, are Math's sister-sons and the animating pair of the Fourth Branch: Gwydion 'the best teller of tales in the world', the foremost enchanter of Welsh prose, and Gilfaethwy, whose desire for Goewin his brother turns into a war. Every introduction names them through the mother.

The Daughter

Arianrhod ferch Dôn is proposed as Math's footholder, tested by the wand, and revealed as the mother of Dylan Eil Ton and of the boy who becomes Lleu; her three tynghedau against her son drive the branch's second half. She is the Fourth Branch's proudest and most wounded figure.

The Craftsman-Sons

Culhwch ac Olwen adds the older arts: Gofannon fab Dôn the smith and Amaethon fab Dôn the ploughman, each indispensable to Ysbaddaden's impossible tasks — the forge and the furrow lodged in the matriarch's house.

The House Named for the Mother

Plant Dôn, the Children of Dôn, stands beside Plant Llŷr as one of the two great kindreds of the Mabinogi world — a divine house organized around a mother who never appears in it. The parallel with Ireland's Túatha Dé Danann, the peoples of the goddess Danu, is the standard comparison of the handbooks.

Across Cultures

Don's great comparison is Irish, and it is as old as modern Celtic studies: Danu (or Dana), the equally shadowy mother after whom the Túatha Dé Danann — the peoples of the goddess Danu — are named. A Welsh divine kindred called the Children of Dôn and an Irish one called the peoples of Danu look like two faces of one inheritance, and the handbooks register the comparison as standard. But it is a comparison, not an equation: no Welsh text identifies the two names, and the philology is genuinely disputed — dēwā 'goddess' beside a river-name of the Danube–Don type.

Within the Welsh corpus her closest kin are the tradition's other mothers and queens: Rhiannon, the Great Queen of Dyfed, and, in the later genealogical frame, her pairing with Beli Mawr as father of her children — asserted by the genealogies, unmentioned by the Mabinogi. Comparative scholarship sets her beside the Irish sovereignty mothers, Macha above all, but the parallel is structural rather than verbal. Her husbandless, speechless sovereignty has no exact parallel anywhere in Celtic literature: she is the matriarch reduced to pure name.

Cultural Legacy

Don rode into English on the same wagon as the rest of the Mabinogi: Lady Charlotte Guest's translation (1838–49) first carried the Fourth Branch into print, and the antiquarian mythography that followed made Plant Dôn the standard label for the northern divine kindred — the house of Gwydion, set beside the house of Llŷr, in every Victorian account of the Welsh gods. Twentieth-century scholarship fixed her place with precision: Ifor Williams's Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930) established the text, Mac Cana's The Mabinogi read the Fourth Branch as the masterpiece of Welsh prose, and the great encyclopedias — Koch's Celtic Culture and MacKillop's Dictionary — gave the matriarch her standing head-words, Danu comparison and all.

Her widest modern audience came through a children's classic: Lloyd Alexander's Chronicles of Prydain (1964–68) made 'the Sons of Don' the ruling house of Prydain, with Math son of Mathonwy and Prince Gwydion at Caer Dathyl — her name on the throne of the best-loved Welsh-derived fantasy ever written. Modern Druid and pagan practice honours her as the Welsh mother-goddess and mother of the magical line, and the Welsh sky keeps her children's names: Caer Gwydion, the Milky Way, and Caer Arianrhod among the stars.

A Meditation

Don is the matriarch who never speaks. Every other great figure of the Four Branches is given a scene, a speech, a deed — Gwydion his enchantments, Arianrhod her curses, Math his wand — and Don is given nothing but the space before her children's names. Yet the whole architecture of the tale leans on that silence: the northern house of Welsh mythology is not the house of Mathonwy her father, or of Math her brother, but the Children of Dôn, the kindred named for the mother. The sources are telling us something about where authority lived in the world behind the tales — that the line itself could be reckoned through the woman, and the name that organized a pantheon could be hers alone.

Her restoration is like her: three plain letters, hidden in plain sight. Don is an English word, an English river, a Spanish title — and underneath all of them the Welsh mother of gods, carrying a name the tradition glossed as 'goddess' itself. The project rescues diacritics from the address bar; it cannot rescue a scene the manuscripts never wrote. What it can do, and does here, is keep the mother-name at the head of the house, spelled exactly as the White Book and the Red Book spell it.

The Unicode Restoration

Don is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length the manuscript itself records. The Middle Welsh manuscripts write three plain Latin letters; the circumflex of modern Dôn is a modern orthographic device, not a manuscript letter, so the temple follows the manuscripts and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the matriarch; it is an honest report about her name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

The Domain Name

Here the record must be candid. Because Don is pure ASCII — and also an ordinary English word and a river-name — its domain was registered in the ordinary way, long ago: don.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection, and no form of the name is currently owned. The project watches the domain for release — the matriarch's address is, for now, someone else's. This temple claims nothing it does not hold: the restoration lives at its canonical lexicon address, and the watched-domain status is part of the record, not an embarrassment to it. Frequently Asked Questions

What does Don mean? The traditional gloss is "Goddess" — from Common Celtic dēwā, the word Welsh keeps in Dyfrdwy, the River Dee. A river-name origin, matching the Danube–Don family, is also argued; the dispute is genuine and this temple registers both lines.

Which tradition does Don belong to? Don is catalogued in the Celtic pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon — the Welsh branch, the Mabinogi's northern divine house.

Why is Don classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Middle Welsh form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The modern Welsh circumflex is a length-mark, not a manuscript letter. The rule is mechanical; the matriarch's standing is beside the point.

Is don.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release; the temple is served at /sites/don/ in the meantime.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines inherit whatever spelling the web normalizes — and for a plain-ASCII name that is also an English word, normalization is not the risk; obliteration is. Don the matriarch, don the honorific, don the verb, the River Don, don.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps the goddess findable at all. This temple is the lexicon's fixed point for the mother of the Mabinogi's magical house — tier, sources, etymology and all — whether or not the address ever comes home.
