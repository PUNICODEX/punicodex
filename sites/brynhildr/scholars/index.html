01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Brynhildr (brynhildr) ​— Valkyrie, ‌Shieldmaiden · ‌Armor battle ​— is the ​tragic center ​of the ​Vǫlsung cycle: the ​valkyrie whom ‌Óðinn pricked ​with the ​sleep-thorn for judging ‌a battle ​against his ‌promise, the ​shieldmaiden Sigurðr woke ‌behind the ​wall of ​flame on Hindarfjall, ​the sworn ​beloved betrayed ​by a ‌draught of forgetfulness ​and an ‌exchanged shape, ​and the ‌woman who burned ​herself on ‌the dragonslayer's ​pyre with a ‌drawn sword ​between them.[1]

​Old Norse ‌Brynhildr compounds bryn- ‌‘mail-coat, byrnie’ ‌(the noun ​brynja) with ​hildr ‘battle’: ‘armor ‌battle’ — ​the battle-maid ​in her mail. ‌The prose ​frame of ​Sigrdrífumál names ‌her Sigrdrífa, ‘victory-urger’; ​Vǫlsunga saga ‌makes the ​identification with ​Brynhildr explicit, and ​the heroic ​lays tell ‌her whole arc ‌— the ​sentence, the ‌waking, the ​oath, the deceit, ​the quarrel ‌of the ‌queens, the ‌pyre, and the ‌ride to ​Hel.[2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Brynhildr ​— identical ​to the ‌ASCII input, because ​normalized Old ​Norse writes ​no diacritic ‌in this name. ​The mechanical ​rule therefore ​places it in ‌Tier 2: ​nothing distinctive ‌is lost ​in transit, a ‌statement about ​orthography and ‌never about ‌the figure. The ​temple is ​domain-less: the ​plain-ASCII brynhildr.com is ​registered outside ‌the collection ‌and watched ​for release.[3]

  2. Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Jesse L. Byock (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1990).
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Brynhildr, brynja, hildr.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Brynhildr ​(nominative). It is ​a transparent ​compound of two ​of the ​most productive name-elements ‌in Germanic: ​bryn-, the stem ​of brynja ‌‘coat of mail, ​byrnie’, and ‌hildr ‘battle’ — ​the same ‌element carried by ​the valkyrie ​names Hildr, Gunnhildr, ​and Ragnhildr ​— hence ‘armor ​battle’, the ‌battle-maid in her ​mail.[1] The final ‌-r is ​the Old Norse ‌nominative ending.

​The ASCII form ‌is not ​a fallback but ‌the medieval ​spelling itself: normalized ​Old Norse ‌writes Brynhildr in ‌plain Latin ‌letters, with no ​acute accent, ​no thorn, and ‌no eth. ​The restoration is ​therefore a ‌matter of canonical ​reference, not ​rescued diacritics, and ‌the mechanical ​tier rule — ‌distinctive feature ​preserved, or not ​— classifies ​the name Tier ​2.

The ‌letter-by-letter transformation runs:

‌- b ​→ B — ‌Same, capitalized
​- r → ​r — ‌Same

  • y ‌→ y ‌— Same
  • ‌n → ​n — Same

‌- h ​→ h — ​Same

  • ​i → i ‌— Same
  • ​l → ​l — Same

​- d ‌→ d — ​Same

  • ​r → r ​— Same

‌Because the name ​is pure ‌ASCII, its domain ​was registered ‌in the ordinary ​way decades ‌ago: brynhildr.com is ‌held by ​a third party ​outside the ​PuniCodex collection and ​is watched ‌for release; this ‌temple claims ​no domain.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈbryn.hil.dr̩/ — ​Old Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Bryn- ​— Voiced bilabial ​stop [b], ‌trilled [r], and ​the close ​front rounded vowel ‌[y] — the ​sound of ‌German ‘ü’ or ​French ‘tu’; ‌the ‘mail-coat’ element, ​stressed on ​the first syllable ​as in all ​Old Norse ​compounds

  • -hildr ‌— Voiceless ​glottal [h], short ‌[i], lateral ​[l], voiced stop ‌[d], and the ​syllabic nominative ‌[r̩]; the ‘battle’ ​element

For ‌the modern speaker, ​the closest ​approximation is: ‘BRÜN-hild-r’ ‌— round the ‌lips for ‌the y as ​in German ​‘für’, and let ‌the final ​r sound lightly ​as its own ‌beat, as ​in Icelandic Brynhildur.

​Kindred and ‌historical forms of ​the name:

‌- Old Norse ​— Brynhildr, ​the valkyrie of ​the Vǫlsung cycle; ​also Sigrdrífa, ‌‘victory-urger’, the name ‌Sigrdrífumál itself ​gives her[2]

  • ‌Old High ​German — Brünhilt, ​the queen of ‌the Nibelungenlied ‌and Þiðreks saga[3]

‌- Historical ‌— Brunhilda, the ​Merovingian queen ‌of Austrasia (d. ​613), long linked ​by scholars ​to the legendary ‌name[4]

  • ​Modern Icelandic — ​Brynhildur, the ​name's unbroken living ‌form

Brynhildr is ​Tier 2 ​because the restoration ​preserves nothing ‌the ASCII form ​loses: nine ‌plain Latin letters ​and a capital. ‌The tier ​is a report ‌on orthography, ‌not a judgment ​on the ​figure — the ​most famous valkyrie ​in the ‌North happens to ‌carry an ​ASCII-clean name.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved ‌in medieval ​Icelandic manuscripts ​in ​the Latin ​alphabet ​as Brynhildr ‌— Old ​Norse ​(North Germanic), ‌attested ​13th c. ‌CE ​in the ‌Codex Regius ​of ​the Poetic ​Edda ​(GKS 2365 ​4to) ‌and the ​vellum tradition ‌of ​Vǫlsunga saga, ‌recording ​material centuries ‌older.[1] Before ​the ‌Latin bookhand, ​Old ​Norse names ‌were ‌carved in ‌the younger ​futhark; ​no runic ‌attestation ​of the ​legendary ‌Brynhildr survives, ​so the ​manuscript ‌form is ​the ‌primary witness.

​A hypothetical ​younger-futhark ​rendering runs ​ᛒᚱᚢᚾᚼᛁᛚᛏᚱ ‌(b-r-u-n-h-i-l-t-R): the ‌ᚢ ​rune covers ‌u and ​y ​alike, and ‌the ‌ᛏ rune ‌writes ‌both t ​and d. ‌The ​normalized Old ​Norse ​form Brynhildr ‌carries no ​feature ​the runes ​would ‌have underspecified ​and ​nothing the ​ASCII alphabet ‌loses ​— no ‌acute, ​no thorn, ‌no ​eth — ‌which is ‌why ​the restoration ​is ​already ASCII-clean ​and the ‌name ‌is Tier ​2.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Brynhildr.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Brynhildr ​is catalogued ‌under the ‌domain Valkyrie, ​Shieldmaiden — ​and the ​sources earn ​both terms.

​### The ‌Valkyrie

Sigrdrífumál's ​prose ​names her ‌among the ​valkyries — ‌Óðinn's choosers ​of the ‌slain — ​and gives ​her the ​valkyrie name ​Sigrdrífa, ​‘victory-urger’; her ‌fall from ​Óðinn's service ‌for an ​independent judgment ‌is the ​myth's first ‌cause.[1]

​The ‌Shieldmaiden

Sigurðr ​finds her ​in full ‌war-gear, the ‌byrnie grown ‌fast to ​her flesh ​— armor ‌and woman ​so ​long one ‌that they ​must be ​cut apart ‌with Gramr. ​The image ‌fixes her ​as the ​corpus's type ​of ​the martial ‌woman.[2]

‌The Keeper ​of the ‌Oath

Her ​sworn word ​— to ‌wed no ‌man who ‌knew ‌fear — ​outlives sleep, ‌deceit, and ​death itself; ​the cycle ​measures every ‌other character's ​oath-breaking against ​her ​fidelity.

‌The Dead ​Who Ride

​Helreið Brynhildar ​follows her ‌past the ​pyre onto ‌the road ​to Hel, ‌where ​she answers ‌a giantess's ‌chiding with ​her whole ​life's account ​— the ​dead heroine ‌still pleading ‌her own ​case.[3]

Sources

  1. Sigrdrífumál (Poetic Edda), prose frame, trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Byock (1990), the waking on Hindarfjall.
  3. Helreið Brynhildar (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Brynhildr is ‌narrative ​rather than ​cultic — ​the ​corpus gives ​her no ‌temple ​regalia, but ​the cycle ‌fixed ​around her ‌a set ​of ‌objects no ​retelling can ​omit:[1]

​- The ​sleep-thorn (svefnþorn) ​— ‌Óðinn's instrument ​of ‌sentence: sleep ​as the ‌price ​of an ‌independent verdict
​- ‌The vafrlogi ​— the ​flickering ‌wall of ‌flame around ‌her ​hall on ​Hindarfjall, crossable ‌only ​by Grani ​and only ‌by ​the fearless
​- The ‌byrnie ​grown fast ‌to her ​flesh ​— war-gear ​become skin, ​cut ‌away with ‌Gramr: the ​valkyrie ‌woken out ​of her ​own ‌armor

  • ‌The drawn ‌sword ‌between the ​lovers — ‌Gramr ​laid naked ​in the ​bed ‌of the ​deception, ​and laid ​between them ‌again ​on the ​pyre
  • ​The ‌ring Andvaranautr ​— the ‌ring ​from the ‌hoard of ​Andvari, ‌taken in ‌the exchanged ​shapes ​and produced ​at the ​quarrel ‌as the ‌proof of ​deceit[2]

Sources

  1. Sigrdrífumál, Sigurðarkviða hin skamma (Poetic Edda); Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Larrington (2014) and Byock (1990).
  2. Guðrúnarkviða II (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014), the quarrel and the ring.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Brynhildr's mythology is ​a single arc — sentenced, woken, ‌sworn, deceived, avenged, and burned ‌— told across the heroic ​lays and Vǫlsunga saga with a ​consistency the corpus rarely achieves.[1]

​### The Valkyrie's Sentence (Sigrdrífumál)

​Two kings fought, Hjálmgunnarr the old ​and Agnar the young, and ‌Óðinn had promised the victory ​to Hjálmgunnarr. The valkyrie Sigrdrífa judged ​otherwise and gave the day ‌to Agnar. Óðinn pricked her with ​the sleep-thorn in revenge and ‌declared she should never again ​fight in victory and should be ‌given in marriage — and ​she answered that she had ​bound herself with an oath to ​wed no man who knew ​fear. The whole tragedy grows ​from this: an independent verdict, punished ‌with sleep.[2]

The Waking ​on Hindarfjall (Vǫlsunga saga)

Sigurðr, fresh ‌from the hoard of Fáfnir, ​saw a light burning on ‌Hindarfjall and rode Grani through the ​vafrlogi. Within the wall stood ‌a hall, and in the ​hall a figure in full armor; ‌he cut the byrnie from ​her with Gramr, from the ​neck down, for it had grown ‌fast to her flesh, and ‌she woke. She taught him the ‌runes of victory and the ​counsels of Sigrdrífumál, and they ​plighted their troth.[3]

The Ale ‌of Forgetfulness (Guðrúnarkviða II · ​Vǫlsunga saga)

At the court ​of Gjúki, Grímhildr gave Sigurðr a ‌draught of forgetfulness, and the ​memory of Brynhildr went out ​of him; he married Guðrún and ‌swore brotherhood with Gunnarr and ​Hǫgni. When Gunnarr desired Brynhildr and ‌his horse would not take ​the flame, the two men ​exchanged shapes: Sigurðr rode the vafrlogi ​in Gunnarr's form, won the ​bride for his sworn brother, ‌and lay three nights beside her ‌with the drawn sword between ​them — taking from her ‌hand the ring Andvaranautr.[4]

The ​Quarrel of the Queens (Guðrúnarkviða ​II)

At the river, washing their ‌hair, Brynhildr waded the farther ‌out for the sake of ‌her husband's deed; Guðrún answered with ‌the truth and held up ​Andvaranautr as proof. Brynhildr knew ‌the ring, turned white as a ​dead woman, and went home ​silent. The revelation — woman ​to woman, by a token of ‌gold — brings the whole ​world of the cycle down.[4]

​The Pyre and the Road ​to Hel (Sigurðarkviða hin skamma ‌· Helreið Brynhildar)

Brynhildr turned Gunnarr ​to vengeance, and Sigurðr was ​slain in his bed by ​young Guthormr, the one brother bound ‌by no oath. When Guðrún's ​wail rose, Brynhildr laughed once, ‌from the bottom of her heart; ​then she stabbed herself and ‌asked to be burned beside Sigurðr ​with a drawn sword between ‌them, as when they had ‌been called by the name of ​man and wife. Helreið Brynhildar ​follows her past the fire: ​a giantess by her mound chides ​her, and Brynhildr answers with ‌her whole life — the ‌sleep, the waking, the oath kept, ​the deceit endured.[5]

Sources

  1. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014): the heroic lays of the Sigurðr cycle.
  2. Sigrdrífumál (Poetic Edda), prose frame, trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Byock (1990), the waking and the troth-plighting.
  4. Guðrúnarkviða II (Poetic Edda); Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Larrington (2014) and Byock (1990).
  5. Sigurðarkviða hin skamma and Helreið Brynhildar (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Brynhildr ​lives two ‌lives in ‌the medieval ​record. The ​Norse tradition ​makes her ​a valkyrie: ​sentenced by ‌Óðinn, woken ​by Sigurðr, ​self-immolated on ‌his pyre. ​The continental ‌tradition makes ​her human: ‌the Nibelungenlied's ​Brünhilt is ​the proud ​queen of ​Iceland whose ​strength no ‌suitor could ​master until ‌Siegfried, invisible ​in his ‌cloak of ​power, won ‌her for ​Gunther ‌— and ​whose quarrel ​with Kriemhilt ‌at the ‌minster door ‌replays the ​quarrel at ​the river. ‌Þiðreks saga ​stands between ​the two, ‌keeping a ​martial Brunhild ​in the ‌far north.[1]

​Since the ‌Grimms, scholars ​have linked ​the legendary ​name to ​the historical ‌Brunhilda of ‌Austrasia, the ​Merovingian queen ‌executed in ​613 — ​a connection ‌plausible for ‌the continental ‌strand ‌and unnecessary ​for the ‌Norse.[2] The ​Grimm brothers ​also heard ​the svefnþorn ‌echoing in ​the thorn-hedged ​sleep of ​Dornröschen: the ‌fairy tale ​keeping the ​valkyrie's sentence ​alive in ‌the nursery. ​Within the ‌corpus, her ​closest kin ‌are the ​valkyries Gunnr ‌and Skuld, ‌and the ​Freyja-figure of ​Freyja, who ​shares with ​the valkyries ‌the choosing ‌of the ​slain.[3]

Sources

  1. Das Nibelungenlied, trans. Hatto (1965); Þiðreks saga af Bern.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (1993), s.v. Brunhild.
  3. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. valkyries.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Brynhildr's ​modern afterlife ‌is Wagner's: ‌the Ring ​cycle ​made Brünnhilde ​the most ​famous valkyrie ​in the ‌world, ​and her ​immolation — ‌riding Grane ​into ‌Siegfried's pyre ​with the ‌ring returned ​to the ​Rhine ​— is ​opera's great ​closing image ‌of ​a world ‌redeemed by ​a woman's ‌refusal ​to accept ‌its lies. ​Wagner built ‌her from ​the ​Norse sources, ‌not the ‌German: Wotan's ‌daughter ​and Siegfried's ​doom is ‌the Edda's ​figure, not ​the ‌Nibelungenlied's queen.[1]

​Behind Wagner ​stand the ‌Victorian ​rediscoveries: William ‌Morris's The ​Story of ​Sigurd ​the Volsung ​(1876) gave ‌English readers ‌the whole ​arc, ‌sleep-thorn to ​pyre, and ​the archetype ‌she ‌fixed — ‌the warrior ‌woman asleep ​behind fire, ‌won ​only by ​the fearless ​— runs ‌through ​the fairy ​tale of ​Sleeping Beauty ‌and ​every later ​shieldmaiden of ​fiction. In ‌Iceland the ​name ‌never left: ​Brynhildur remains ‌a living ​given ‌name, and ‌Old Norse ​Brynhildr stays ​the standard ​citation ​form in ‌every handbook ‌of heroic ​legend.[2]

Sources

  1. Wagner, Richard, Der Ring des Nibelungen (1853–74), libretti; on the Norse sources, see Lindow, Norse Mythology: A Guide (2001), s.v. Brunhild.
  2. Morris, William, The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs (London: Ellis and White, 1876).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Brynhildr herself ​has no ‌cult site, ‌votive deposit, ​or named ​image — ​she is a ​figure of ​heroic legend, ‌not of ​cult. The ​material record ‌attests the ​legend around her. ‌The Sigurðr ​carvings of ‌Södermanland — ​the Ramsund ​carving (Sö ​101) and the ​Gök stone ​(Sö 327), ‌both eleventh ​century — ‌depict the ​slaying of Fáfnir, ‌the roasting ​heart, the ‌talking birds, ​and the ‌slain Reginn, ​proving the Vǫlsung ​cycle's visual ‌currency in ‌the late ‌Viking Age.[1]

​The carved ​portal of ‌Hylestad stave church ​in Norway ​(c. 1200) ‌retells the ​saga's scenes ​in wood, ‌and the Kirk ​Andreas cross ‌on the ​Isle of ​Man shows ​Sigurðr roasting ​the dragon's heart. ‌The Drävle ‌runestone (U ​1163) shows ‌a woman ​offering a ​drinking horn ‌to a rider, ‌sometimes interpreted ‌as Sigrdrífa ‌or Brynhildr, ​though the ‌identification is ​debated.[1] The textual ​witnesses hang ​on thin ‌threads: the ​heroic lays ​survive in ​the Codex Regius ‌of the ​Poetic Edda ​(GKS 2365 ​4to, c. ‌1270) — ​with the famous ‌lacuna of ​a lost ‌gathering that ​Brot af ‌Sigurðarkviðu breaks ‌across — ​and Vǫlsunga saga ​is preserved ​in a ​vellum fragment ‌(NGU 182 ‌4to) and ​paper copies.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigurd, Brunhild.
  2. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014), introduction (the Codex Regius and the lacuna); Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Byock (1990), introduction (the manuscript tradition).

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda is Brynhildr's ‌principal witness, and ‌her arc can ​be traced lay ​by lay. Sigrdrífumál gives ​the beginning: the ​prose frame names ​her Sigrdrífa, tells ‌the sleep-thorn sentence, ​and preserves her ​wisdom — the ‌runes of victory ​and the counsels ‌she gives the ​waking hero.[1] Grípisspá foretells ‌the whole tragedy ​in advance: Sigurðr's ​uncle Grípir prophesies ​the dragon, the ​waking, the deceit, ​and the death, ‌so that the ​listener walks into ‌the story knowing every ​door that will ‌close.[1]

The middle ​of the story ‌survives only in ​pieces. The Codex ‌Regius lost a ​whole gathering — ​the famous lacuna ‌— and Brot ‌af Sigurðarkviðu, ‘the fragment ‌of a lay ​of Sigurðr’, begins ​mid-poem at the ‌plotting of the ​murder; what stood ​on the lost ‌leaves is the ​single greatest gap ​in the manuscript. ‌Guðrúnarkviða I and II ​give the aftermath ‌and the revelation: ​Guðrún's grief, the ​quarrel at the ​river, the ring ​produced as proof.[1]

‌The end is ‌told twice over. ​Sigurðarkviða hin skamma, ‌‘the short lay of ​Sigurðr’, is her ​great lay: the ‌incitement, the murder ‌in the bed, ‌the single laugh ‌at Guðrún's wail, ​the self-wounding, and ‌the deathbed commands ​— to be ​burned beside Sigurðr with ​a drawn sword ‌between them, as ​when they were ​called man and ​wife, with the ‌prophesied woes of ​the Gjúkungs spoken ​from the pyre's ​edge.[2] Helreið Brynhildar, ‘Brynhildr's ‌ride to Hel’, ​is unique in ‌the corpus: a ​dead heroine pleading ‌her own case ​against a chiding ‌giantess, recounting the ‌sleep, the waking, ​and the deceit, ​and closing with the ​hope of reunion ​— the cycle's ‌only voice granted ‌to Brynhildr beyond ​the fire.[3]

Sources

  1. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014): Sigrdrífumál, Grípisspá, Brot af Sigurðarkviðu, Guðrúnarkviða I–II.
  2. Sigurðarkviða hin skamma (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Helreið Brynhildar (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌knows ‌Brynhildr inside ​Snorri's retelling ​of ​the Sigurðr ​legend ​in Skáldskaparmál. ‌Snorri compresses ​the ​cycle into ‌a ​genealogy-and-narrative account ‌of the ​Vǫlsungs: ‌the reforging ​of ​Gramr, the ​slaying of ​Fáfnir, ​the waking ‌of ​Brynhildr, the ‌troth-plighting, ​the forgetfulness, ‌the bride-winning ​for ‌Gunnarr, the ​quarrel, ‌and the ​deaths — ​offered ‌as the ‌background ‌to the ​kennings that ​call ‌gold ‘Fáfnir's ​lair’ ​or ‘the ‌metal of ​Gnitaheiðr’.[1]

​Snorri's treatment ‌is ​significant less ‌for ​new detail ​than for ​what ​it proves: ‌that ‌the whole ​Brynhildr arc ‌was ​working knowledge ​for ‌an educated ‌Icelander of ‌the ‌1220s, told ​as ‌fact in ​a handbook ​of ​poetics. Skáldskaparmál's ‌þulur ​also keep ​the ​valkyrie-lists in ‌which her ​name-element ​world lives ​— ‌the catalogue ​of battle-maids ‌where ​Gunnr, Skuld, ‌and ​their sisters ‌are named ‌— ​the formal ​machinery ​of the ​valkyrie concept ‌Brynhildr ‌embodies in ​narrative.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the Sigurðr narrative), trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur of the valkyries, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​runestone invokes ‌Brynhildr: she ‌is a ​figure of ​heroic legend, ​not of ​cult, and ​the runic ‌corpus knows ​her only ​at one ‌remove. What ​the stones ‌attest is ​the legend ‌around her. ​The eleventh-century ​Sigurðr carvings ​of Södermanland ​— the ​Ramsund carving ‌(Sö 101) ​and the ‌Gök stone ​(Sö 327) ‌— depict ​the slaying ‌of the ​dragon, the ‌roasting heart, ​the talking ​birds, and ‌the slain ‌Reginn, carved ‌for Christian ​families as ​memorial art: ‌the Vǫlsung ​cycle was ​living public ‌story in ​the decades ​around 1030.[1]

‌The Drävle ​runestone (U ‌1163) is ​the corpus's ​closest approach ​to Brynhildr ​herself: it ‌shows a ‌woman offering ​a drinking ‌horn to ​a rider, ​a scene ‌sometimes interpreted ‌as Sigrdrífa ‌or Brynhildr, ‌though the ​identification is ‌debated and ​the inscription ​names human ​dead, not ‌heroines.[1] The ​name's raw ​material is ​thoroughly at ‌home in ​the inscriptions: ​hildr is ​one of ‌the commonest ​deuterothemes in ‌runic anthroponymy, ​and brynja ‌‘mail-coat’ a ​plain common ‌noun — ‌a compound ​of everyday ​heroic vocabulary ​elevated into ​the most ‌famous valkyrie ‌name in ​the North.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigurd, Brunhild.
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. brynja, hildr.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Vǫlsunga saga ​is Brynhildr's ‌fullest medieval home: ‌a legendary ​saga of c. ​1260–70 that ​works the Eddic ​lays into ​continuous prose and ‌supplies what ​the lacuna destroyed. ​The saga ‌makes the identification ​the poems ‌leave implicit — ​Sigrdrífa is ‌Brynhildr — and ​tells the ​whole arc: the ​sleep-thorn, the ​waking in the ​byrnie grown ‌fast to her ​flesh, the wisdom ‌and the ​troth, the draught ‌of forgetfulness, ​the exchanged shapes ‌and the ​three nights with ‌the drawn ​sword, the quarrel ​at the ‌river, the murder, ‌the laugh, ‌and the pyre. ​Where the ​Poetic Edda's middle ‌is missing, ​Vǫlsunga saga is ​the bridge ‌— and it ​tells us ​that medieval readers ‌crossed the ​same gap the ‌same way.[1]

​The continental strand ​belongs to ​the sagas' wider ​world. Þiðreks ‌saga af Bern, ‌the Norwegian ​compilation of German ‌heroic legend, ​keeps a martial ​Brunhild in ‌the far north, ‌mastered by ‌Sigurd's trickery for ‌Gunnar — ​the bridge-figure between ‌the valkyrie ​of the Edda ​and the ​queen of the ‌Nibelungenlied. The Nibelungenlied ​itself (c. ​1200) makes Brünhilt ​fully human: ‌the proud queen ​of Iceland, ​won by an ​invisible Siegfried ‌for Gunther, and ​undeceived at ‌last by the ​quarrel of ‌the queens at ​the minster ‌door. The two ‌traditions never ​merge in the ​Middle Ages; ​Wagner merged them ​in the ‌nineteenth century, and ‌chose the ​Edda's version.[2]

Sources

  1. Vǫlsunga saga, trans. Byock (1990).
  2. Þiðreks saga af Bern; Das Nibelungenlied, trans. Hatto (1965).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Brynhildr ​is the ‌tradition's figure ‌of the ​oath that ​outlives ​its world. ​Everything taken ​from her ‌is ​taken by ​small magic ‌— a ​thorn, a ‌draught, ​a borrowed ‌shape, a ​ring — ​and none ​of ​it touches ​the thing ‌she actually ​is: ‌the woman ​who judged ‌rightly against ​a god, ‌who ​asked only ‌for a ​man without ​fear, ‌and who ‌kept her ‌account with ​the truth ​to ‌the last ​breath.[1]

Her ​one laugh, ‌heard as ​the ​world breaks, ‌is among ​the most ‌unsettling ​sounds in ​the Edda; ​her pyre, ​with the ‌drawn ‌sword between ​the dead, ‌is its ​clearest statement ​that ‌a marriage ‌made by ‌deceit could ‌be ​annulled only ‌by fire. ​The cycle ​does not ​ask ‌us to ​forgive her ​vengeance. It ​asks ‌whether any ​of the ​men around ​her — ‌the ​forgetful hero, ‌the oath-bound ​brothers, the ‌scheming queen ​— ‌ever matched ‌the standard ​she set: ​to ​know what ​happened, and ‌to refuse ‌to pretend ​otherwise.

Sources

  1. Sigurðarkviða hin skamma (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
