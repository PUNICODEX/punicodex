Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

Hokkaidō's sanctuaries belong to two ritual worlds. The older is Ainu: a religion of exchange with the kamuy — the spirit-owners of bear, fish, fire, and tools — maintained in the kamuy-nomi household prayer, with its carved wine-sticks and shaved inau wands, and crowned by the iomante, the 'sending-off' of the bear, in which the mountain god's visiting body was feasted, praised, and returned to Kamuy Mosir so that the gift would come again. The ceremony's last full performances belong to the early twentieth century; suppressed, misunderstood, and starved of practitioners, it survives now as studied revival and public demonstration rather than living rite. The younger sanctuaries are Meiji: Hokkaidō Jingū in Sapporo, founded in 1871 around the 'three deities of pioneering,' placed the frontier under State Shinto's wing — a theological colonization corps marching with the survey teams. Buddhist temples followed the fishing ports; Orthodox crosses dotted the Kurils. The island's ritual history is a palimpsest: Ainu exchange beneath Japanese enshrinement, each claiming the same mountains, and only now beginning to be read together.

In the Earth

Hokkaidō holds some of the world's oldest pottery: Jōmon-period cord-marked vessels from sites like Taishō date beyond 14,000 years, among the earliest ceramic traditions on earth. The Shiretoko peninsula is a UNESCO World Heritage site for its marine-terrestrial food web; the Oshoro stone circles and the Ofuna shell middens mark the island's deep ritual past. Hakodate's Goryōkaku — the star fort of the last Tokugawa loyalists (1869) — is where the old Japan made its final stand on the new island.

05

Iconography

How Hokkaidō was imaged across the ages

The Image

An island has no canonical image, but Hokkaidō has been remade by every map drawn of it. Edo-period cartographers left Ezochi half-blank at the edge of the archipelago — the blankness itself an image, a land beyond the knowable. The Meiji state fixed its picture with instruments: Matsuura Takeshirō's surveys of 1858–69 walked the coasts and named the features, and the resulting maps rendered the island legible, griddable, colonizable — Sapporo's planned streets are the map stepped into masonry. The visual record of the Ainu was meanwhile fixed by outsiders' lenses: nineteenth-century expedition photographs and the ethnographic albums of John Batchelor posed a 'vanishing people' for imperial science, images that now read as evidence of what the mapping cost. The Ainu themselves left the island's most haunting art in small carved things — the ikupasuy libation sticks with their bird and bear heads, robe-patterns of spirals and thorns, the white-shaved inau — a sacred iconography of exchange, portable and human-scaled, outlasting every atlas.

06

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

The island entered world knowledge through visitors' books. La Pérouse's expedition charted Hakodate in 1787 and carried the word Ezo to Europe; von Siebold's natural-history volumes dressed its coasts in Latin binomials; and Isabella Bird's Unbeaten Tracks in Japan (1880) gave anglophone readers their first sustained portrait of the Ainu, tender and condescending in equal measure. The Meiji renaming closed the Edo frame: with Ezochi retired for Hokkaidō in 1869, the island joined the modern map as Japan's 'north sea road,' and its image — frontier, snow country, breadbasket — was broadcast worldwide with the twentieth century's photographs. Scholarship followed conquest: Batchelor's grammar and folklore (1901) preserved language and story even as his missionary eye framed it; Kindaichi Kyōsuke's recording of the Kutune Shirka in the 1920s saved the great yukar from the death of its reciters. Postwar research rebuilt the record — the Jōmon deep past excavated, the Ainu economy documented — and the Ainu movement, from Kayano Shigeru's memoir onward, turned the old images against themselves: the 'vanishing people' of the imperial albums re-emerged as the island's first authors. The reception's axis has shifted from looking at Hokkaidō to hearing Ainu Mosir speak.

Identifications Across Traditions

Ainu religion absorbed and resisted Japanese and Russian pressure in equal measure: Orthodox missions among the Kuril Ainu, Buddhist temples on the coasts, and the yukar epics — sung in an isolate language with no close relatives — keeping the older world intact in verse. The twentieth century added a third strand: the Ainu cultural revival, led by figures like the poet and legislator Kayano Shigeru, made the island's first culture a national conversation.

Modern Legacy

Hokkaidō supplies a third of Japan's food from a tenth of its land; Sapporo beer, the Snow Festival's ice palaces, and the lavender of Furano are national brands. The 2020 opening of Upopoy, the National Ainu Museum and Park at Shiraoi, gave the island's first people their first national institution. In the history of colonialism, the Kaitakushi is studied worldwide as Japan's own frontier — the laboratory where it practiced the modernization it later exported.

The Meditation

What Hokkaidō means — beyond the dictionary

Hokkaidō is the island of two names — the one it was given and the one it had. It asks the frontier's hardest question, the one every settled map must answer: whose land was this before it was yours — and can the new name learn to carry the old one's meaning?

The island's two names have begun, at last, to speak to each other: the Ainu language taught in Upopoy's halls, the iomante explained rather than exoticized, the snow country marketed by its indigenous name's dignity. Frontiers are usually where names die; Hokkaidō is attempting the rarer thing — a map where the new name carries the old one's meaning. It is the experiment every settler society eventually must run, and the north island is running it in public.

And the snow keeps its own counsel over both names. Drift ice feeds the sea, the sea feeds the town, and the bear sleeps through the argument of maps — the kamuy-cosmos and the prefecture sharing one winter. The island's patience is its answer: the land outlasts every name it is given, and quietly teaches each new name what it means.

The drift ice still arrives each winter on schedule older than any name, feeding the bloom that feeds the sea — the island's oldest economy, unbranded. The Ainu ceremony sent the bear-spirit home with gifts so it would return; the modern island sends its food south with the same hope. Frontier or homeland, the north keeps its own account: what the land gives, the land can stop giving — and every name we call it is a promise to keep listening.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Hokkaidō, Northern Island, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Hokkaidō?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Hokkaidō is /hok.kai.doː/ — approximately hok-KAI-doh, with the long final ō.

02What does Hokkaidō mean?

Hokkaidō means Northern sea circuit in the japanese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Hokkaidō?

Hokkaidō is associated with The bear (Kim-un-kamuy, the mountain god of the Ainu — honoured in the iomante ceremony), The drift ice (The winter sea that feeds the whole food chain of the north), The lavender field (Furano's summer signature — the cultivated frontier in bloom).

04Why restore Hokkaidō in Unicode?

Plain ASCII hokkaido strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Hokkaidō?

“Ainu Mosir and Kamuy Mosir” — the The Land story that anchors the mythology of Hokkaidō, told in full on the lore page.
