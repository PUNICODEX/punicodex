Mythology

Tefnut’s mythology is concentrated at the ​world’s beginning and in the great Eye-of-Ra cycle. As one of the first two beings she needs few adventures — but the one she has, the flight to ​Nubia and the embassy that retrieves her, became one of the most told and most illustrated stories of the New Kingdom.

The Spat-Forth Pair (Heliopolitan Cosmogony)

In the Memphite ‌and Heliopolitan accounts the creator — Atum or Ra — produced his first two children by spitting (tf) and sneezing: Tefnut the moisture and Shu the air. Their ​names are the verbs of that first physiology; the world’s atmosphere is the afterbirth of the god’s own body.

The Flight to Nubia (Eye of Ra Cycle)

When ‌the creator grew old, men plotted against him — and his Eye, sent to punish them, abandoned her post in fury and fled south as a raging lioness. ​Egypt withered without her. Shu or Thoth was sent to coax her home with the Destruction of Mankind tale’s rhetoric of flattery; her return, pacified, restores the inundation and ‌the sun’s order.

The Return of the Goddess (Leiden Papyrus)

The literary composition ‘The Return of the Eye’ gives Tefnut’s wanderings their fullest poetic form — the goddess ​who leaves, rages, is sung back, and returns transformed from destroyer to blessing. It is the mythic template behind every Egyptian pacification ritual of dangerous goddesses.

Mother of ‌Nut and Geb (Genealogical Theology)

With Shu she bore Nut the sky and Geb the earth — the second divine generation. As grandmother of Osiris, Isis, Seth and ​Nephthys she stands at the head of every royal pedigree; the titulary of kings names her among the oldest company of the gods.

Across Cultures

Tefnut merges almost completely with her sister-forms of the solar Eye ​— Sekhmet, Bastet, Hathor, Mut — each a local face of the one dangerous goddess. Greeks knew her chiefly through Plutarch as one of the primal elements, moisture to ​Shu’s air; modern study treats her and Shu as the key to Egypt’s ‘physical’ cosmogony, where the first creatures are states of the atmosphere.

Within the Egyptian tradition, ‌closely related names in the corpus include Ꜣb, Ꜣḫ, Ꜣmun, ꜥnḫ, ꜣnpw, and ꜥꜣpp.

The spit-and-sneeze cosmogony remains a favourite exhibit of Egyptian theology’s concreteness — ​the first act of creation as a bodily reflex.
