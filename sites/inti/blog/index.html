Inti, Child of the Creator (Theogony)

In the theology the Inca taught at Cuzco, Inti was not the first power. The ​creator Viracocha — the incomprehensible god behind the world, as the chroniclers struggled to render him — made the sun, the moon, and the stars and set them in heaven as lights for ‌humankind. Inti and his sister-wife Mama Quilla were thus the children of the highest god, commissioned to govern the sky in their father's place (Sarmiento de Gamboa, Historia Indica 7; Betanzos, Suma ​y Narración 2). The genealogy is political architecture: by making Inti a son rather than a source, the Inca placed their own emperor — Inti's son — only two steps below creation itself. ‌When the Sapa Inca stood before the people as the unique son of the Sun, the title carried a whole cosmology in four words. The myth means that empire, in the Andes, was ​a family business conducted in the language of the sky.

The Golden Staff of Manco Cápac (Foundation)

The founding myth of Cuzco is a solar errand. Inti and Mama Quilla, grieving that ‌humankind lived without law, cloth, or maize, sent their children — a son, Manco Cápac, and a daughter, Mama Ocllo — from the Island of the Sun in Lake Titicaca, bearing a golden ​staff. Wherever the staff sank whole into the earth, there they were to raise the city. At the hill called Huanacauri, above the valley of Cuzco, the staff went down, and the siblings ‌taught the gathered people to plough, to weave, and to live under rule (Sarmiento, Historia Indica 11–12; Betanzos 4; Murúa, Historia general del Piru). The myth makes the Inca capital literally planted in ​the ground by the sun's own metal: Cuzco was not built so much as sown. Every furrow of its terraces repeated the lesson — agriculture, the technology that let the mountains feed ‌an empire, arrived as a gift from Inti's household, and the ruler in Cuzco ruled as the sun's eldest child.

The Vision at Susurpuquio (Empire)

Before he was Pachacuti — 'He Who Shakes ​the Earth' — the prince Yupanqui was an unlikely heir, and the Chanka confederation stood ready to erase Cuzco. In the account Sarmiento de Gamboa drew from Inca informants (Historia Indica 35–38), the ​young man took refuge at the spring of Susurpuquio, and there the Creator Viracocha came to him in a dream, and Inti promised that the sun himself would fight beside him. The ‌Chanka attack failed; Yupanqui became the ninth Sapa Inca; and he answered the vision on an imperial scale. He rebuilt Cuzco as the navel of the world, re-founded the Coricancha — the Golden ‌Enclosure — as his father Inti's house, and lined its courts with chambers of gold. The myth's logic is reciprocal and exact: a god gave a man a victory at a spring, and ‌the man gave the god a temple of gold. Conquest and devotion were the same transaction, and the empire expanded, as its own theology said, because the sun willed it.

A Meditation

Most sun gods are clocks or lamps. Inti is something rarer: a sun who is also a father, a title, and ‌a border. To be his child was to hold an office; to hold the office was to be luminous by definition; and the whole astonishing apparatus of Inca civilization — the terraces, the ‌storehouses, the road that runs along the equator — can be read as a household administered by the sun's eldest son. The theology solved the oldest problem of government: legitimacy. If the ‌emperor is the sun's son, rebellion is not politics but eclipse, a darkness no subject can afford. Yet there is tenderness in it too: the Inca greeted his father each morning, feasted him ​at each solstice, and carried his image into battle as a banner of gold. The name restored here is the shortest genealogy in the Americas: Inti — the sun who adopted a nation.
