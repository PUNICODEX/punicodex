01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Sigyn (sigyn) — Loyalty, Wife of Loki · Victorious girl-friend — is the wife of Loki and the mother of his human sons, the goddess who stands beneath the earth holding a basin over her bound husband's face, catching the venom of the snake Skaði fastened above him — and the figure whose single act of staying gave the medieval North its explanation for earthquakes: when she turns away to empty the full basin, the venom falls, Loki writhes, and the world shakes.[1]

Her attestation is thin and remarkably old. Snorri names her in his catalogue of the Æsir with a wife's economy; the skald Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, composing around 900, already used the kenning 'the burden of Sigyn's arms' for Loki, proving the marriage was proverbial a century before Iceland's conversion; and Völuspá, the oldest poem of the Codex Regius, shows her seated beside the bound captive 'by no means happy about her husband'.[2]

PuniCodex restores the name as Sigyn — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places the name in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. The temple is domain-less: the plain-ASCII sigyn.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.[3]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name is attested in Old Norse as Sigyn, and it belongs to the goddess alone — the medieval record knows no human bearer. Its etymology is probable rather than proven: the standard analysis takes it as a compound of sigr, 'victory', and vina, 'girl-friend, female friend', yielding 'victorious girl-friend' — a name built on the same friendly-woman element as the masculine vinr compounds of the personal-name stock.[1] Cleasby–Vigfusson record the name under Loki's wife; Simek and Orchard give the same derivation with the usual scholarly reserve, and none of the lexica proposes a serious rival.[2]

The ASCII form is not a fallback here but the form itself: normalized Old Norse writes five plain Latin letters and a capital. The Unicode restoration Sigyn therefore preserves nothing the ASCII alphabet loses, and the mechanical rule classifies the name Tier 2 — an orthographic fact, not a judgment on the goddess. The y marks a rounded vowel in sound, not a mark on the page.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • s → S — Same, capitalized
  • i → i — Same
  • g → g — Same
  • y → y — Same
  • n → n — Same

The project holds no domain for this name. The plain-ASCII sigyn.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release; this temple claims nothing it does not hold.[3]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈsiɣ.ʏn/ — Old Norse Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Si- — Voiceless alveolar sibilant [s] and short close front unrounded [i], stressed on the first syllable as in every Old Norse word; the i is short, not the long í
  • -g- — Intervocalic g is the voiced velar fricative [ɣ], a softened gh as in Spanish lago, never a hard stop between vowels
  • -yn — Short rounded front [ʏ], the Old Norse y — round the lips while saying i, as in German hübsch — then a clean voiced alveolar nasal [n]

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'SEE-ghyn' — two syllables with the weight on the first, the g soft and gargled, the y rounded and kept short; never 'SIGH-in'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Sigyn, wife of Loki; the name is attested only for this goddess
  • Younger Futhark — ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ (sikun), a normalized phonetic reconstruction — the short-twig row distinguishes neither k from g nor short from long vowels[2]
  • Skaldic verse — Sigyn in Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng (c. 900): Loki is 'farmr Sigynjar arma', the burden of Sigyn's arms[3]
  • Modern Scandinavian — Sigyn, a rare but living given name in Sweden, revived from the Edda

Sigyn is Tier 2 because the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses: no diacritic, no distinctive letter, no vowel length that spelling records. English and Old Norse write the name identically.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name is preserved in medieval Icelandic manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Sigyn — Old Norse (North Germanic), attested 13th c. CE in the Codex Regius and related codices, recording material centuries older.[1] Before the Latin bookhand, Old Norse names were carved in the Younger Futhark; no runic attestation of this theonym survives, so the manuscript form is the primary witness. The runic rendering is ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ (sikun).

The scholarly rendering proceeds step by step:

  • ᛋ (sól) writes /s/.
  • ᛁ (ís) writes both /i/ and /e/ — the short-twig row does not separate the front vowels.
  • ᚴ (kaun) writes both /k/ and /g/ (and the ng cluster) — the voiced stop of the manuscript form is invisible to the runes.
  • The spelling sikun is a normalized phonetic reconstruction: Younger Futhark does not distinguish voiced from voiceless stops, the rounded y from plain u, or short from long vowels — the runic form underspecifies exactly what the manuscript spelling records.[2]
  • The name's etymology — sigr 'victory' + vina 'girl-friend', yielding 'victorious girl-friend' — is registered in the lexica as probable, not proven.[3]

The Unicode restoration Sigyn is therefore identical to the ASCII form — the restoration is a matter of canonical reference, not rescued orthography.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix; Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. Sigyn.

05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Sigyn is catalogued under Loyalty, Wife of Loki — and the pairing is exact: the sources give her no hall, no cult, no second adventure; her whole divine identity is the marriage and the vigil it becomes.[1]

Loyalty

Hers is the corpus's purest loyalty because it is loyalty without a cause the texts endorse: Loki's sentence is just, the flyting's bill has come due, and still she stands there. The tradition never has her speak for him; it has her stay by him — a distinction the myth insists on.[1]

Wife of Loki

Snorri introduces her in three words — Loki's wife is named Sigyn — and everything the tradition remembers flows from the marriage: the human sons Nari and Narfi, the kenning 'the burden of Sigyn's arms', and the place beside him in the pit. Between Angrboða, the giantess who bore Loki the monsters, and Sigyn, the wife within the walls, the tradition draws its map of his two households.[1]

The Basin

The venom basin is the only office any source assigns her: holding it, filling it, emptying it. An office measured in drops is still an office, and it is the one mercy in the binding scene.[1]

Earthquakes

When the basin is full she must turn away, and in that gap the venom falls, Loki writhes against the iron bonds, and the earth shakes — 'those are now called earthquakes': her brief absence, written into the geology of the world.[2]

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 34 and 50, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. The Poetic Edda, trans. Carolyne Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014): Lokasenna, prose epilogue.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Sigyn's iconography is the sparsest in the Edda — one scene, endlessly repeated, fixed around a handful of objects no retelling can omit:[1]

  • The venom basin — loyalty made object: the mercy she holds over him, measured drop by drop
  • The venom-snake — Skaði's sentence, fastened above Loki's face: the fixed threat she stands between
  • The iron bonds — Nari's entrails turned to iron: the binding her vigil softens but cannot loose[1]
  • The three stones — shoulders, loins, and knees pinned in the gorge: the fixed point of her watch
  • The earthquake — the world shaking when the bowl is lifted: her absence, counted in tremors[2]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Sigyn's mythology is a naming, a kenning, a stanza, and one great scene — among the shortest dossiers in the corpus, and one of the oldest.

The Wife of Loki (Gylfaginning 34)

Snorri names her in the catalogue of the Æsir: Loki's wife is named Sigyn, and their son is Nari or Narfi. No father, no hall, no office — the wife exists to anchor Loki's human family, set in the same chapter against the giantess Angrboða, who bore him the wolf Fenrir, the World Serpent, and Hel. That the marriage was already proverbial is proven by the skalds: Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng (c. 900) calls Loki 'farmr Sigynjar arma', the burden of Sigyn's arms — a kenning that works only for an audience that already knows whose wife she is.[1]

The Captive in the Grove of Kettles (Völuspá 35)

The seeress of Völuspá, the oldest poem of the Codex Regius, sees the punishment before it happens: a captive lying under the grove of kettles, evil-minded, in form like Loki — 'and there sits Sigyn, by no means happy about her husband. Do you understand yet, or what more?' Seated, sorrowing, and staying: the whole of her eddic portrait, complete in two lines.[2]

The Flyting's Aftermath (Lokasenna, prose epilogue)

Lokasenna ends in a prose coda: Loki is bound across three stones with the entrails of his son Nari, while his son Narfi is turned into a wolf; Skaði fastens a venomous snake above his face. Sigyn sits there holding a basin under the drip, and when the basin is full she goes to pour it out — but while she is gone the venom falls on Loki's face, and he writhes so hard that the whole earth shakes. Those, the text says, are called earthquakes; there he lies until Ragnarök.[3]

The Binding of Loki (Gylfaginning 50)

Snorri sets the same scene at the close of the Baldr cycle. After the death of Baldr, the Æsir hunt Loki to Franangr's falls, where he hides as a salmon and is caught in the net he himself invented. His son Váli — not Óðinn's son of the same name — is changed into a wolf and tears apart his brother Nari; with Nari's entrails the gods bind Loki across three stones, and the bonds turn to iron. Skaði fastens the snake, and Sigyn, his wife, stands beside him holding the basin — when she turns away to empty it, the venom falls and the earth shakes. Snorri and the Lokasenna prose swap which son is gutted and which is made a wolf; they do not differ on the wife with the bowl.[4]

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Sigyn's truest kin are inside the corpus itself. She is the mirror of Nanna — the two wives of the Baldr catastrophe, one following her dead husband into the fire, the other staying beside her living one in the pit: devotion answered by death in the one, by endurance without end in the other. The parallel is structural, not borrowed — two tellings of the northern conviction that the household pays what the battlefield owes.[1]

Cross-culturally, scholarship has been cool. The bound-trickster scene has been set beside Prometheus since the nineteenth century, but no comparison touches the wife with the bowl, and the handbooks treat Sigyn strictly within the Loki complex: her attestation is thin, her etymology only probable, and no cult can be reconstructed from a basin held over a bound god.[2] What can be said cleanly is that no other figure in the Edda is defined so entirely by a single act of staying. Within the corpus, her related figures are the two households her story binds: Loki, Skaði, Angrboða, and Nanna.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigyn, Loki.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Sigyn; Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Sigyn.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Sigyn's afterlife is the afterlife of one image. The medieval earthquake-theory — the earth shakes when she lifts the bowl — survived for centuries as the North's household explanation of tremors, and every retelling of Loki's punishment, from the Kearys' Victorian Heroes of Asgard onward, keeps the wife and the basin at the centre of the scene: it is the one moment of grace in the flyting's long aftermath, and illustrators have never been able to leave it out.[1]

Nineteenth-century romanticism made her the Edda's type of wifely fidelity; twentieth-century scholarship made her the test case for how much a single stanza and a prose coda can hold. The name itself went quietly back into use — Sigyn is a rare but living given name in Sweden — and modern Heathen and Ásatrú communities honour her as the goddess of loyalty, fidelity, and endurance under an unjust sentence: the one who stays.[2]

Sources

  1. Keary, Annie, and Eliza Keary, The Heroes of Asgard: Tales from Scandinavian Mythology (London: Macmillan, 1857).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigyn.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Sigyn has one candidate image, and it is famous. The Gosforth Cross in Cumbria, carved in the first half of the tenth century, carries a programme of Ragnarök scenes that includes a bound male figure with a smaller figure kneeling beside him holding a bowl — standardly interpreted as Loki bound and Sigyn with the basin, the one Viking-Age monument that may show her at her post.[1] The identification is iconographic inference, not inscription: the cross names nobody, and the reading rests on the match with the literary scene.

Otherwise she is a wholly textual goddess: no runestone invokes her, no votive deposit names her, and no other image can be securely identified with her. Her attestation hangs on the manuscript witnesses of the Loki cycle — the Codex Regius for Völuspá 35 and the Lokasenna prose epilogue, and the principal codices of Snorri's Edda for Gylfaginning 34 and 50 — with Þjóðólfr's Haustlöng (c. 900), quoted in Skáldskaparmál, proving the kenning 'the burden of Sigyn's arms' was already current a century before Iceland's conversion.[2]

Sources

  1. Bailey, Richard N., Viking Age Sculpture in Northern England (London: Collins, 1980), on the Gosforth Cross.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigyn, Loki.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic Edda gives Sigyn exactly two lines of verse and one prose coda — and both are load-bearing. In Völuspá 35 the seeress, surveying the punishments that precede Ragnarök, sees a captive lying under the grove of kettles, evil-minded, in form like Loki — 'and there sits Sigyn, by no means happy about her husband. Do you understand yet, or what more?' It is her only verse appearance, and it is in the corpus's oldest and most authoritative poem: seated, sorrowing, and staying was already her whole character before Snorri was born.[1]

The Lokasenna prose epilogue supplies the scene the stanza implies. After the flyting at Ægir's feast, Loki is caught and bound across three stones with the entrails of his son Nari, while his son Narfi is turned into a wolf; Skaði fastens the venomous snake above his face, and Sigyn sits holding the basin under the drip — when she goes to empty it, the venom falls and the earth shakes, 'and those are called earthquakes'.[2] Elsewhere the poems keep her silence: Hyndluljóð's catalogue of Loki's monstrous brood names Angrboða, not Sigyn, and no poem gives her a spoken word — her eddic voice is the fact of her presence.

Sources

  1. Völuspá 35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Lokasenna, prose epilogue (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014); Hyndluljóð (Loki's brood), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose Edda is Sigyn's fullest witness, and it frames her in three touches. Gylfaginning 34 names her in the catalogue of the Æsir — Loki's wife, mother of Nari or Narfi — in the same breath that gives Loki his monstrous brood by Angrboða: the two households of the corpus's most divided figure, the human family within the walls and the monsters without. Gylfaginning 50 gives her the scene: after the salmon-net at Franangr and the binding on the three stones, 'Sigyn, Loki's wife, stands beside him and holds a basin under the dripping venom', and when she turns to empty it, the venom falls, he writhes, and 'those are now called earthquakes'.[1]

Skáldskaparmál adds the two archival facts. The ásynja þulur keep her by name in the catalogue of the goddesses — a deity of one scene, filed with bureaucratic fidelity among the ásynjur. And Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng, composed about 900 and quoted for the Þjazi myth, calls Loki 'farmr Sigynjar arma', the burden of Sigyn's arms: a kenning that presupposes the marriage as common knowledge three centuries before the Codex Regius was written — the oldest datable attestation of her name.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 34 and 50, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (þulur; Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, Haustlöng), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The runic corpus contains no invocation of the goddess Sigyn. No runestone asks her favour, no amulet bears her name, and no inscription yet found links a dedicant to her cult — a silence she shares with most figures of the Loki cycle and one that marks her as a literary-mythological figure rather than an independently culted deity.[1]

What can be said runologically is the rendering itself: in the Younger Futhark the name would have been carved ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ (sikun) — a normalized phonetic reconstruction, since the sixteen-rune row distinguishes neither k from g nor short from long vowels, and thus underspecifies exactly the features the manuscript spelling preserves. No Viking-Age carving of the theonym is known; the manuscript spelling of the Eddas remains the primary witness.[2]

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigyn, Loki.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the Eddas, Sigyn's medieval afterlife is a study in near-absence — and in what survives anyway. Saxo Grammaticus, who gives Nanna a whole second life in Denmark, never mentions her: the Gesta Danorum's Loki-figure (the bound Utgartha-Locus) carries no wife with a bowl, and the Danish tradition preserves no counter-version of her vigil.[1]

What the skaldic record preserves instead is the proof of her antiquity. Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng, composed about 900, already calls Loki 'farmr Sigynjar arma' — the burden of Sigyn's arms — in the midst of the Þjazi episode: a kenning that only works for an audience to whom 'Sigyn's husband' needs no explanation. Between the seeress's two lines in Völuspá and the skald's kenning, the wife with the bowl is attested in the two oldest strata of the whole corpus, and everywhere after only by the scene itself.[2]

Sources

  1. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sigyn.
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, Haustlöng), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Meditation & Reflection

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Sigyn is the Edda's one study of fidelity without hope. Every other loyalty in the corpus has a horizon: vengeance will come, the ransom may succeed, Ragnarök will end it. Hers has none — the binding is until the world's end, the snake does not sleep, the basin fills at the same rate forever. And the tradition is pitilessly exact about the terms of her mercy: it is not that she fails. It is that she must sometimes put the bowl down.[1]

In that gap the whole myth lives. The Norse imagination looked at the shaking earth and did not see a god's rage or a giant's hammer — it saw the moments when a devoted woman turns away to empty a basin. Loki deserves his sentence; the texts never pretend otherwise. Sigyn is not there to acquit him. She is there because love does not consult the verdict, and the oldest poem in the collection already knew it: there sits Sigyn, by no means happy — and there she sits.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 50, trans. Faulkes (1987); Völuspá 35, trans. Larrington (2014).
