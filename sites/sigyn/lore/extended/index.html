Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

The name Sigyn reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the norse tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse — Sigyn, wife of Loki; the name is attested only for this goddess, never as a human personal name in the medieval record.
  • Younger Futhark — ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ (sikun), a normalized phonetic reconstruction — the sixteen-rune row distinguishes neither k from g nor short from long vowels.
  • Skaldic verse — Sigyn in Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng (c. 900), where Loki is 'farmr Sigynjar arma' — the burden of Sigyn's arms.
  • Modern Scandinavian — Sigyn, a rare but living given name in Sweden, revived from the Edda.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Sigyn in Later Traditions

Sigyn's truest kin are inside the corpus itself. She is the mirror of [[nanna|Nanna]] — the two wives of the Baldr catastrophe, one following her dead husband into the fire, the other staying beside her living one in the pit; devotion answered by death in the one, by endurance without end in the other. Scholarship has been cool toward grander comparisons. Simek and Orchard treat Sigyn strictly within the Loki complex: her attestation is thin, her etymology only probable, and no cult can be reconstructed from a bowl held over a bound god. Lindow reads the scene as the tradition's darkest domestic image — the flyting's bill coming due at the household, not on the battlefield — and notes that the earthquake aetiology made Sigyn's vigil the medieval North's explanation for the trembling earth. What can be said cleanly is that no other figure in the Edda is defined so entirely by a single act of staying.

Modern Legacy

Sigyn's afterlife is the afterlife of one image. The medieval earthquake-theory — the earth shakes when she lifts the bowl — survived for centuries as the North's household explanation of tremors, and every retelling of Loki's punishment, from the Kearys' Victorian 'Heroes of Asgard' onward, keeps the wife and the basin at the centre of the scene: it is the one moment of grace in the flyting's long aftermath, and illustrators have never been able to leave it out. Nineteenth-century romanticism made her the Edda's type of wifely fidelity; twentieth-century scholarship made her the test case for how much a single stanza and a prose coda can hold. The name itself went quietly back into use — Sigyn is a rare but living given name in Sweden — and modern Heathen and Ásatrú communities honour her as the goddess of loyalty, fidelity, and endurance under an unjust sentence: the one who stays.

In the Earth

Sigyn has one candidate image and it is famous: the Gosforth Cross in Cumbria, carved in the first half of the tenth century, whose programme of Ragnarök scenes includes a bound male figure with a smaller figure kneeling beside him holding a bowl — standardly interpreted as Loki bound and Sigyn with the basin (Bailey; Lindow), the one Viking-Age monument that may show her at her post. Otherwise she is a wholly textual goddess: no runestone invokes her, no votive deposit names her, and no other image can be securely identified. Her attestation hangs on the manuscript witnesses of the Loki cycle — the Codex Regius for Völuspá 35 and the Lokasenna prose epilogue, and the Codex Regius, Codex Wormianus, and Uppsala codices of Snorri's Edda for Gylfaginning 34 and 50 — with Þjóðólfr's Haustlöng (c. 900), quoted in Skáldskaparmál, proving the kenning 'the burden of Sigyn's arms' was already current a century before Iceland's conversion.

05

The Meditation

What Sigyn means — beyond the dictionary

Sigyn is the Edda's one study of fidelity without hope. Every other loyalty in the corpus has a horizon: vengeance will come, the ransom may succeed, Ragnarök will end it. Hers has none — the binding is until the world's end, the snake does not sleep, the basin fills at the same rate forever. And the tradition is pitilessly exact about the terms of her mercy: it is not that she fails. It is that she must sometimes put the bowl down.

In that gap the whole myth lives. The Norse imagination looked at the shaking earth and did not see a god's rage or a giant's hammer — it saw the moments when a devoted woman turns away to empty a basin. Loki deserves his sentence; the texts never pretend otherwise. Sigyn is not there to acquit him. She is there because love does not consult the verdict, and the oldest poem in the collection already knew it: there sits Sigyn, by no means happy — and there she sits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Sigyn, Loyalty, Wife of Loki, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Sigyn?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Sigyn is /ˈsiɣ.ʏn/ — approximately 'SEE-ghyn' — two syllables with the weight on the first, the g a soft gargled gh, the y rounded like German ü and kept short; never 'SIGH-in' and never 'sig-WIN'.

02What does Sigyn mean?

Sigyn means Victorious girl-friend in the norse tradition.

03What are the symbols of Sigyn?

Sigyn is associated with The venom basin (Loyalty made object — the mercy she holds over him, measured drop by drop), The venom-snake (Skaði's sentence, fastened above Loki's face — the fixed threat Sigyn stands between), The iron bonds (Nari's entrails turned to iron — the binding her vigil softens but cannot loose), The three stones (Loki's anchor in the gorge — shoulders, loins, and knees pinned beneath the earth), The earthquake (The world shaking when the bowl is lifted — her brief absence, written in tremors).

04Why restore Sigyn in Unicode?

Sigyn is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Sigyn?

“The Wife of Loki” — Sigyn's dossier is small and strangely old: a naming in Snorri's catalogue of the gods, a kenning from a skald of Harald Fairhair's age, one stanza in the oldest poem of the Edda, and then the scene that made her immortal — the binding of Loki, where she does the only thing left to do and does it forever. The tradition never gives her a word to say. It gives her a bowl. The full telling lives on the lore page.
