The hidden history behind Sigyn

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Sigyn needs nothing of the kind, and her story needs everything: two lines in the oldest poem of the Edda, a kenning from the age of Harald Fairhair, and one scene beneath the earth that the tradition never let go of — the wife with the bowl, catching the venom over her bound husband's face. This post walks the full record — name, runes, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the most patient figure in the corpus has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII sigyn.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Sigyn (sigyn) — Loyalty, Wife of Loki · Victorious girl-friend — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Loyalty, Wife of Loki". The name's standard gloss is "Victorious girl-friend", from sigr 'victory' and vina 'girl-friend' — a probable derivation, not a proven one.

Sigyn is the wife of Loki and the mother of his human sons, Nari and Narfi — and the goddess of a single act of staying. When the Æsir bound Loki across three stones with his own son's entrails, and Skaði fastened a venom-snake above his face, Sigyn took her place beside him holding a basin under the drip. Only when the basin fills and she turns away to empty it does the venom fall — and then Loki writhes so hard that the whole earth shakes, and men call it an earthquake.

PuniCodex restores the name as Sigyn — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII sigyn.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in normalized Old Norse as Sigyn, and it belongs to the goddess alone — the medieval record knows no human bearer. The standard etymology takes it as a compound of sigr, 'victory', and vina, 'girl-friend, female friend', yielding 'victorious girl-friend' — a friendly-woman name for the friendliest wife in the corpus. De Vries registers the derivation; Cleasby–Vigfusson record the name under Loki's wife; Simek and Orchard repeat it with the usual scholarly reserve. None of the lexica proposes a serious rival, and none calls the question settled.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which the name would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs sikun.

Here the short-twig row underspecifies almost everything the manuscripts preserve: ᛁ (ís) writes both i and e, ᚴ (kaun) writes both k and g, and the rounded y falls to plain u. The rendering is therefore a normalized phonetic reconstruction, not an attestation — no Viking-Age carving of the theonym is known, and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Eddas, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈsiɣ.ʏn/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: SEE-ghyn — two syllables with the weight on the first, the g a soft gargled gh as in Spanish lago, the y rounded like German ü and kept short; never 'SIGH-in'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Sigyn, wife of Loki; the name is attested only for this goddess
  • Younger Futhark — ᛋᛁᚴᚢᚾ (sikun), a normalized phonetic reconstruction
  • Skaldic verse — Sigyn in Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng (c. 900): Loki is 'farmr Sigynjar arma', the burden of Sigyn's arms
  • Modern Scandinavian — Sigyn, a rare but living given name in Sweden

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. The i is short, the intervocalic g a fricative rather than a stop, and the y a rounded front vowel the spelling never marks — sound carries what script does not.

Mythology

Sigyn's mythology is a naming, a kenning, a stanza, and one great scene — among the shortest dossiers in the corpus, and one of the oldest.

The Wife of Loki (Gylfaginning 34)

Snorri names her in the catalogue of the Æsir: Loki's wife is named Sigyn, and their son is Nari or Narfi. No father, no hall, no office — the wife anchors Loki's human family, set in the same chapter against the giantess Angrboða, who bore him the wolf Fenrir, the World Serpent, and Hel. That the marriage was already proverbial is proven by the skalds: Þjóðólfr of Hvinir's Haustlöng, composed around 900, calls Loki 'farmr Sigynjar arma' — the burden of Sigyn's arms — a kenning that works only for an audience that already knows whose wife she is.

The Captive in the Grove of Kettles (Völuspá 35)

The seeress of Völuspá, the oldest poem of the Codex Regius, sees the punishment before it happens: a captive lying under the grove of kettles, evil-minded, in form like Loki — 'and there sits Sigyn, by no means happy about her husband. Do you understand yet, or what more?' Seated, sorrowing, and staying: the whole of her eddic portrait, complete in two lines.

The Flyting's Aftermath (Lokasenna, prose epilogue)

Lokasenna ends in a prose coda as brutal as anything in the corpus. Loki is bound across three stones with the entrails of his son Nari, while his son Narfi is turned into a wolf; Skaði fastens a venomous snake above his face. Sigyn sits there holding a basin under the drip, and when the basin is full she goes to pour it out — but while she is gone the venom falls on Loki's face, and he writhes so hard that the whole earth shakes. Those, the text says, are called earthquakes; there he lies until Ragnarök.

The Binding of Loki (Gylfaginning 50)

Snorri sets the same scene at the close of the Baldr cycle. After the death of Baldr, the Æsir hunt Loki to Franangr's falls, where he hides as a salmon and is caught in the net he himself invented. His son Váli — not Óðinn's son of the same name — is changed into a wolf and tears apart his brother Nari; with Nari's entrails the gods bind Loki across three stones, and the bonds turn to iron. Skaði fastens the snake, and Sigyn stands beside him holding the basin. Snorri and the Lokasenna prose swap which son is gutted and which is made a wolf; they do not differ on the wife with the bowl.

Symbols & Iconography

Sigyn's iconography is the sparsest in the Edda — one scene, endlessly repeated, fixed around a handful of objects no retelling can omit:

  • The venom basin — loyalty made object: the mercy she holds over him, measured drop by drop
  • The venom-snake — Skaði's sentence, fastened above Loki's face: the fixed threat she stands between
  • The iron bonds — Nari's entrails turned to iron: the binding her vigil softens but cannot loose
  • The three stones — shoulders, loins, and knees pinned in the gorge: the fixed point of her watch
  • The earthquake — the world shaking when the bowl is lifted: her absence, counted in tremors

Archaeology & Evidence

Sigyn has one candidate image, and it is famous. The Gosforth Cross in Cumbria, carved in the first half of the tenth century, carries a programme of Ragnarök scenes that includes a bound male figure with a smaller figure kneeling beside him holding a bowl — standardly interpreted as Loki bound and Sigyn with the basin, the one Viking-Age monument that may show her at her post. The identification is iconographic inference, not inscription: the cross names nobody. Otherwise she is wholly textual: no runestone invokes her, and her attestation hangs on the Codex Regius (Völuspá 35, the Lokasenna prose), the principal codices of Snorri's Edda (Gylfaginning 34 and 50), and Þjóðólfr's Haustlöng — proof that the kenning 'the burden of Sigyn's arms' was already current a century before Iceland's conversion.

Realm & Domain

Sigyn's domain is Loyalty, Wife of Loki — and the pairing is exact: the sources give her no hall, no cult, no second adventure; her whole divine identity is the marriage and the vigil it becomes.

Loyalty

Hers is the corpus's purest loyalty because it is loyalty without a cause the texts endorse: Loki's sentence is just, and still she stands there. The tradition never has her speak for him; it has her stay by him.

Wife of Loki

Snorri introduces her in three words, and everything the tradition remembers flows from the marriage: the human sons, the kenning, the place beside him in the pit.

The Basin

The venom basin is the only office any source assigns her: holding it, filling it, emptying it. An office measured in drops is still an office, and it is the one mercy in the binding scene.

Earthquakes

When the basin is full she must turn away, and in that gap the venom falls and the earth shakes — her brief absence, written into the geology of the world.

Across Cultures

Sigyn's truest kin are inside the corpus itself. She is the mirror of Nanna — the two wives of the Baldr catastrophe, one following her dead husband into the fire, the other staying beside her living one in the pit: devotion answered by death in the one, by endurance without end in the other. Cross-culturally, scholarship has been cool: the bound-trickster scene has been set beside Prometheus since the nineteenth century, but no comparison touches the wife with the bowl, and the handbooks treat Sigyn strictly within the Loki complex — her attestation too thin for cult reconstruction, her etymology only probable. What can be said cleanly is that no other figure in the Edda is defined so entirely by a single act of staying. Within the corpus, her related figures are the two households her story binds: Loki, Skaði, Angrboða, and Nanna.

Cultural Legacy

Sigyn's afterlife is the afterlife of one image. The medieval earthquake-theory — the earth shakes when she lifts the bowl — survived for centuries as the North's household explanation of tremors, and every retelling of Loki's punishment, from the Kearys' Victorian 'Heroes of Asgard' onward, keeps the wife and the basin at the centre of the scene. Nineteenth-century romanticism made her the Edda's type of wifely fidelity; twentieth-century scholarship made her the test case for how much a single stanza and a prose coda can hold. The name itself went quietly back into use — Sigyn is a rare but living given name in Sweden — and modern Heathen and Ásatrú communities honour her as the goddess of loyalty, fidelity, and endurance under an unjust sentence: the one who stays.

A Meditation

Sigyn is the Edda's one study of fidelity without hope. Every other loyalty in the corpus has a horizon: vengeance will come, the ransom may succeed, Ragnarök will end it. Hers has none — the binding is until the world's end, the snake does not sleep, the basin fills at the same rate forever. And the tradition is pitilessly exact about the terms of her mercy: it is not that she fails. It is that she must sometimes put the bowl down. In that gap the whole myth lives. The Norse imagination looked at the shaking earth and did not see a god's rage or a giant's hammer — it saw the moments when a devoted woman turns away to empty a basin. Loki deserves his sentence; the texts never pretend otherwise. Sigyn is not there to acquit him. She is there because love does not consult the verdict, and the oldest poem in the collection already knew it: there sits Sigyn, by no means happy — and there she sits.

The Unicode Restoration

Sigyn is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length that spelling records. Old Norse Sigyn carries none of these. The manuscripts write five plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the goddess; it is an honest report about her name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Sigyn mean? The standard gloss is "Victorious girl-friend" — a compound of sigr 'victory' and vina 'girl-friend'. The derivation is probable rather than proven; the lexica register it with reserve, and so does this temple.

Which tradition does Sigyn belong to? Sigyn is catalogued in the Norse pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon.

Why is Sigyn classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the goddess's standing is beside the point.

Is sigyn.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

What does Sigyn do in the sources? She holds the basin. In Völuspá she sits beside the bound captive; in the Lokasenna prose and Gylfaginning 50 she stands under the venom-snake catching its drip over Loki's face, and when she turns away to empty the full basin, his writhing shakes the earth — the myth's explanation of earthquakes.
