Overview

Hēraklēs (herakles) — The Glory of Labors — ​belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Strength, Labours, Heroism". The name means "Glory of Hera (from Ἥρα + κλέος)".

Hēraklēs is the greatest of Greek heroes and ​the only one to become a full Olympian god. His very name means 'Glory of Hera,' yet Hera persecuted him from the cradle, driving him to madness and murder. His life is a ​sequence of impossible tasks performed under duress: the Twelve Labours, the conquest of monsters, the rescue of captives, and finally a death by fire that turned into immortality.

The Name

The name is attested in Greek as Ἡρακλῆς. Etymologically it means "Glory of Hera (from ​Ἥρα + κλέος)".

The ASCII form herakles survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Hēraklēs recovers the ​vowel length of the original directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves vowel length — a description computed from the displayed form itself.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • h → H — Rough ‌breathing

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Greek as Ἡρακλῆς — ‌Greek alphabet (Classical / Attic), attested Ancient Greek, c. 8th century BCE – present, in Greece and the Greek-speaking Mediterranean. The script is written left-to-right.

The scholarly transliteration is Hēraklēs (Greek alphabet with ​polytonic accents), giving the normalized reading /hɛːˈra.klɛːs/.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • The Greek form Ἡρακλῆς is written in the Classical Greek alphabet.
  • Letters with acute, grave, or circumflex accents preserve the pitch ‌accent of Ancient Greek.
  • Macrons and omegas (η, ω) mark long vowels, a feature lost in the plain ASCII form.
  • The Unicode restoration Hēraklēs encodes the scholarly spelling as a registrable domain name.

​The name is written in Greek as Ἡρακλῆς (Hēraklēs), composed of Ἥρᾱ (Hḗrā, 'Hera') and κλέος (kléos, 'glory'). The registrable form Hēraklēs marks both long eta vowels with macrons and omits the final circumflex ​for DNS compatibility. The result preserves the two length features that make the name a full restoration while remaining typeable as an internationalized domain label.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is ​/hɛː.ra.klɛ̂ːs/ — Classical Attic Greek Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Hē- — Rough breathing [h] plus long open-mid front eta [ɛː] — the 'Hera' element, sustained and deep.
  • -ra- — Short alpha with rolling ​alveolar rho, the link between the two halves of the name.
  • -klēs — Kappa-lambda followed by long eta with circumflex [klɛ̂ːs], carrying both length and the falling pitch accent.

For the modern speaker, the ​closest approximation is: "hay-RAH-KLAYS" — first syllable deeper and longer than English 'hay'; the final syllable is held and falls in pitch, like 'klays' sung on a descending note.

Kindred and historical forms ‌of the name:

  • Greek — Ἥρᾱ (Hḗrā), 'Hera'
  • Greek — κλέος (kléos), 'glory'
  • Latin — Herculēs, the Roman adaptation via Etruscan hercle

Hēraklēs preserves both features because Classical Attic Ἡρακλῆς contains both length ​(long η in the first and final syllables) and stress (circumflex on the final -klēs). The registrable form Hēraklēs preserves length with macrons; the acute/circumflex is omitted because fully accented Greek letters are often ‌untypeable and are not reliably registrable as IDN labels. Reconstruction follows Allen, Vox Graeca: The Pronunciation of Classical Greek, 3rd ed. (Cambridge, 1987); LSJ; and Beekes, Etymological Dictionary of Greek (Brill, 2010).

Mythology

​Hēraklēs's mythology is enormous and contradictory, preserved in epic, lyric, tragedy, and handbook. What holds it together is the figure of a mortal strong enough to challenge gods and monsters, yet vulnerable enough to ‌weep, rage, and die. Here are four of its load-bearing walls.

A Hero Foretold (Theogony)

Hesiod already knows Hēraklēs as the hero fated to settle the gods' war with the Giants: in the ​Theogony (950–955) he appears among the mortal unions of Zeús — by Alkmēnē, who bore him 'bold in might' in Thebes — and the poet makes his birth a debt of cosmic order, ‌for Zeús's plan needed a mortal strong enough to finish what the gods began. His name, 'Glory of Hera,' is the tradition's own irony: the queen of the gods persecutes him from the cradle, ​sending two serpents against his crib, which the infant strangled one in each fist — the scene Pindar (Nemean 1) and Theocritus (Idyll 24) immortalized. Homer already sings of him as one who ‌walked the earth yet now feasts among the immortals (Odyssey 11.602–604). The hero's whole career is compressed in that double fact: born to be attacked, destined to be deified.

The Lion, the Hydra, ​and the Boar (The Labours)

The first labour sent Hēraklēs against the Nemean lion, whose hide no weapon could pierce; he strangled it in its cave and wore the pelt thereafter, its gaping maw ​as his helmet (Apollodorus 2.5.1). The second pitted him against the Lernaean hydra, whose severed heads multiplied — Iolaos cauterized each neck with fire while Hēraklēs struck, and Hera, aiding the monster, sent ‌a giant crab that became the constellation Cancer (Apollodorus 2.5.2; Eratosthenes, Catasterismi). The fourth labour brought the Erymanthian boar alive to Mycenae — the comic masterpiece of the cycle, Eurystheus so terrified he hid ‌in a bronze storage jar while the hero hoisted the squealing beast overhead. Behind the folktale comedy lies the theology of the series: each labour drags a creature of chaos into the human ‌world and converts it to order — a hide that becomes armor, a swamp made safe, a mountain terror that becomes a king's lesson.

Kerberos and the Living Descent (The Underworld)

The twelfth ​labour sent Hēraklēs down to fetch Kerberos 'with no weapon, neither iron nor bronze' (Apollodorus 2.5.12) — through a dark crack at Tainaron, past the gates where Hades stood with drawn sword until Athena ​intervened. In the dark he found Theseus and Peirithoos frozen in chairs of forgetfulness for their attempt to steal Persephonē; he freed Theseus, but the earth held Peirithoos. He met the shade of ‌Meleager, whose tale of dying for love moved him to seek the sister's hand — the fatal marriage to Deianeira. Then he asked Hades for the hound, and when Hades consented, he wrestled ​the three-headed beast into daylight, choking it with his bare arms until it submitted. The labour is the cycle's thesis in one image: the strongest hero enters death unarmed and returns with death's own ​guardian leashed. Every descent rehearsed his apotheosis.

The Death of Hēraklēs (Tragedy)

Sophocles' Women of Trachis gives the hero his end. The centaur Nessus, dying from Hēraklēs' arrow, told Deianeira to keep his ‌blood as a love-charm; years later, fearing her husband's love for Iole, she anointed a robe with it and sent it to him. The blood — poisoned by the Hydra's venom on the arrow ​— ate into his flesh like acid. Brought home in agony from Euboea, he ordered his own pyre on Mount Oeta, gave his bow to Poeas, and mounted the flames. Athena's chariot received ​him: from the fire he ascended to Olympus, reconciled at last with Hera, married to Hebe, Youth — and Homer already knew the ending (Odyssey 11.602–604), while Euripides stages the apotheosis on the ‌machine in his Heracles. The last labour is the only one performed on himself — and its reward is the one Eurystheus never assigned: immortality as the price of a fully burned life.

Symbols ​& Iconography

The iconography associated with Hēraklēs concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Club — His primary weapon, cut from wild olive on Mount ‌Helicon
  • Lion skin — The impenetrable pelt of the Nemean lion, his first labour
  • Bow — The gift of Apollo, used against the Stymphalian birds and Geryon
  • The Pillars — The western boundary ​of the world, sometimes said to bear the inscription 'Non plus ultra'
  • Golden apples — The fruit of the Hesperides, retrieved in his eleventh labour

Vase painting, sculpture, and votive reliefs disseminate the visual ​identity of Hēraklēs. Artists returned to attributes such as Club, Lion skin, Bow, creating a recognizable iconographic shorthand.

The restored spelling Hēraklēs reproduces in text the same precision that painters sought in image. ​Each attribute encodes a claim about power, identity, or function.

The Homeric Hymns

The Homeric Hymns, a collection of ancient Greek hexameter poems, include invocations and narratives that shape the reception of Hēraklēs. While Hēraklēs does not always receive a ‌separate, surviving hymn, the figure appears in the broader epic and hymnic environment that celebrates the Olympian order.

The hymns map divine genealogy, power, and cultic honor, and Hēraklēs's role as strength places the ​name within that hexameter world. The PuniCodex restoration Hēraklēs preserves the prosodic features that rhapsodes would have articulated.

Archaeology & Evidence

Thebes claimed Hēraklēs's birth and held games in his honor. Mycenaean Tiryns and Thebes have yielded palace remains that later Greeks ​associated with his legends. Nemea preserves the cave of the Nemean lion and the stadium of the Nemean Games. Olympia's Temple of Zeus displayed the Twelve Labours in its metopes. Athens maintained a ‌prominent Herakleion and depicted him with Theseus on vase paintings. Mount Oeta in Thessaly was the traditional site of his funeral pyre and apotheosis.

Realm & Domain

Hēraklēs is the greatest of Greek heroes ‌and the only one to become a full Olympian god. His very name means 'Glory of Hera,' yet Hera persecuted him from the cradle, driving him to madness and murder. His life is ‌a sequence of impossible tasks performed under duress: the Twelve Labours, the conquest of monsters, the rescue of captives, and finally a death by fire that turned into immortality.

The Twelve Labours

Imposed ‌by Eurystheus at Hera's prompting: the Nemean lion, Lernaean hydra, Ceryneian hind, Erymanthian boar, Augean stables, Stymphalian birds, Cretan bull, mares of Diomedes, girdle of Hippolyta, cattle of Geryon, apples of the Hesperides, and ​Kerberos from Hades.

Divine Strength

Son of Zeús by Alkmēnē, he possesses strength beyond mortality from infancy; as a baby he strangled the serpents Hera sent against him.

Madness and Atonement

Hera's frenzy ‌made him kill his wife Megara and their children; the Delphic oracle sentenced him to serve Eurystheus, transforming violence into labour.

Apotheosis

Poisoned by the blood of the centaur Nessus, he mounted a ​funeral pyre on Mount Oeta; from the flames he ascended to Olympus and married Hebe.

Across Cultures

The Greeks themselves identified Hēraklēs with foreign heroes and gods. Herodotus (2.42–45) argues that the Greek Heracles ​is the youngest of three figures bearing the name: an Egyptian god, a Phoenician hero identified with Melqart, and the Greek son of Alcmene. The Phoenician Melqart of Tyre, worshipped from Spain to ​the Levant, was the closest parallel: a god of civic protection, colonization, and annual death-and-rebirth rituals. The Romans made him Hercules and emphasized his civilizing labors; his cult was among the earliest Greek imports ‌to Italy. In Egypt he was sometimes identified with Khonsu or Heryshaf. Through Stoic and Cynic philosophy he became the model of virtuous endurance — the hero who chooses the difficult path of ​excellence.

Cultural Legacy

Hēraklēs never left Western culture. The Roman Hercules gave us the word herculean; his Twelve Labours remain the archetype of the ​heroic quest, reused from medieval romance to modern video games. The constellation Hercules still dominates the northern summer sky. Renaissance and Baroque artists painted his choice between Virtue and Vice; Disney and Marvel ‌recast him as a family-friendly strongman. Bodybuilding culture, military units, and even NASA have claimed his name. More deeply, Hēraklēs is the type of the suffering hero whose glory is earned through pain ​— a figure who answers the ancient question of how a mortal can matter in a world ruled by gods.

A Meditation

Hēraklēs is the most human of the great heroes because his strength is inseparable from ‌his wounds. He kills what he loves, serves a king he despises, and is destroyed by the very violence he survived. His name prophesies glory, but the glory comes only after a lifetime ​of labour. In this he is the Greek answer to the problem of suffering: not that pain is meaningless, but that it can be transformed into renown.

The restoration of Hēraklēs is therefore ‌fitting. His name contains the long vowels of endurance — the sustained η that opens and closes it — and the circumflex of a voice that falls only after it has risen. To say ​it correctly is to remember that glory is not given; it is wrestled from monsters, one labour at a time.
