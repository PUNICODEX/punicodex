The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᛘᛅᚾᛁ

The name in its original Norse form. Máni (ᛘᛅᚾᛁ) is attested in the source tradition — “Moon (the personified moon, brother of Sól)”. Its acute stress marks carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

mani

Reduced to plain mani, the name loses everything that made it specific: acute stress marks. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Máni

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Máni restores acute stress marks, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Máni.com → xn--mni-ela.com

The non-ASCII characters in Máni are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Máni.

Pronunciation

How Máni was spoken

/ˈmɑːni/ Old Norse Reconstruction
M- Bilabial nasal [m], unchanged from Proto-Germanic *m to Old Norse; the name opens with the same consonant as English 'moon'
-á- Long open back [ɑː] with the acute marking length; Old Norse stress falls on the first syllable by default
-ni Short alveolar nasal [n] and close front [i]; the light second syllable of the nominative singular

Modern Approximation

'MAH-nee' — hold the first vowel long and open, as in 'father', with the weight squarely on the first syllable and a quick, light '-nee' to close.

Etymological Kin

  • Old English Mōna, the masculine moon of the Anglo-Saxons, whose name survives in Monday (Mōnandæg)
  • Old High German Māno, the continental Germanic moon, likewise masculine
  • Proto-Germanic *Mēnô, 'moon', a masculine noun — an inheritance the Germanic languages kept where most Indo-European tongues made the moon feminine
  • Modern Icelandic Máni, the living reflex, unchanged in form and gender

The Accent / Script Rule

Máni is Tier 1: the acute on á marks the long vowel [ɑː], a distinctive feature the ASCII form Mani loses; Old Norse initial stress is the default and needs no further mark. The name is simply the common noun máni, 'moon', raised to a person — the same word that gives mánaðr, 'month'. Attestation and reconstruction follow Cleasby & Vigfusson, An Icelandic-English Dictionary (1874), s.v. máni; and Zoëga, A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (1910), s.v. máni.

05

Moon, Night, Time

The domain of Máni

In the norse tradition, Máni is the moon itself made personal — the shining body that crosses the night sky, imagined as a god who steers it. Where Sól, his sister, drives the sun, Máni governs the gentler light: Snorri says he 'steers the course of the moon and rules the new and the waning moons,' the phases by which men first learned to count their days.

His is a charged quietness. The gods themselves assigned him his course, a wolf runs forever at his heels, and two earth-born children ride with him, their pail still visible on his face. Every month that turns is his signature: mánaðr, 'month', is his own word, and Monday is his day.

Mundilfari's Boast

A father's pride named his children Moon and Sun; the gods answered the boast by setting the pair to drive the lights of heaven.

Hati's Pursuit

The wolf Hati Hróðvitnisson runs before the moon and will one day take it; at Ragnarǫk the Moon-Hound swallows the light and spatters the sky with blood.

Bil and Hjúki

Two children with a pail on a pole, taken up from the well Byrgir; they follow Máni, Snorri says, 'as can be seen from earth' — the man in the moon.

Measurer of Months

He rules the new and waning moons, the counting of time itself; mánaðr, 'month', is his word, and the calendar was his first temple.

Sacred Symbols

Ný ok nið (new and waning moons) The phases over which Máni rules, the divine charge by which the gods ordained the counting of years for men
Hati Hróðvitnisson The wolf who runs before the moon and gives its crossing its haste, the promise that even the lights of heaven are hunted
Mánagarmr (Moon-Hound) The mightiest of the Iron-Wood brood, who fattens on the lives of the dying and swallows the moon at the world's end
Sægr and Simul (pail and pole) The burden Bil and Hjúki carried from the well Byrgir, still legible as the markings on the moon's face
The month-mark Mánaðr, the month — the unit of time that bears his name in every Germanic calendar
06

Mythology

Stories of Máni

Máni's mythology is brief but exact: he has no hammer-duels and no disguises, only a course to keep and a hunter behind him. The Eddas give him three scenes — a father's fatal boast, a wolf's patient pursuit, and two children lifted from a well — and together they explain both why the moon moves and why it keeps its human face. Around those scenes gathers the quieter charge the Vafþrúðnismál records: the gods shaped the new and waning moons so that men could reckon their years.

Vafþrúðnismál / Gylfaginning

Mundilfari's Boast

In the Vafþrúðnismál, the giant Vafþrúðnir tells Óðinn that Mundilfari is the father of Máni and of Sól alike. Snorri, in Gylfaginning, gives the story its edge: Mundilfari's two children were so fair that he named his son Máni, 'Moon', and his daughter Sól, 'Sun', and married Sól to a man named Glenr. The gods were angered by this ofdramb — this overweening pride — and took the siblings and set them in the heavens.

The punishment is an appointment. Sól is made to drive the horses of the sun, Árvakr and Alsviðr, while Máni is charged with steering the course of the moon and ruling its waxing and waning. What a father claimed in arrogance, the gods confirmed in office: the names became the duties, and the children became the sky.

Grímnismál / Gylfaginning

The Wolf at the Moon's Heels

The moon does not drift; it flees. Snorri explains its speed by its hunter: two wolves run with the lights of heaven, and Hati Hróðvitnisson is the one who runs before the moon and means to take it — 'and so it will come to pass.' The Grímnismál knows the same pursuit, naming Hati as the son of Hróðvitnir who waits upon the bright bride of the sky.

The chase has an ending, and the Eddas do not flinch from it. From the Iron-Wood brood, Snorri says, will come the mightiest wolf of all, called Mánagarmr, the Moon-Hound: he fills himself with the lives of all who die, swallows the moon, and spatters heaven and air with blood, so that the sun loses its shining. The Vǫluspá names him the moon's devourer in a troll's skin. At Ragnarǫk the long pursuit is finished, and the calendar itself goes dark.

Gylfaginning

Bil and Hjúki at the Well

Snorri records that Máni took two children up from the earth, Bil and Hjúki, as they walked from the well called Byrgir, carrying on their shoulders the pail Sægr — often read as a mead-tub — slung from the pole Simul. Their father is named Viðfinnr. 'These children follow Máni,' Snorri adds, 'as can be seen from earth': look at the moon and you will find them there.

This is the Norse answer to the man in the moon — not a face but two small figures under a burden, fixed forever in mid-errand. The detail of the well, the pail, and the pole is too homely to be invented ornament; it reads as the residue of nursery-lore older than the manuscript, the kind of story told to children who had already been shown the markings in the moon. Scholars have sometimes linked the pair to the Jack and Jill of English rhyme, but that connection is modern speculation, not attestation.

Extended Lore

Names are not merely labels; they are compressed worlds. Máni compresses the oldest technology there is: the counting of time by a light that reliably changes. Before calendars were written they were watched, and the watcher-god is the moon — not its conqueror, like Þórr riding out, but its keeper, driving a course he did not choose, with a wolf behind him and two lost children for company. His myth is the honest one about time: it moves because it is pursued, it is beautiful because it is borrowed, and it will one day be swallowed. Yet the word he leaves behind — mánaðr, the month — is how every farmer, sailor, and poet after him held the dark at bay. Unicode restoration returns that world to readable form: the acute on á is the long vowel the whole calendar leans on.
