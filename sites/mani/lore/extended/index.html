Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script ᛘᛅᚾᛁ Máni — "Moon (the personified moon, brother of Sól)"
Unicode Restoration Máni Restored stress, length, and script
Modern ASCII mani Plain-ASCII fallback

Máni is Tier 1: the acute on á marks the long vowel [ɑː], a distinctive feature the ASCII form Mani loses; Old Norse initial stress is the default and needs no further mark. The name is simply the common noun máni, 'moon', raised to a person — the same word that gives mánaðr, 'month'. Attestation and reconstruction follow Cleasby & Vigfusson, An Icelandic-English Dictionary (1874), s.v. máni; and Zoëga, A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (1910), s.v. máni.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Old English — Mōna, the masculine moon of the Anglo-Saxons, whose name survives in Monday (Mōnandæg)
  • Old High German — Māno, the continental Germanic moon, likewise masculine
  • Proto-Germanic — *Mēnô, 'moon', a masculine noun — an inheritance the Germanic languages kept where most Indo-European tongues made the moon feminine
  • Modern Icelandic — Máni, the living reflex, unchanged in form and gender

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

In the norse tradition, Máni is the moon itself made personal — the shining body that crosses the night sky, imagined as a god who steers it. Where Sól, his sister, drives the sun, Máni governs the gentler light: Snorri says he 'steers the course of the moon and rules the new and the waning moons,' the phases by which men first learned to count their days.

His is a charged quietness. The gods themselves assigned him his course, a wolf runs forever at his heels, and two earth-born children ride with him, their pail still visible on his face. Every month that turns is his signature: mánaðr, 'month', is his own word, and Monday is his day.

Máni in Later Traditions

Máni belongs to the common Germanic inheritance in which the moon is grammatically and mythologically masculine — Old English Mōna, Old High German Māno, Gothic mēna — a striking counterweight to the feminine moons of the classical world, Greek Selēnē and Latin Luna. The word itself runs deeper: Proto-Indo-European *mḗh₁nōt- / *mḗh₁n̥s meant both 'moon' and 'month', the double sense Máni still carries in mánaðr, so that his personification is the personification of time-reckoning itself. The pairing of a moon-god with a sun-goddess, brother and sister, is the Germanic reversal of the Greek Selene and Helios pattern, though the daily celestial chase — light pursued by devourer — has echoes from the wolves of Ragnarǫk back to the eclipse-monsters of other traditions. The liveliest survival is folkloric: the Norse reading of the moon's markings as two children with a pail stands at the head of the whole northern 'man in the moon' tradition that later fills English ballads and nursery rhymes, and the tempting equation of Hjúki and Bil with Jack and Jill has been argued since the nineteenth century — as speculation, never as proof.

Modern Legacy

Máni's deepest legacy is calendrical: every month and every Monday in the Germanic-speaking world is his, for Mōnandæg and its Norse kin are simply the moon's day, and the words descend from his name. In literature his clearest heir is Tolkien's Tilion, the Maia who steers the vessel of the moon across the sky — the personified moon-driver of the Eddas translated into a new mythology. Notably, Wagner left him out: the Ring runs on gold, fire, and Wotan's will, and the moon-driver never steps onto that stage, a measure of how thin Máni's high-cultural afterlife is beside Þórr's or Óðinn's. The chase around him fares better: the pursuing wolves Skǫll and Hati stalk recent retellings, including God of War Ragnarök (2022), where the stolen moon and its hunters drive a whole arc of the story. And the oldest survival is still the most democratic — the face and figures people point out to children in the full moon, which Snorri already explained by Bil and Hjúki eight centuries ago. In modern heathenry Máni is honored quietly, at the turning of the months he still measures.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Máni in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Máni, Moon, Night, Time, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Máni?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Máni is /ˈmɑːni/ — approximately 'MAH-nee' — hold the first vowel long and open, as in 'father', with the weight squarely on the first syllable and a quick, light '-nee' to close..

02What does Máni mean?

Máni means Moon (the personified moon, brother of Sól) in the norse tradition.

03What are the symbols of Máni?

Máni is associated with Ný ok nið (new and waning moons) (The phases over which Máni rules, the divine charge by which the gods ordained the counting of years for men), Hati Hróðvitnisson (The wolf who runs before the moon and gives its crossing its haste, the promise that even the lights of heaven are hunted), Mánagarmr (Moon-Hound) (The mightiest of the Iron-Wood brood, who fattens on the lives of the dying and swallows the moon at the world's end), Sægr and Simul (pail and pole) (The burden Bil and Hjúki carried from the well Byrgir, still legible as the markings on the moon's face), The month-mark (Mánaðr, the month — the unit of time that bears his name in every Germanic calendar).

04Why restore Máni in Unicode?

Plain ASCII mani strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Máni?

In the Vafþrúðnismál, the giant Vafþrúðnir tells Óðinn that Mundilfari is the father of Máni and of Sól alike. Snorri, in Gylfaginning, gives the story its edge: Mundilfari's two children were so fair that he named his son Máni, 'Moon', and his daughter Sól, 'Sun', and married Sól to a man named Glenr. The gods were angered by this ofdramb — this overweening pride — and took the siblings and set them in the heavens.The punishment is an appointment. Sól is made to drive the horses of the sun, Árvakr and Alsviðr, while Máni is charged with steering the course of the moon and ruling its waxing and waning. What a father claimed in arrogance, the gods confirmed in office: the names became the duties, and the children became the sky.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Máni and related cults.
  • Máni has no secure iconography: no picture stone, runestone, or amulet has been convincingly identified as his image, and his attestation is essentially textual. The Poetic Edda poems that name him — Vafþrúðnismál, Grímnismál, and Vǫluspá — survive chiefly in the Codex Regius (GKS 2365 4to, c. 1270), with Vǫluspá also in the Hauksbók; Snorri's account in Gylfaginning is transmitted by the main Snorra Edda manuscripts (GKS 2367 4to, Codex Upsaliensis, Codex Wormianus). The older strata of Scandinavian sky-religion are, however, material: the Bronze Age Trundholm sun chariot (c. 1400 BC) proves a native tradition of a heavenly body drawn by horse across the sky, and wheel- and disc-marked Gotland picture stones show the sun and moon as enduring cultic signs. The runic record knows the common noun máni and the month-names descended from it, but no runic inscription names the god directly.
