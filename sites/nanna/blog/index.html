The hidden history behind Nanna

Some names in the lexicon need rescuing from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Nanna needs nothing of the kind, and her story needs everything: two scenes in Snorri's Edda, among the shortest dossiers in the whole corpus, and a counter-life in Saxo where she is not Baldr's widow at all but the bride two rivals bleed for. This post walks the full record — name, runes, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the wife who followed her husband into the fire has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII nanna.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Nanna (nanna) — Joy, Wife of Baldr · Mother, daring — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Joy, Wife of Baldr". The name's traditional gloss is "Mother, daring", though its etymology is genuinely disputed.

Nanna, daughter of Nepr, is the wife of Baldr the Good and the mother of Forseti — and the only figure in the Norse corpus who dies of a broken heart. Seeing her husband laid on his pyre aboard the ship Hringhorni, her heart burst with grief, and the Æsir laid her on the fire beside him. In Hel she keeps her place at his side, and when Hermóðr rides down to ransom the dead god, Nanna sends gifts back up the road: a linen robe for Frigg, a gold finger-ring for Fulla.

PuniCodex restores the name as Nanna — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII nanna.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in normalized Old Norse as Nanna, and its etymology is one of the honest open questions of the northern onomasticon. The stronger philological line connects it with Germanic nanþ- 'to dare' — the root behind Old English nēþan and Gothic nanþjan — yielding 'the daring one', a fitting name for a woman who walks unbidden into a funeral fire. The competing reading takes Nanna as a nursery word of the widespread 'mother' type — a Lallname, like Greek nanna 'aunt' — a pet-name elevated to a theonym. De Vries, Cleasby–Vigfusson, Simek and Orchard all register the dispute rather than settle it, and so does this temple: the double gloss 'mother, daring' keeps both lines in view.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᚾᛅᚾᛅ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which the name would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs n-a-n-a.

This is the rare case where the runic row underspecifies nothing: the name carries neither a diacritic nor a consonant the short-twig alphabet writes ambiguously. No Viking-Age carving of the theonym is known — the rendering is a normalized reconstruction, and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Eddas, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈnɑn.nɑ/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: NAHN-nah — two equal beats, the double n held a fraction long in the middle, both vowels short and open, as in modern Icelandic Nanna.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Nanna, daughter of Nepr, wife of Baldr
  • Younger Futhark — ᚾᛅᚾᛅ, the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Latin — Nanna, unchanged; Saxo Grammaticus writes the name exactly as the Icelanders did
  • Modern Scandinavian — Nanna, an unbroken living given name in Danish and Icelandic

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. The a is short and open in both positions, and the geminate nn is held audibly across the syllable boundary — the one articulatory event the spelling actually records.

Mythology

Nanna's mythology is two scenes in Snorri and a second life in Saxo — among the shortest dossiers in the Edda, and among the most devastating.

The Wife of Baldr (Gylfaginning 32)

Snorri names her in the survey of the Æsir's households with genealogical economy: Baldr's wife is Nanna, daughter of Nepr, and their son is Forseti. Of Nepr nothing else is told — the father exists only to place the daughter. The son inherits a hall and an office: Forseti dwells in Glitnir, whose pillars are red gold and whose roof is silver, and stills all strife between gods and men. Grímnismál keeps the same hall in its numbered survey of heaven's dwellings — the family's continuation, held in verse independent of Snorri.

The Heart That Burst (Gylfaginning 49)

When Baldr fell to the mistletoe thrown by blind Höðr at Loki's guidance, the Æsir bore him to the sea and brought out Hringhorni, his ship, the greatest of all ships — launched by the giantess Hyrrokkin when the gods themselves could not move it. On the pyre they laid Baldr, and when Nanna saw him, her heart burst with grief and she died; she was laid on the fire beside him. Óðinn laid the ring Draupnir on the flames; Þórr hallowed the pyre with Mjölnir; Baldr's horse was led to the fire with all its gear. No weapon touches her; no enemy wills her death. In a corpus full of spear-thrusts and poisoned cups, hers is the one death by love alone.

The Gifts from Hel (Gylfaginning 49)

Hermóðr the Bold rides nine nights through dark valleys to ransom Baldr, and in Hel's hall he finds his brother in the high seat — and Nanna beside him, keeping her place in death as in life. Hel sets the famous condition: all things in the world, living and lifeless, must weep Baldr out. When Hermóðr turns for home, the dead send tokens with him. Baldr sends Draupnir back to Óðinn — and Nanna sends a robe of linen to Frigg, her husband's mother, with other gifts, and a gold finger-ring to Fulla. The ransom fails at the giantess Þökk's dry tears — 'let Hel hold what she has' — and Nanna and Baldr remain in Hel together. Even Völuspá's promised return after Ragnarök names Baldr and Höðr reconciled, and does not name her.

Saxo's Counter-Life (Gesta Danorum III)

Saxo Grammaticus gives Nanna the fuller life the Icelanders withheld — and a different husband. His Nanna is the daughter of Gevarus, king of Norway; Høtherus, Gevarus's foster-son, sues for her hand, and she answers with a set speech on the ill match between mortal and demigod. But Balderus, son of Odin, desires her too, and the rivalry becomes war: Høtherus, armed with the sword Mimming won from the satyr Mimingus, defeats the gods' fleet at sea — even Thor's club is cleft — and wins Nanna's hand. Only later does he wound the invulnerable Balderus with the one blade that can harm him. Snorri's Nanna is the wife who dies on her husband's pyre; Saxo's is the bride two rivals bleed for — the same woman read as devotion in Iceland and as desire's prize in Denmark.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Nanna is narrative rather than cultic — the corpus gives her no temple regalia, but fixed around her two scenes a small set of objects no retelling can omit:

  • The linen robe — the gift sent from Hel to Frigg, her husband's mother: domestic love that death does not cancel
  • The gold finger-ring — Fulla's keepsake from the dead: friendship remembered across the boundary, small and exact
  • The pyre of Hringhorni — the fire she entered unslain and unbidden: grief as its own cause of death
  • Glitnir — the hall of red gold and silver where her son Forseti dwells: the household's survival in the world above
  • The high seat in Hel — her place beside Baldr in the hall of the dead: marriage persisting past the grave

Archaeology & Evidence

Nanna is a wholly textual goddess: no runestone invokes her, no votive deposit names her, and no image can be securely identified with her. Her attestation hangs on the manuscript witnesses of the Baldr cycle — the Codex Regius and related codices of Snorri's Edda for Gylfaginning 32 and 49, and the medieval Danish tradition of Saxo's Gesta Danorum for her second life as Gevarus's daughter. The nearest material context is indirect: Úlfr Uggason's Húsdrápa, composed about 983 and preserved in Skáldskaparmál, proves that Baldr's funeral was carved on the panels of Óláfr pái's hall at Hjarðarholt a generation before Iceland's conversion — though the surviving stanzas do not name Nanna herself. The eddic poems that touch her household survive in the Codex Regius: Grímnismál 15 numbers Glitnir among heaven's dwellings.

Realm & Domain

Nanna's domain is Joy, Wife of Baldr — and the pairing is exact: the sources give her no independent office, no hall of her own, no cult; her whole divine identity is the marriage and what survives it.

Joy

The household of Baldr and Nanna is the corpus's one picture of unshadowed divine happiness — the fairest god, his wife, and their son Forseti, whose hall stills all strife. Joy is what the myth establishes so that grief can be measured against it.

Wife of Baldr

Snorri introduces her with genealogical economy — Baldr's wife, Nepr's daughter — and everything the tradition remembers of her flows from the marriage: the death on his pyre, the place beside him in Hel, the gifts sent home to his mother and his household.

Grief

Hers is the only death in the Edda that needs no weapon: seeing Baldr on the pyre, her heart burst. In a corpus of spear-thrusts and poison, the death by love alone is the starkest statement that the gods are not exempt from loss.

Devotion

From Hel, where nothing returns, she answers Hermóðr's errand with household gifts — linen for Frigg, a gold ring for Fulla: devotion keeping its accounts past death's door.

Across Cultures

Nanna's closest kin are inside the corpus itself: she is the divine pattern of which Brynhildr is the heroic echo — the woman who mounts her dead beloved's pyre, as Brynhildr has herself burned beside Sigurðr in the Völsung cycle. Cross-culturally, nineteenth-century scholars reached for the widows of Greek and Sanskrit tradition — Alcestis dying in her husband's place, the debated Indo-European comparisons around the suttee — but current handbooks are cooler: Simek and Orchard treat Nanna as a figure of the Baldr complex whose attestation is too thin for cult reconstruction and whose etymology is too disputed for safe comparison. What can be said cleanly is that no other northern goddess is defined so entirely by a single act of grief, and none answers from the grave with so domestic a grace as a robe and a ring. Within the corpus, her related figures are the household her story binds together: Baldr, Forseti, Frigg, and Fulla.

Cultural Legacy

Nanna's afterlife runs quietly through the Baldr story's long reception: every retelling of the death of the shining god, from the Kearys' Victorian 'Heroes of Asgard' onward, keeps her two scenes — the burst heart on the shore and the gifts entrusted to Hermóðr. Nineteenth-century romanticism made much of the wife on the pyre; twentieth-century scholarship made her the test case for how little the Edda needs to say. Her sturdiest legacy is the name itself: Nanna never died out in the North and remains a living given name in Danish and Icelandic, borne today by women who inherit the most grief-stricken and most faithful name in the Edda. Modern Heathenry remembers her in Baldr rites as the goddess of devotion measured past death's door.

A Meditation

Nanna is the Edda's proof that grief is not a lesser fate than battle. Every other death in the Baldr cycle is negotiated — prophesied, sworn against, tricked, ransomed, avenged. Hers alone simply happens, at the sight of the pyre, and the narrative grants it without comment, as if the gods understood this was a death no oath could have prevented. And then the tradition does something finer still: it lets her act after death. From Hel, where nothing returns, she sends a robe and a ring — not pleas, not prophecy, but the remembered kindnesses of a daughter-in-law and a friend. The ransom fails; the gifts arrive. In a mythology hurtling toward Ragnarök, Nanna is the small, exact consolation: that love keeps its accounts even in the land of the dead, and pays them in linen and gold.

The Unicode Restoration

Nanna is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Norse Nanna carries none of these. The manuscripts write five plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the goddess; it is an honest report about her name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Nanna mean? The traditional gloss is "Mother, daring" — the etymology is genuinely disputed between Germanic nanþ- 'daring, striving' and a nursery word of the 'mother' type. The lexica register both lines without settling the question, and so does this temple.

Which tradition does Nanna belong to? Nanna is catalogued in the Norse pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon.

Why is Nanna classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the goddess's standing is beside the point.

Is nanna.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

How does Nanna die in the sources? In Snorri's Gylfaginning, of grief itself: seeing Baldr's body carried to the pyre on Hringhorni, her heart burst, and she was laid on the fire beside him. Saxo's Gesta Danorum knows no such death — his Nanna outlives the feud and marries Høtherus.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical.
