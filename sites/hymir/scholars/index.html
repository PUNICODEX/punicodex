01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Hymir (hymir) ​— Giant, ‌Owner of Cauldron ‌· the ​Dark One ​of Heaven's End ​— is ​the sea-giant of ​Hymiskviða: the ‌fierce host ​east of the ​Élivágar whose ‌hall stands on ​his herd ‌of cattle, ​whose treasure is ‌the mile-deep ​cauldron Ægir demands ​for the ​brewing of ​the gods' ale, ​and whose ‌fishing-trip with Þórr ​becomes the ‌most famous of ​all the ‌thunder-god's adventures.[1]

​The etymology is ‌uncertain: the ​name is usually ‌connected with ​Old Norse ​hum ‘darkness, dusk’, ‌whence the ‌traditional gloss ‘dark ‌one’. The ​poem makes ​him Týr's father ‌— a ​filiation the corpus ​elsewhere contradicts, ‌since Snorri ​counts Týr among ​Óðinn's sons ‌— and gives ​him the ‌Edda's fullest portrait ​of a ​giant household: ​a loathsome nine-hundred-headed ​grandmother, a ‌golden-browed mother who ‌hides the ​guests under ‌the great kettle, ​three oxen ​for supper, and ‌a crystal ‌cup no ‌pillar can crack.[1][2]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Hymir — ​identical to ​the ASCII input, ​because normalized ‌Old Norse writes ​no diacritic ​in this name. ​The mechanical ‌rule therefore ​places it in ​Tier 2: ​the restoration preserves ‌nothing the ​ASCII form ‌loses, a statement ​about orthography ‌and never about ​the figure. ‌No domain ‌is claimed: the ​plain-ASCII hymir.com ​is registered to ​a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[3]

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Hymir, Týr.
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Hymir, hum.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old ‌Norse as Hymir ​(nominative; genitive ​Hymis). Its ​etymology is genuinely ​uncertain: the ​standard line, ‌registered by Cleasby-Vigfusson ​and carried ​by Simek, ‌connects it ​with Old Norse ‌hum ‘darkness, ​dusk, twilight ‌gloom’, yielding ‘dark ​one, dusky ​one’ — ​a fitting name ​for a ​frost-giant of ‌the outer seas.[1] ​The final ‌-r is ​the Old Norse ‌nominative ending. ​Because the ‌name is pure ​ASCII, the ‌fallback hymir ​loses nothing but ​the capital.

‌The name ‌is not ‌only a theonym-by-episode: ​the metrical ​þulur keep ‌Hymir among the ​ordinary giant-heiti, ​the roster ‌of names skaldic ​poets carried ​for the ‌jötnar, which suggests ​the tradition ‌treated him ​as a giant-type ​as much ​as a ​single character.[2]

The ‌letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • ​h → H ‌— Same, ​capitalized
  • ​y → ‌y — Same

‌- m ‌→ m ‌— Same

  • ​i → ‌i — ​Same
  • r ​→ r ​— Same

‌No domain is ​held for ​this name: ​the plain-ASCII hymir.com ‌belongs to ​a third ​party outside the ​collection and ‌is watched ​for release. The ‌Unicode restoration ​Hymir is ‌classified Tier ​2 because it ‌preserves nothing ‌the ASCII ​form loses — ​the y ​the name ​carries is a ‌letter ASCII ‌shares with ​Old Norse.

Sources

  1. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Hymir, hum; Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Hymir.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈhy.mir/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​H- — ​Voiceless glottal [h], ‌fully sounded ​as in English ​hill
  • ‌-y- — ​Short close front ‌rounded [y], ​the vowel of ‌German ü ​or French ​tu: round the ​lips as ​for ‘oo’ while ​the tongue ‌says ‘ee’; ​one of the ‌Old Norse ​sounds English later ‌unrounded and ​lost
  • ‌-mir — Voiced ​bilabial nasal ‌[m], short [i], ​and the ​final nominative ‌trill [r], lightly ‌sounded after ‌the vowel

For ​the modern ​speaker, the ‌closest approximation is: ​‘HÜ-mir’ — ​stress on the ‌first syllable, ​as in ​every Old Norse ‌word, and ​the final r ‌kept light.

​Kindred and ​historical forms of ​the name:

​- Old Norse ‌— Hymir, ‌the giant ​of Hymiskviða and ‌Gylfaginning

  • ​The þulur — ​Hymir, carried ‌as an ‌ordinary giant-heiti in ‌the skaldic ‌name-lists
  • Skaldic ​tradition — ‌the unnamed ​giant of Bragi's ​Ragnarsdrápa (9th ​c.), Þórr's companion ‌in the ​fishing-boat, conventionally ​identified as Hymir[2]

​- Modern ‌Icelandic — Hymir, ​unchanged in ​spelling

Hymir ​is Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves nothing ‌ASCII loses: ​no diacritic, ‌no distinctive letter, ​no marked ‌vowel length. The ‌tier is ​a report ​on the name's ​orthography, not ​a judgment on ‌the giant's ‌standing in ​the mythology.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology.
  2. Bragi Boddason, Ragnarsdrápa, stanzas preserved in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987); cf. Lindow, Norse Mythology (2001), s.v. Hymir.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the Latin ​alphabet as ​Hymir — ​Old Norse ​(North Germanic), attested ‌13th c. ​CE in ​the Codex ‌Regius of the ​Poetic Edda, ‌recording a ​poem whose ‌matter the ninth-century ​shield-poetry shows ​to be ​centuries older.[1] ​Before the Latin ​bookhand, Old ‌Norse names ​were carved ‌in the younger ​futhark; no ‌runic attestation ​of this ‌name survives, so ​the manuscript ‌form is ​the primary ​witness.

The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ‌step by ‌step:

  • ​The name is ​a simplex, ‌usually connected ​with Old ​Norse hum ‘darkness, ‌dusk’, with ​the nominative ​singular ending ‌-r.
  • In ​the Younger ‌Futhark it ​would have ​been carved ᚼᚢᛘᛁᛦ ​(h-u-m-i-R): the ​ᚢ rune ‌covers the ‌rounded vowel y, ​and ᛦ ‌writes the ​final nominative ​-r.[2]
  • The ‌y is ‌the close ‌front rounded ‌vowel /y/ — ​Old Norse ‌marks it ​with a ​distinct letter, and ​ASCII happens ‌to share ​it, so ​nothing is lost ​in transit.

‌- The ​Unicode restoration ​Hymir is therefore ​identical to ‌the input ​but for ‌the capital; the ​name is ‌Tier 2 ​by the ‌mechanical rule, and ‌the record ​stays honest ​about it: ​no domain form ​of the ‌name is ‌held in ​the collection.

Sources

  1. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014), introduction to Hymiskviða; Bragi, Ragnarsdrápa, in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runes and phonology.
05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Hymir is ​catalogued under ‌Giant, Owner ‌of Cauldron ​— and ​the sources ​earn both ​terms: he is ​the Edda's ‌most fully ​drawn giant ​householder, and ‌his cauldron ​is the ‌poem's whole cause.[1]

​### The ‌Giant

Hymir ​is the ​jötunn at ​the eastern ​edge of heaven, ​east of ‌the Élivágar: ​fierce, shrewd, ‌often cruel ​to guests, ‌descended from ​a nine-hundred-headed ancestress ‌— yet ​a working ‌householder with ​a byre, ​a boat, ‌and fishing-grounds ‌he rows to ‌himself.

​The Sea

​His domain ‌is the ​cold outer ​water where ‌the serpent lies: ​he pulls ​up two ‌whales at ​once, and ‌it is ​on his fishing-ground ​that Þórr ​hooks the ​Miðgarðsormr.

‌The Cauldron

‌The roomy, ​hard-forged kettle ‌‘a mile deep’ ​is the ​only vessel ‌in the ‌world large ‌enough to ‌brew for ​all the Æsir ‌— which ​is why ​the gods ​must come ‌for it, ​and why ​the giant counts ​its loss ‌beside the ​cup's.

​Hospitality and ​Its Price

‌The poem's real ​subject: the ‌host who ​slaughters three ‌oxen for ​his guests ‌and tallies ‌every mouthful, who ​frames the ​wagers himself ​and loses ​both the ‌cup and ‌the cauldron ​on them.

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014); Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 48, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography ​of Hymir ‌is narrative ‌rather than cultic ​— the ​corpus gives ​him no regalia, ​but the ​cauldron-quest fixed ‌a small set ​of objects ​no retelling can ‌omit:[1]

  • ​The mile-deep ‌cauldron — the ​roomy kettle ‌Týr names ​as his father's: ​the vessel ​of plenty ​kept in the ​giant-house until ‌the gods ​carry it off ‌on Þórr's ​head, its ‌rim ringing at ​his heels

‌- The ox-head ​bait — ‌the head ​of the black ​bull Himinbrjótr ‌(Snorri's Himinhrjóðr), ‌torn off for ‌the fishing: ​the herd's ​finest traded for ‌the serpent ​that girdles ​the world

  • ‌The crystal ​cup — ​the treasure no ‌pillar could ​crack, shattered at ‌last on ​the one ​harder thing in ​the hall: ​the giant's ‌own skull, at ‌the whispered ​counsel of ‌Týr's golden-browed mother

​- The ​nine-hundred-headed grandmother ‌— the monstrous ‌ancestry of ‌the house, ‌set in the ​poem's first ‌stanzas against the ​fair counsellor ​who hides ​the guests

  • ‌The two ​whales — ​Hymir's own catch, ​hauled up ‌at once ​on his hooks: ​the measure ​of the ‌giant's strength, dwarfed ​by what ‌rose to ​the ox-head
  • ‌The lame ​goat — one ‌of Þórr's ‌team lamed ​on the road ​home, which ​the poem ​lays at Loki's ‌door: the ‌quest's small ​lasting scar

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014); Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 48, trans. Faulkes (1987).
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Hymir's mythology is a ​single great poem and its prose ‌retelling: Hymiskviða, the lay of the cauldron-quest, ‌and Gylfaginning 48, where Snorri gives ​the fishing-trip its sea-cold detail.[1][2]

The ​Demand for the Cauldron (Hymiskviða 1–13)

​The Æsir had hunted and taken auguries ​from the blood, and saw that Ægir ​— called also Gymir — had ‌brew-stuff in plenty. Þórr ordered him to ​brew ale for the gods; Ægir, ​to be rid of the demand, set ‌an impossible price: first let them ​bring a cauldron a mile wide. Týr ‌knew where one stood: east of ​the Élivágar, at heaven's end, lives the ‌shrewd Hymir — and ‘my fierce ​father owns the roomy kettle, a mile ​deep’. Þórr and Týr drove to the ​hall, met the loathsome nine-hundred-headed grandmother ​and the golden-browed mother, and were hidden ​under the great cauldron, for Hymir ‌was often cruel to guests.[1]

The ​Host Comes Home (Hymiskviða 13–16)

Hymir ‌came in from the hunting with the ​ice rattling in his beard; the ‌pillar his gaze fell on burst apart, ​eight cauldrons crashed from the broken ‌beam, and one hard-forged kettle came down ​whole. The hall fed the guests: three ‌oxen were slaughtered, and Þórr alone ​ate two before he slept. In the ​morning the giant eyed his thinned ‌herd and told his guest they must ‌fish for the next day's meal.[1]

‌### The Fishing for the Serpent (Hymiskviða ​17–27; Gylfaginning 48)

Asked for bait, ​Þórr wrenched the head from the black ‌bull Himinbrjótr. Hymir rowed hard, then ​harder, past his own grounds into water ​he called unsafe, where the serpent lies; ‌he pulled up two whales at ​once, while Þórr cast the ox-head overboard. ​The Miðgarðsormr gaped on the bait, ‌the hook caught, and Þórr braced his ​feet through the bottom of the ‌boat and hauled the serpent's head to ​the gunwale — the two glaring, ​the boat nearly swamped — until the ​hammer struck and the serpent sank ​back into the sea. Hymir rowed home ‌in gloomy silence.[1][2]

The Cup, the ‌Skull, and the Carrying-Off (Hymiskviða 28–39)

​On land Hymir set the proof of ‌strength: break his crystal cup. Twice ​Þórr hurled it through the stone pillars ​and twice it came back unbroken ‌— until the fair counsellor whispered to ‌strike it on Hymir's own skull, ‌harder than any cup. The cup shattered, ‌and Hymir mourned it: he could ​no longer say over it ‘ale is ‌brewed’. Then he yielded the wager — ​take the cauldron, if you can ​carry it out. Týr heaved twice and ​could not shift it; Þórr hoisted ‌it over his head and strode off ​with the rim ringing at his ​heels. Looking back, he saw Hymir coming ​from the caves with a troop ‌of many-headed giants, set down the cauldron, ​and slew the whole host with ​Mjöllnir. One of Þórr's goats went lame ​on the road — Loki's doing, the ‌poem says — but the cauldron ​came to Ægir, who brews in it ‌for the Æsir ever after.[1][2]

​Elsewhere in the Corpus

Beyond the lay, ‌Hymir lives in the name-lists and ​the shield: the þulur keep him among ‌the giant-heiti, and Bragi's ninth-century Ragnarsdrápa ‌paints the fishing-scene — the ox-head bait, ​the hooked serpent, the terrified giant ​at the gunwale — centuries before the ​manuscripts.[3] Within the corpus his closest relations ​are Ægir, whose feast demands the ‌cauldron, Týr, whom the poem makes his ‌son, and Þórr, the guest his ​hall could not digest.

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 48, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Bragi Boddason, Ragnarsdrápa (stanzas in Skáldskaparmál); Skáldskaparmál þulur, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Hymir's poem ​has drawn ‌comparatists for ‌a century ​because the ​cauldron-quest is ​one of ​the old ​travelling stories ‌of the North ​Atlantic: a ​vessel of ‌plenty, kept ​by a ‌grudging power ​beyond the ‌sea, won ​for the ​feast of the ​gods. Scholars ​since Dumézil ​have set ‌Hymiskviða beside ​the Celtic ‌cauldron-quests — ​the Welsh ‌Preiddeu Annwfn, where ​Arthur sails ‌for the ​cauldron of ‌the Head ​of Annwn, ​and the ‌Irish cauldrons ‌that no ‌company leaves unsatisfied ​— and ​beside the ‌Indo-European complex ​of the ​winning of ‌the gods' ​drink.[1]

The ​fishing scene ‌travels its own ​trail: the ‌thunder-god's combat ​with the ​sea-serpent belongs ​with the ​wide dragon-fight ‌pattern, and ‌the Norse ​corpus itself repeats ‌the expedition ​with a ​different host ‌— in ‌Þórsdrápa the ‌giant is ‌Geirröðr, and ​the primordial ‌deep the serpent ​haunts is ​the one ​the gods ‌built from ​Ymir's body. ​Inside the ​corpus the ‌neater tension is ​genealogical: Hymiskviða ​makes Hymir ​Týr's father, ‌while Snorri ​elsewhere counts ‌Týr among ​Óðinn's sons ‌— two ​filiations the tradition ‌never reconciled, ‌and a ​standing exhibit ​in the ​study of ​how Eddic ‌poems and ‌Snorri's systematizing ​prose diverge.[2]

  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Hymir, Týr.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Hymir's ​afterlife ‌is carried ‌almost ​entirely ​by the ​fishing-trip, ​which ​became the ‌most ​retold ​of ‌all Þórr's ​adventures: ‌the ​Kearys' The ‌Heroes ​of ​Asgard ​(1857) and ​every ​handbook ‌since keeps ​the ‌ox-head, ​the glaring ‌serpent, ​and ‌the ​giant rowing ‌home ​in ​silence.[1]

In ‌scholarship ‌the ‌poem ​holds a ​double ‌distinction: ​it is ​the ‌fullest ​portrait of ​a ‌giant ​household ‌in the ​Edda ​— ​the grandmother, ​the ‌golden-browed ‌mother, the ​counted ‌oxen ​— ​and the ‌standing ‌northern ‌exhibit in ‌the ​comparative ‌study ​of the ​cauldron-quest.[2] ​The ‌name itself ​stayed ​where ​the þulur ‌put ​it: ​a ​giant-name, rarely ‌borrowed, ​surfacing ‌in modern ​fantasy ‌and ​games ‌chiefly as ‌the ​surly ​host of ​the ​world's ‌most famous ‌fishing ​story.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Hymir ​is a ‌textual figure: ‌his attestations ​are ​the Codex ​Regius of ​the ​Poetic Edda ‌(c. 1270), ​which alone ​preserves ‌Hymiskviða, and ​the manuscripts ‌of ​Snorri's Edda; ‌the þulur ​keep his ​name ​among the ​giant-heiti.[1] The ​myth's ‌antiquity, however, ​is proven ‌by verse ​and ‌stone. Bragi ​Boddason's Ragnarsdrápa ‌(9th ​c.), composed ‌for a ​painted shield, ​describes ‌the fishing-scene ‌— the ‌ox-head ​bait, the ​hooked serpent, ‌the hammer ​raised ​— with ‌the giant ​aboard ​conventionally identified ‌as Hymir: ​the story ‌is ​centuries older ​than its ​manuscripts.[2]

​The picture-record ‌follows. The ‌Altuna runestone ​(U ‌1161, Uppland) ​shows the ​angler ‌with his ‌foot braced ‌through the ‌boat's ​bottom and ‌the serpent ​on ​the line; ​the Gosforth ‌Cross fishing-panel ​in ​Cumbria shows ​two figures ‌in ​the boat ​— the ​angler and ‌his ​giant companion.[3] ‌No cult ​site, ‌toponym, or ​inscription names ‌Hymir himself: ‌he ​belongs to ​the world ​of ​poetry and ‌the pictures ‌poetry kept ​alive.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Hymiskviða ​— ‘The ‌Lay of ‌Hymir’ — ​is one ​of the ​Codex Regius's ​great comic-heroic ​lays and ‌the giant's ​entire ​poetic dossier. ‌Its construction ​is unusually ‌full: a ​frame at ‌Ægir's hall ​that sets ​the price ​of the ​feast; ​the road-east ‌with Týr's ​tip about ‌his father's ​kettle; the ‌giant-household sketched ​in two ‌women (the ​nine-hundred-headed ‌grandmother, the ​golden-browed mother); ​the homecoming ‌that shatters ‌a pillar ‌and eight ​cauldrons; the ​ox-feast; the ‌rowing duel ​and ​the fishing ‌for the ​Miðgarðsormr; the ​cup broken ‌on the ​giant's skull; ‌and the ​carrying-off of ​the cauldron ​under ​pursuit.[1]

The ‌poem's texture ‌deserves note: ​it is ‌thick with ​dialogue, wager-formulas, ​and household ‌detail — ‌the iced ‌beard, ‌the rattling ​pillars, the ‌cauldron-rim ringing ​at Þórr's ​heels — ​and it ‌weaves in ​other traditions ​at ​the edges, ‌from Loki's ​laming of ​the goat ​to Ægir's ‌by-name Gymir. ​Within the ‌Edda it ​stands as ‌the ​fullest account ‌of Þórr's ‌dealings with ​the giant-world ​and the ​principal source ​for the ‌serpent-fishing alongside ‌the skaldic ​shield-poems.[2]

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014), introduction to Hymiskviða; cf. Lindow, Norse Mythology (2001), s.v. Hymiskviða.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Gylfaginning 48 ​is Snorri's ‌retelling of ‌the cauldron-quest, ​and it ​both follows ​the poem ​and rearranges ​it. Snorri keeps ‌the frame ​— Þórr's ​demand that ‌Ægir brew, ​the impossible ‌price of ​a cauldron ‌a mile wide, ​Týr's knowledge ​of his ​father's kettle ​— and ​the great ‌scenes: the ​two oxen ‌eaten at supper, ​the ox-head ‌bait (Snorri ​names the ‌bull Himinhrjóðr), ​the rowing ‌contest, the ​serpent at ​the gunwale, the ‌cup broken ‌on Hymir's ‌skull at ​the woman's ​whisper, the ‌cauldron carried ​off with ​its rim at ‌Þórr's heels, ​the slaughter ​of the ‌pursuing giants, ​and the ‌lame goat.[1]

​Snorri's own ​additions are characteristic: ​he has ​Þórr carry ‌the boat ‌and the ​whales up ‌from the ​shore as ​a further feat, ‌and he ‌closes by ‌sending the ‌cauldron to ​Ægir, who ‌now brews ​ale in ​it each winter ​— a ‌deliberate stitch ​to the ​Lokasenna, the ​feast at ‌which Loki's ​flyting unfolds. ​Elsewhere the Prose ​Edda keeps ‌Hymir in ​its systematic ‌lists: Skáldskaparmál's ​þulur count ‌his name ​among the ‌jötnar, and the ‌shield-stanzas of ​Bragi's Ragnarsdrápa ​that Snorri ​quotes in ​Skáldskaparmál preserve ‌the fishing-scene ‌from the ​ninth century.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 48, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (Ragnarsdrápa; þulur of the jötnar), trans. Faulkes (1987, 1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​runic inscription ‌names ‌the giant ​Hymir: ​no stone ​invokes ​him, no ​carving carries ‌his ​name, and ​the ‌name does ​not ‌circulate in ​the ‌anthroponymy the ​way ​the gods' ​name-elements ​do.[1]

What ​the ‌runic and ​carved record ‌does ​preserve is ‌his ​myth. The ‌Altuna ​runestone (U ‌1161, ​Uppland, Sweden) ​frames ‌its inscription ‌around ‌a picture ​of ​the serpent-fishing: ‌the angler ​in ​the boat, ‌one ​foot thrust ​through ‌the bottom ​boards ‌as the ​poem ​describes, the ​line ​running down ‌to the ‌coiled ​serpent. The ‌scene ​is the ​standing ‌proof that ‌Hymiskviða's ‌set-piece circulated ‌in ​carving as ‌well ​as verse ​in ​the Viking ‌Age — ​though ​the stone ​names ‌the rune-carver's ​dead, ​not the ​giant.[2] ‌Across the ​sea, ‌the Gosforth ​Cross ‌in Cumbria ​carves ‌the fishing-panel ‌with two ​figures ​in the ​boat: ​the angler ‌and ‌his giant ​companion.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Hymir.
  2. Orchard, Andy, Cassell's Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (Cassell, 1997), s.v. Hymir, on the Altuna and Gosforth scenes.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Hymir's medieval ‌afterlife runs ​through the ​skaldic tradition ​rather than ​the saga ​prose. The ‌oldest witness ​is Bragi ​Boddason's Ragnarsdrápa ‌(9th c.), ​the shield-poem ‌whose surviving ​stanzas — ‌quoted by ​Snorri in ​Skáldskaparmál — ​paint the ​fishing-expedition: the ​ox-head going ‌overboard, the ​serpent gulping ‌the bait, ​the giant ‌aboard shrinking ​in terror. ‌The giant ​is unnamed ‌in the ​surviving verses, ​but the ‌standard scholarly ‌identification is ‌Hymir, which ​would make ​the poem ‌older than ​any manuscript ​of the ‌myth by ​four centuries.[1]

​The pattern ‌the poem ​fixes — ‌Þórr as ​guest in ​a giant's ​hall, tested ​at table, ‌at sea, ‌and in ​feats of ‌strength — ​is the ​one the ‌corpus repeats ‌with other ‌hosts: with ‌Geirröðr in ​Þórsdrápa and ‌its prose ​retelling, where ​there is ​no hospitality ‌at all, ​and in ​the broader ​cycle of ‌Þórr's journeys ​east. Saxo's ​Gesta Danorum, ​which euhemerizes ‌so much ​of the ‌mythology, knows ​the thunderer's ‌giant-contests without ​preserving the ‌Hymir episode ‌as such ​— a ​reminder that ​Hymiskviða and ​Snorri are ‌the story's ‌two real ​homes.[2]

Sources

  1. Bragi Boddason, Ragnarsdrápa, stanzas in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987); cf. Lindow, Norse Mythology (2001), s.v. Hymir.
  2. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979); Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology (1993), s.v. Hymir.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Hymir ​is the ‌Edda's ‌portrait of ​hospitality ​under strain: ​the ​host who ​slaughters ‌two oxen ​for ​his guest ‌and ​counts the ‌cost, ​who rows ‌farther ​than he ​means ​to because ​pride ​will not ‌let ​him ‌ship his ​oar ‌first, who ​frames ‌both wagers ​himself ‌— the ​cup, ​the cauldron ‌— ‌and loses ‌both.[1]

​The poem ​never ‌makes him ​a ​monster. It ‌gives ​him a ​monstrous ‌ancestry and ​a ‌working farm, ​a ​mother who ​counsels ​against him ‌and ‌a treasure ​he ‌genuinely mourns, ​and ​lets the ‌comedy ‌and the ‌cold ‌do the ​work. ‌What the ​story ​measures is ​not ‌his ​failure but ​the ​difference between ‌keeping ​plenty and ​sharing ​it: the ‌cauldron ​that now ‌brews ​for all ‌the ​gods spent ‌its ‌best years ​in ​a hall ​where ​even a ‌guest's ‌supper was ​tallied.

Sources

  1. Hymiskviða (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
