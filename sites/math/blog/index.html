The hidden history behind Math

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Math is the quieter case: a king whose name stands at the head of the masterpiece of medieval Welsh prose, written in the plainest letters the alphabet owns. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, sound, geas, justice, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the lord of Gwynedd has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII math.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Math (math) — Magic, Kingship · Bear — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain “Magic, Kingship”. The name's traditional gloss is “Bear”, from Proto-Celtic *matu-.

Math fab Mathonwy is the lord of Gwynedd in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi — the branch that bears his name. His kingship hangs on the strangest condition in Welsh literature: he cannot live except with his feet in the lap of a virgin, unless the turmoil of war releases him. Around that geas the whole plot turns: Gwydion's engineered war for the swine of Annwfn, the wrong done to the footholder Goewin, the justice of the three animal years, and the making of a bride from flowers.

PuniCodex restores the name as Math — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Middle Welsh writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the king.

The Name

The name is attested in Middle Welsh as Math — the manuscripts write Math uab Mathonwy, “Math son of Mathonwy” — and the standard etymology derives it from Proto-Celtic matu-, “bear”.

The root is well attested in Celtic onomastics. Old Irish knows math, “bear”, chiefly in the compound mathgamain; Cath Maige Tuired names Mathgen as the seer of the Túatha Dé Danann — a name continuing Matu-genos, “bear-born”, a compound the Gaulish inscriptions also attest as Matugenos. The father's name, Mathonwy, is built on the same element: the Fourth Branch's first family is, in effect, a bear-kindred. The handbooks register the bear-derivation as the best-supported reading while noting that no interpretation of the name is beyond dispute.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Middle Welsh manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Math — Math uab Mathonwy in the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest, the two codices in which the Fourth Branch survives.

That matters in the usual way: the manuscript form is the primary witness, and it is already plain ASCII. Middle Welsh orthography writes the voiceless dental fricative as th, exactly as Modern Welsh does; the spelling Math requires no diacritic and no reconstruction. The restoration here is a matter of canonical reference — the fixing of one standard form against the drift of anglicized retellings — not the rescue of letters the keyboard cannot type.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /maːθ/ — Modern Welsh.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: MAATH — a long, open 'ah', held like the a of 'father', closing on the soft voiceless th of English 'thin'. One syllable; the whole weight of the name sits on the length of the vowel and the hush at the end.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Middle Welsh — Math uab Mathonwy, the manuscript form in the White Book and the Red Book
  • Old Irish — math, 'bear', in the compound mathgamain
  • Irish onomastics — Mathgen, 'bear-born', the seer of the Túatha Dé Danann
  • Gaulish — Matugenos, 'bear-born', the epigraphic cognate
  • Attested form — Math, plain ASCII, identical to the manuscript spelling

The vowel is long by the Welsh rule for monosyllables before a single fricative. The th is the voiceless dental fricative [θ], spelled th in Welsh from the medieval period onward.

Mythology

Math's mythology is one story — the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, which bears his name: Math fab Mathonwy.

The Geas of the Footholder

Math son of Mathonwy was lord over Gwynedd, and he could not live except with his feet in the lap of a virgin, unless the turmoil of war prevented him. The footholder was Goewin daughter of Pebin of Dôl Pebin in Arfon, the fairest maiden of her generation; and while Math kept the lap, his sister's sons Gwydion and Gilfaethwy ruled the land in his stead. When Gilfaethwy sickened with love for Goewin, Gwydion read the geas like a statute: the king leaves the lap for war — therefore there will be a war.

The War for the Swine

Pryderi fab Pwyll of Dyfed held the hobeu, the swine sent him from Annwfn by its king Arawn — the first pigs in Wales. Gwydion went south disguised as a poet, conjured twelve stallions with golden harness, twelve greyhounds with golden collars, and twelve golden shields, and traded the day's glamour for the herd. Pryderi mustered and came north, and Math led Gwynedd out to meet him — out of the lap, as the condition required. Pryderi demanded single combat, and at the maenor of Penardd Gwydion slew him “by strength and valour and by magic and enchantment”; Dyfed gave hostages and went home. While Math marched, the brothers forced Goewin in his own chamber. The war had only ever been the key, and the lap the lock.

The Queen and the Three Skins

Goewin told the king what had been done to her, and Math's first act was redress: he took her as his wife and queen, so that the disgrace should not fall on her. Then he judged. He struck Gwydion and Gilfaethwy with his wand into a stag and a hind for a year; when they came back with a fawn, he made them a boar and a sow for the second year, and a wolf and a she-wolf for the third. Each year's offspring — Hyddwn, Hychddwn Hir, and Bleiddwn — he took in beast-shape, restored to human form, baptized, and raised. After the third year he struck the brothers back into men: they had had punishment enough, he said, and been greatly shamed — and he received them back into honour.

The Stepping over the Wand

Asked to name a new footholder, Gwydion named his own sister, Arianrhod daughter of Dôn. Math put the question and set the test — step over the wand — and as she stepped she dropped a sturdy yellow-haired boy, who was baptized Dylan and went straight to the sea and its nature; and as she turned to go she dropped “something small”, which Gwydion hid in a chest at the foot of his bed. From the chest came Lleu Llaw Gyffes, and the branch's second half began.

The Bride Made of Flowers

When Arianrhod's third tynged denied Lleu any wife of the races then on earth, Math joined Gwydion in the one answer left: they took their wands and made a woman from the flowers of the oak, the broom, and the meadowsweet, baptized her Blodeuwedd, and gave her to Lleu. The tale records no death for Math; the branch ends with Lleu avenged and ruling Gwynedd, and the old king withdraws from the record.

Symbols & Iconography

Math's regalia are one tool, one office, and one menagerie — the most juridical attribute-set in the Welsh corpus:

  • The magic wand (hutlath) — the rod that tests Arianrhod, transforms the brothers, and helps weave Blodeuwedd: proof, punishment, and creation in one instrument
  • The footholder's lap — the office at the centre of the geas: the king's feet in a virgin's lap, unless war calls him out
  • Goewin ferch Pebin — the footholder of Dôl Pebin in Arfon, wronged in the king's own house and made his queen in redress
  • The three beast-sons — Hyddwn, Hychddwn Hir, and Bleiddwn: stag, pig, and wolf born of the punished brothers and baptized as men
  • The swine of Annwfn — the otherworld herd whose engineered theft broke the geas
  • Oak, broom, and meadowsweet — the three flowers from which Math and Gwydion made Blodeuwedd

Archaeology & Evidence

Math is a textual figure: no inscription names him, no votive object invokes him, and the pagan past of the Fourth Branch is a literary survival rather than an epigraphic one. His manuscript witnesses are exact: the White Book of Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, NLW Peniarth 4–5, c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, c. 1382–c. 1410), the two great codices of medieval Welsh prose. The epigraphic evidence belongs to the name's root rather than the king: Gaulish personal names such as Matugenos, “bear-born”, attest the matu- element on stone and metal across the continental Celtic world. The tale's geography is walkable terrain — Dôl Pebin in Arfon, the maenor of Penardd in the south, and the Gwynedd of the medieval princes.

Realm & Domain

Math's domain is Magic, Kingship — and the Fourth Branch fuses the two terms so completely that neither can be told without the other.

Kingship

He is lord of Gwynedd, and his kingship is sacral in the strictest sense: his life is bound by a condition, and the office of footholder is a standing institution of his court. While he keeps the lap, his nephews govern; the realm runs on the king's stillness.

Magic

The wand — the hutlath — is his alone. It tests virginity, executes sentences, and creates life. No other figure in the Mabinogi wields so many kinds of power through a single instrument, and Gwydion performs his greatest acts with, or under the licence of, his uncle's craft.

Justice

Between the two domains stands the third thing the tale makes of them: judgment. Math's transformations are not spectacle but sentence — measured in years, fitted to the offence, and closed with full restoration.

Across Cultures

Math's clearest cross-border kinship is institutional rather than personal. Irish tradition knows the taboo-bound king intimately: the gessi of Conaire Mór in Togail Bruidne Da Derga hedge his reign with prohibitions, and the tale is the slow account of their breaking — the same machinery as the footholder geas, a kingship valid only while its condition holds. The name itself crosses the water on its own root: Cath Maige Tuired names Mathgen as the seer of the Túatha Dé Danann, and Gaulish epigraphy attests Matugenos — both “bear-born”, from the matu- that gives Math his name. Mac Cana reads the Children of Dôn as the Welsh counterpart of the Túatha Dé Danann, which would make Math the elder statesman of the Welsh divine race. Within the Mabinogi the root echoes across the branches: Matholwch, the ill-fated Irish king of the Second Branch, carries the same Math- element.

Cultural Legacy

The Fourth Branch rode the twentieth century better than any other part of the Mabinogi, and Math rode with it. Evangeline Walton's The Virgin and the Swine (1936) made the geas and the three animal years the moral engine of a modern novel of old religion and new. Lloyd Alexander's Chronicles of Prydain (1964–68) gave young readers a Math son of Mathonwy as the aged High King of Prydain, the geas laid aside and the grave authority kept. Alan Garner's The Owl Service (1967) drew its haunting from the Blodeuwedd strand that Math's wand set in motion — the branch repeating itself down a Welsh valley's generations. In scholarship he stands at the centre of the Mabinogi industry: Ifor Williams's Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (1930) fixed the text, Sioned Davies's Oxford translation (2007) is the standard English voice of the tale, and the footholder geas remains one of the most-discussed cruxes in Celtic studies.

A Meditation

Math is the proof that a story's most powerful figure can be its stillest. He does not trick, march, or seduce; he sits, and the sitting is the kingship — feet in a maiden's lap, the realm held by nephews, the whole arrangement waiting for someone to notice that war is the one door out of the geas. What makes him great is what he does after the door is used against him: redress before revenge, a wronged girl made queen, a sentence fitted exactly to the crime and limited exactly to it, and then — the rarest thing in any mythology of punishment — restoration without residue. His wand does everything the tale needs: it tests, it transforms, it creates. But its deepest work is juridical. Math is the king whose magic is law and whose law has a measure — three years, three skins, and then the account is closed.

The Unicode Restoration

Math is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Middle Welsh Math carries none of these. The manuscripts write four plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the king; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

The Domain Name

Here the record must be candid.
