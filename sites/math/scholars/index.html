01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Math (math) ​— Magic, Kingship ‌· Bear — ‌is the lord ​of Gwynedd in ​the Fourth Branch ​of the ​Mabinogi, Math fab ​Mathonwy: the king ‌whose life is ​bound by the ​strangest condition in ‌Welsh literature — ​he cannot live ‌except with his ​feet in the ‌lap of a ​virgin, unless the ​turmoil of war ​releases him ​— master of ​the magic wand ‌that tests, punishes, ​and creates, and ‌uncle of Gwydion ​and Gilfaethwy, whose ‌crimes and crafts ​drive the branch.[1]

‌The tale opens ​with his office ‌and its machinery: ​Goewin daughter ​of Pebin of ‌Dôl Pebin in ‌Arfon holds the ‌office of footholder, ​and the nephews ​rule the realm ‌while the king ​keeps the lap. ​Gwydion's war for ‌the swine of ​Annwfn is contrived ​precisely to move ‌Math out ​of the geas; ‌the wrong done ​to Goewin in ​his absence is ​answered by Math ​with redress — ‌he makes her ‌his queen — ​and by the ‌most exact justice ​in the Mabinogi, ​three years of ‌animal transformation ‌for the two ‌offenders.[1] The branch ‌closes its account ​with him silently: ‌no death is ​recorded, and the ​restored Lleu rules ​Gwynedd after.[2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Math — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, ‌because normalized Middle ​Welsh writes no ​diacritic in this ​name. The mechanical ‌rule therefore places ​it in Tier ‌2: nothing distinctive ​is lost in ‌transit, a statement ​about orthography and ‌never about the ‌king. No domain ​is claimed: ​the plain-ASCII math.com ​is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh as ‌Math — ​the manuscripts write ​Math uab Mathonwy, ​'Math son of ​Mathonwy' — and ​the standard etymology ‌derives it ​from Proto-Celtic matu-, ​'bear'.[1] The root ‌is well attested ​in Celtic onomastics: ‌Old Irish knows ​math, 'bear', chiefly ‌in the ​compound mathgamain; the ​Irish name Mathgen ​— borne by ​the seer of ​the Túatha Dé ‌Danann in ​Cath Maige Tuired ‌— continues Matu-genos, ​'bear-born', a compound ‌the Gaulish inscriptions ​also attest.[2][3] The ‌father's name ​Mathonwy is built ‌on the same ​element, so that ​the Fourth Branch's ‌first family is, ‌in effect, a ‌bear-kindred.[4]

The ​etymology matters for ​how the figure ‌is read: a ​king named Bear ​whose rule is ‌hedged by ​taboo sits comfortably ​in the older ‌stratum of Celtic ​sacral kingship, and ‌the handbooks register ​the bear-derivation ​as the best-supported ​reading while noting ​that no interpretation ‌of the name ‌is beyond dispute.[4]

​The ASCII ‌form math loses ​nothing, because the ​Middle Welsh spelling ‌is already plain ‌Latin letters; the ‌restoration is therefore ‌a matter ​of canonical reference, ‌not rescued diacritics, ​and the name ​is classified Tier ​2 by the ‌project's mechanical ​rule.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • ​m → M ‌— Same, capitalized

​- a → ​a — ​Same

  • t ‌→ t — ​Same
  • h ‌→ h — ​Same

The project ‌holds no domain ​for this ‌name: math.com is ‌registered to a ​third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ​for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed pronunciation ‌of the ‌name is ​/maːθ/ — ​Modern Welsh.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Ma- — ‌Long open ​[aː]: Welsh ​monosyllables lengthen ‌the stressed ​vowel before ‌a single ​final fricative ‌— a ​long, open ​'ah'[2]
  • ​-th — ​Voiceless dental ​fricative [θ], ‌the sound ​of English ‌thin, spelled ​th in ‌Welsh from ​the medieval ‌period onward[1]

​For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest ​approximation is: ‌'MAATH' — ‌one syllable, ‌the length ​on the ​vowel, the ‌hush on ​the end.

​Kindred and ‌historical forms ​of the ​name:

  • ‌Middle Welsh ​— Math ‌uab Mathonwy, ​the manuscript ​form in ​the White ​Book and ‌the Red ‌Book
  • ​Old Irish ‌— math, ​'bear', in ​the compound ‌mathgamain
  • ‌Irish onomastics ‌— Mathgen, ‌'bear-born', the ​seer of ‌the Túatha ​Dé Danann

​- Gaulish ​— Matugenos, ‌'bear-born', the ​epigraphic cognate[3]
​- Attested ​form — ‌Math, plain ​ASCII, identical ​to the ​manuscript spelling

‌Math is ​Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves ‌nothing ASCII ​loses: the ‌letters of ‌the manuscript ​form are ​the letters ​of the ​keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in Middle ‌Welsh manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Math — ​Math uab Mathonwy ​in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch and ​the Red ‌Book of ​Hergest, the two ‌codices in ​which the ‌Fourth Branch survives.[1] ​Middle Welsh ​orthography writes ​the voiceless ​dental fricative as ​th, exactly ‌as Modern ​Welsh does; the ‌spelling Math ​requires no ‌diacritic and no ​reconstruction.[2]

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • ​Middle Welsh ​was written ‌in the Latin ‌alphabet from ‌the earliest ​prose; the White ​Book and ‌Red Book ​hands write Math ​with the ‌plain letters ​M-a-t-h.
  • The ​vowel is ‌long by ​the Welsh ‌rule for monosyllables ​before a ​single fricative, ​giving Modern Welsh ​/maːθ/; the ‌th is ‌[θ], the sound ​of English ‌thin.[2]
  • ​The etymological dictionaries ​derive the ‌name from ‌Proto-Celtic *matu- ‌'bear', with Old ‌Irish math ​and Gaulish ‌Matugenos as the ​comparative witnesses.[3]

​- The ​Unicode restoration Math ‌is therefore ​identical to ​the ASCII form ​apart from ‌the capital; ​the temple ​form is a ​canonical reference, ‌not a ​rescued spelling.

One ‌caution attaches ​to the ‌record: the plain-ASCII ​math.com is ‌held outside ‌the collection, so ​no domain ​form of ​the name ​is owned; the ‌manuscript spelling ‌remains the ​primary witness.[4]

05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Math is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌'Magic, Kingship' — ​and the ​Fourth Branch fuses ​the two ​terms so ​completely that neither ‌can be ​told without the ​other.[1]

‌Kingship

He ​is lord of ‌Gwynedd, and ​his kingship is ‌sacral in ​the strictest ​sense: his life ​is bound ​by a condition ​— feet ‌in a ​virgin's lap, unless ‌war releases ​him — and ‌the office ​of footholder ‌is a standing ​institution of ‌his court. While ​he keeps ​the lap, ‌his nephews govern; ‌the realm ‌runs on the ​king's stillness.[1] ​The geas ‌places him in ​the pan-Celtic ​dossier of the ‌taboo-bound ruler, ​the figure ​Irish tradition knows ‌through the ​gessi of kings.[2]

‌### Magic

​The wand ​— the hutlath ​— is ​his alone. It ‌tests virginity ‌(Arianrhod's stepping), ​executes sentences (the ‌three animal ​years), and creates ​life (Blodeuwedd ‌from the ‌flowers). No other ‌figure in ‌the Mabinogi wields ​so many ‌kinds of ​power through a ​single instrument, ​and Gwydion — ‌the branch's ​working enchanter ​— performs his ​greatest acts ‌with, or under ​the licence ​of, his ​uncle's craft.[1]

‌Justice

Between ​the two domains ‌stands the ​third thing ‌the tale makes ​of them: ‌judgment. Math's transformations ‌are not ​spectacle but ​sentence — measured ​in years, ​fitted to the ‌offence, and ‌closed with ​full restoration.[1]

Sources

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), Fourth Branch.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Math's ​regalia are ‌one tool, ‌one office, ​and ​one menagerie ​— the ​most juridical ​attribute-set ‌in the ​Welsh corpus:[1]

​- The ‌magic ​wand (hutlath) ‌— The ​rod that ‌tests ​Arianrhod, transforms ​the brothers, ​and helps ​weave ​Blodeuwedd: proof, ‌punishment, and ​creation in ‌one ​instrument

  • ‌The footholder's ​lap — ‌The ​office at ‌the centre ​of the ​geas: ‌the king's ‌feet in ‌a virgin's ​lap, ​unless war ‌calls him ​out
  • ​Goewin ‌ferch Pebin ​— The ​footholder of ‌Dôl ​Pebin in ‌Arfon, wronged ​in the ​king's ​own house ​and made ‌his queen ‌in ​redress
  • ‌The three ​beast-sons — ​Hyddwn, ‌Hychddwn Hir, ‌and Bleiddwn ‌— stag, ‌pig, ​and wolf ‌born of ​the punished ​brothers ​and baptized ‌as men

​- The ​swine ​of Annwfn ‌— The ​otherworld herd ​whose ​engineered theft ‌broke the ​geas: the ‌war ​was always ‌about the ​lap

  • ‌Oak, ‌broom, and ​meadowsweet — ​The three ​flowers ​from which ‌Math and ‌Gwydion made ​Blodeuwedd

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Math's mythology is one ​story — the Fourth Branch of ‌the Mabinogi, which bears his name: ‌Math fab Mathonwy.[1]

The Geas ​of the Footholder

Math son of Mathonwy ​was lord over Gwynedd, and he ​could not live except with his ​feet in the lap of a ​virgin, unless the turmoil of war prevented ‌him. The footholder was Goewin daughter ​of Pebin of Dôl Pebin in ​Arfon, the fairest maiden of her ‌generation; and while Math kept the lap, ​his sister's sons Gwydion and Gilfaethwy ‌ruled the land in his stead. ​When Gilfaethwy sickened with love for ‌Goewin, Gwydion read the geas like a ​statute: the king leaves the lap ​for war — therefore there will ​be a war.[1]

The War ​for the Swine

Pryderi fab Pwyll of ​Dyfed held the hobeu, the swine ‌sent him from Annwfn by its ​king Arawn — the first pigs ‌in Wales. Gwydion went south disguised as ​a poet, conjured twelve stallions with ‌golden harness, twelve greyhounds with golden ​collars, and twelve golden shields, and ‌traded the day's glamour for the herd. ​Pryderi mustered and came north, and ‌Math led Gwynedd out to meet ​him — out of the lap, ​as the condition required. Pryderi demanded single ‌combat, and at the maenor of ‌Penardd Gwydion slew him 'by strength ‌and valour and by magic and ​enchantment'; Dyfed gave hostages and went home. ​While Math marched, the brothers forced ‌Goewin in his own chamber.[1][2]

​The Queen and the Three Skins

​Goewin told the king what had been ‌done to her, and Math's first ​act was redress: he took her ​as his wife and queen, so ‌that the disgrace should not fall on ​her. Then he judged. He struck ‌Gwydion and Gilfaethwy with his wand ​into a stag and a hind ​for a year; when they came back ​with a fawn, he made them ​a boar and a sow for ‌the second year, and a wolf ‌and a she-wolf for the third. Each ​year's offspring — Hyddwn, Hychddwn Hir, ‌and Bleiddwn — he took in ​beast-shape, restored to human form, baptized, ​and raised. After the third year he ‌struck the brothers back into men: ‌they had had punishment enough, he ‌said, and been greatly shamed — ‌and he received them back into honour.[1]

​### The Stepping over the Wand

‌Asked to name a new footholder, ​Gwydion named his own sister, Arianrhod ​daughter of Dôn. Math put the question ​and set the test — step ‌over the wand — and as ​she stepped she dropped a sturdy ​yellow-haired boy, who was baptized Dylan and ​went straight to the sea and ‌its nature; and as she turned ​to go she dropped 'something small', ​which Gwydion hid in a chest at ​the foot of his bed. From ‌the chest came Lleu Llaw Gyffes, ​and the branch's second half began.[1][2]

‌### The Bride Made of Flowers

When ​Arianrhod's third tynged denied Lleu any ‌wife of the races then on ​earth, Math joined Gwydion in the ‌one answer left: they took their wands ‌and made a woman from the ​flowers of the oak, the broom, ​and the meadowsweet, baptized her Blodeuwedd, ​and gave her to Lleu. The tale ​records no death for Math; the ‌branch ends with Lleu avenged and ‌ruling Gwynedd, and the old king ​withdraws from the record.[1][3]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Math's clearest ​cross-border kinship ‌is institutional ‌rather than ​personal. Irish ​tradition knows ​the taboo-bound ​king intimately: ​the gessi ‌of Conaire ​Mór in ​Togail Bruidne ‌Da Derga ​hedge his ‌reign with ​prohibitions, and ‌the tale ​is the ​slow account of ​their breaking ​— the ​same machinery ‌as the ​footholder geas, ‌a kingship ​valid only ‌while its ​condition holds.[1] ‌The name ​itself crosses ‌the water ​on its ​own root: ‌Cath Maige ‌Tuired names ‌Mathgen as the ​seer of ​the Túatha ‌Dé Danann, ​and Gaulish ​epigraphy attests ‌Matugenos — ​both 'bear-born', ​from the ‌matu- that ​gives Math ‌his name.[2] ​Mac Cana ​reads the ​Children of ​Dôn as ‌the Welsh ‌counterpart of ​the Túatha Dé ‌Danann, which ​would make ​Math the ‌elder statesman ‌of the ‌Welsh divine ‌race.[3]

Within ​the Mabinogi ‌the root ​echoes across ​the branches: ​Matholwch, the ‌ill-fated Irish ​king of ​the Second ​Branch, carries ‌the same Math- ​element. His ​household ties ​are the ‌branch's own: ​his sister ‌Dôn's children ​— Gwydion, ‌Gilfaethwy, Arianrhod ​— supply ‌his officers, ‌his criminals, ​and his ​successor, and ​the co-made ​bride Blodeuwedd ‌is his ‌one act ​of creation.[4]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

The Fourth ​Branch rode ‌the twentieth ‌century better ​than any ​other part ​of the ​Mabinogi, and ​Math rode ‌with it. ​Evangeline Walton's ​The Virgin ‌and the ​Swine (1936) ‌— her ​retelling of ‌Math fab ​Mathonwy — ​made the ​geas and ​the three ​animal years ‌the moral ​engine of ‌a modern ​novel of ‌old religion ​and new.[1] ‌Lloyd Alexander's ​Chronicles of ‌Prydain (1964–68) ​gave young ​readers a ‌Math son ‌of Mathonwy ‌as the ​aged High ​King of ‌Prydain, the ​geas laid ​aside and ‌the grave ​authority kept.[2] ​Alan Garner's ‌The Owl Service ​(1967) drew ‌its haunting ​from the ​Blodeuwedd strand ​that Math's ​wand set ‌in motion ‌— the ​branch repeating ‌itself down ​a Welsh ​valley's generations.[3]

‌In scholarship ‌he stands ‌at the ‌centre of ​the Mabinogi ‌industry: Ifor ​Williams's Pedeir ​Keinc y ​Mabinogi (1930) ‌fixed the ​text on ​which all ​modern work ‌rests, Sioned ​Davies's Oxford ​translation (2007) ​is the ‌standard English ​voice of ‌the tale, ​and the ‌footholder geas ​remains one ‌of the ‌most-discussed cruxes ​in Celtic ​studies — ​the point ​where kingship ‌ideology, taboo, ‌and narrative ​machinery meet.[4]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Math ​is a ‌textual figure: ‌no ​inscription names ​him, no ​votive ​object invokes ​him, and ‌the ​pagan past ​of the ‌Fourth ​Branch is ‌a literary ​survival ‌rather than ​an epigraphic ​one.[1] ​His manuscript ​witnesses are ​exact: ‌the Fourth ​Branch ‌survives in ​the White ‌Book ​of Rhydderch ‌(Aberystwyth, NLW ​Peniarth ‌4–5, c. ​1350) and ​the ‌Red Book ‌of Hergest ‌(Oxford, ​Jesus College ​MS 111, ‌c. ​1382–c. 1410), ​the two ‌great ​codices of ​medieval Welsh ‌prose.[2]

​The epigraphic ‌evidence belongs ​to ​the name's ​root rather ​than ‌the king: ‌Gaulish personal ​names ‌such as ​Matugenos, 'bear-born', ​attest ‌the matu- ‌element on ‌stone ‌and metal ​across the ‌continental ​Celtic world.[3] ​The tale's ​geography ‌is walkable ​terrain ​— Dôl ​Pebin in ‌Arfon, ​Goewin's home; ​the maenor ​of ‌Penardd in ​the south, ‌where ​Pryderi fell; ‌and the ​Gwynedd ‌of the ‌medieval princes, ​whose ​later court ​culture preserved ​the ‌branch that ‌bears Math's ​name.[4]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Math belongs ​to the ‌Welsh corpus, ‌not the Irish ​cycles, but ​the Irish ​material frames him ​twice over. ​The first ‌frame is institutional: ​the geas ​of the ‌footholder is the ​Welsh reflex ‌of the ​geis, the taboo ‌that Irish ​literature attaches ​above all to ​kings. Its ​fullest dossier ​is Togail Bruidne ‌Da Derga, ​where the ‌gessi of Conaire ​Mór hedge ‌his reign ​with prohibitions — ‌he may ​not go ‌righthandwise around Tara ​nor lefthandwise ​around Brega, ‌may not hunt ‌the evil ‌beasts of ​Cerna, may not ​settle a ‌quarrel between ​two of his ​own thralls ‌— and ​the tale is ​the slow, ‌fated account ​of their ‌breaking. Math's condition ​runs on ​the same ​machinery: a kingship ​valid only ‌while its ‌prohibition holds, and ​a plot ‌that exists ​to spring it.[1]

​The second ‌frame is ‌onomastic. Old Irish ‌knows the ‌word math, ​'bear', chiefly in ‌the compound ​mathgamain; and ​Cath Maige Tuired ​names Mathgen ‌as the ​seer of the ​Túatha Dé ​Danann — ‌a name continuing ​Matu-genos, 'bear-born', ​the same ​root that gives ‌Math and ​Mathonwy their ‌names.[2][3] No Irish ​narrative counterpart ‌of Math ​himself has been ‌established, and ‌the modern ​handbooks do not ​propose one; ​the kinship ​is of institution ‌and of ‌name, not ​of story.[4]

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Math's whole ​dossier is ‌the Fourth ‌Branch of the ​Mabinogi, Math ​fab Mathonwy ​— the tale ​bears his ​name, and the ‌Welsh corpus ​gives him ​no second story. ‌The branch ​survives in ‌the two great ​codices, the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch and ​the Red ​Book of Hergest, ​and Ifor ​Williams's Pedeir ‌Keinc y Mabinogi ​remains the ‌standard edition.[1] ​The tale presents ‌him as ​lord of ‌Gwynedd of the ​line of ‌Mathonwy; the medieval ​genealogies make ​Dôn a ‌daughter of Mathonwy, ‌which is ‌why Gwydion, ​Gilfaethwy, and Arianrhod ​are the ‌king's sister's ​children, as the ​branch itself ‌calls the brothers.[2]

​Around the ​branch runs ‌the wider world ​of the ‌Four Branches: ​the swine at ​the heart ​of the ​plot came from ‌Arawn of ‌Annwfn by way ​of the ‌First Branch's ​friendship with Pwyll, ​and Pryderi ‌— their ‌owner and Gwydion's ‌victim — ‌is the ​First and Third ‌Branches' own ​son, child of ​Rhiannon.[3] The ​Second Branch ‌supplies the echo ​of his ​name in ​Matholwch king of ‌Ireland. Beyond ​the Mabinogi ​the record thins ​quickly: later ‌Welsh tradition remembers ​his house ‌— the ​Children of Dôn, ‌Dylan of ​the sea, ‌the flower-wife — ‌more than ​the old ​king himself, and ​no tale, ​saint's life, or ‌chronicle gives ‌him a ​further adventure.[4]

Sources

  1. Williams, Ifor, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi (Caerdydd: Gwasg Prifysgol Cymru, 1930).

  3. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), Fourth Branch.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Math. ‌The early ​medieval ​Welsh epigraphic ​record ​— some ​hundreds ‌of inscribed ​stones ​in Latin ‌and ogham ​— ‌preserves personal ​names ‌and formulae, ​not ​theonyms, and ​no ​reading of ​Math ‌or Mathonwy ​has ‌been proposed ​for ‌any of ​them; ‌the pagan ​past ‌of the ​Fourth ​Branch is ‌a literary ‌survival, ‌not an ​epigraphic ​one.[1]

The ‌epigraphy ​does witness ​the ‌name's root. ​Gaulish ​personal names ‌in ​Matu- — ‌above ​all Matugenos, ​'bear-born', ​attested in ​the ‌inscriptions of ‌the ​continental Celtic ‌world ​— stand ​on stone ‌what ‌the Welsh ‌manuscripts ‌carry on ​vellum: ‌the same ​matu-, ​'bear', that ​names ‌the king ​and ​his father.[2] ​The ‌comparison is ​onomastic, ​not cultic ​— ‌no altar ​was ‌ever dedicated ​to Math ‌— ​but it ‌anchors ‌the Fourth ​Branch's ​first family ​in ​the deep ‌stratum ‌of Celtic ​naming.[2]

Sources

  1. Edwards, Nancy, A Corpus of Early Medieval Inscribed Stones and Stone Sculpture in Wales, 3 vols. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 2007–13).
  2. Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise, 2nd ed. (Paris: Errance, 2003), s.v. matu-.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Math ​is the ‌proof that ‌a story's ​most powerful ​figure can ​be its ​stillest. He ​does not ‌trick, march, ​or seduce; ​he sits, ‌and the ​sitting is ‌the kingship ​— feet ‌in a ​maiden's lap, ​the realm ​held by ​nephews, the ​whole arrangement ‌waiting for ​someone to ‌notice that ​war is ‌the one ​door out ‌of the ​geas.[1] What ‌makes him ​great is ​what he ‌does after ‌the door ‌is used ​against him: ​redress before ‌revenge, a ​wronged girl ​made queen, ‌a sentence ​fitted exactly ​to the ‌crime and ​limited exactly ‌to it, ​and then ​— the ​rarest thing ​in any ‌mythology of ‌punishment — ​restoration without ‌residue.

His ​wand does ​everything the ‌tale needs: ‌it tests, ‌it transforms, ‌it creates. ​But its ‌deepest work ​is juridical. ​Math is ​the king ‌whose magic ​is law ​and whose ​law has ‌a measure ​— three ​years, three ​skins, and ‌then the ​account is ‌closed. In ​a corpus ‌full of ​geases broken ‌and fates ‌fulfilled, he ​is the ​one ruler ​whose condition, ​once broken, ‌teaches him ‌to govern ​the breaking.[2]

  1. Davies, Sioned, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), Fourth Branch.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
