01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Bodhidharma (bodhidharma) — The Dharma of Awakening, First Patriarch · Dharma of awakening — belongs to the Chinese tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Zen Patriarch, Wall-Gazer". The name means "Dharma of awakening"[1].

Bodhidharma is the First Patriarch of Zen: the Indian prince-turned-monk who crossed the Pamirs to China around 520 CE, sat facing a wall at Shaolin for nine years, and transmitted the Buddha’s wordless teaching outside the scriptures. His legend is the bridge between India and East Asia — and the seed of martial, meditative, and cultural traditions that span the continent.[2]

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˌboʊdɪˈdɑːrmə/ — Sanskrit → Chinese transmission (Pútídámó).[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Bodhi- — From Sanskrit bodhi, “awakening” — the name begins in the Buddha’s own enlightenment-word.
  • -dhar- — From √dhṛ, “to hold, sustain” — the same root as dharma’s “that which holds”.
  • -ma — The Chinese rendered it 菩提達磨 (Pútídámó); the Japanese, Daruma; the West, Bodhidharma.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: “boh-dee-DAR-muh”

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Chinese — Pútídámó 菩提達磨 — the First Patriarch
  • Japanese — Daruma 達磨 — the roly-poly doll is his image

The name means “awakening-perfection” or “dharma of awakening” — the Indian prince who carried Zen to China.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Bodhidharma’s mythology is a layer-cake of history and legend: the historical missionary of the early sixth century; the wall-gazer of the Zen records; the Shaolin temple’s patron of the martial arts; and Japan’s round Daruma doll, whose blank eyes you fill in when your wish comes true. The legend grew because the teaching needed a body — and the body it chose was defiant, foreign, and perfectly still.[1]

The Crossing to China (Transmission Records)

The Zen records send him to the court of the Liang emperor Wudi — the great Buddhist patron who asks, ‘what is the highest meaning of the holy truth?’ and is told ‘vast emptiness, nothing holy’. The emperor, scandalized, asks who stands before him: ‘Don’t know’, says the future patriarch, and leaves for the north. The dialogue founded the Zen interview-style in a single exchange.[2]

Nine Years, One Wall (Wall-Gazing Legend)

At Shaolin he sat facing a wall nine years; legend adds that in frustration at sleep he cut off his eyelids — and tea, the meditator’s ally, sprang from where they fell. Huike, his future successor, stood in the snow and cut off his own arm to prove his sincerity; Bodhidharma accepted him and transmitted the teaching. The tradition’s founding images are all of this order: resolve measured in what one will lose.

The Temple and the Arts (Shaolin Cycle)

The legend that he taught the Shaolin monks exercises to strengthen their meditation — the seed of Shaolin kung fu — belongs to the later layers, but it fixed his place in the martial imagination: the first patriarch as the first master of body-discipline. The Yi Jin Jing muscle-classic attaches itself to his name.

The Round Wish-Keeper (Daruma Doll)

Japan made him a doll: round as the wall-sitter’s robe, weighted to right itself — nanakorobi yaoki, fall seven times, rise eight. You paint one blank eye when you make a wish, the other when it comes true. The fierce Indian master ended as Japan’s most cheerful household god of perseverance, keeping his scowl and his self-righting bottom.

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Bodhidharma is the Silk Road made flesh: Indian dharma, Chinese wall, Japanese doll. The Zen lineages count him as the twenty-eighth Indian and first Chinese patriarch — the hinge of the transmission; and the Tibetan traditions remember him (as Bodhidharmottara) among the great siddhas. His defiance of the Liang emperor became the template of the Zen master’s whole pedagogy.[1]

Within the Chinese tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Bāguà, Cáishén, Chángé, Fúxī, Guāndì, and Guānyīn.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

He stands at the head of every Zen lineage — Rinzai and Sōtō alike trace the wall; the Shaolin temple’s global fame rests on his legend; and the Daruma doll, with its self-righting scowl, is Japan’s most reproduced folk object. ‘Zen’ as the West knows it begins, by the tradition’s own account, with his ‘don’t know’.[1]
