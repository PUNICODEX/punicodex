The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Pryderi

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Pryderi is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Care, anxiety”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

pryderi

The plain pryderi form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Pryderi. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Pryderi was spoken

/prəˈdɛr.i/ Modern Welsh
Pr- Voiceless bilabial stop [p] released directly into a trilled [r] — a clean initial cluster with no vowel between, as in English 'prince'.
-y- The Welsh obscure vowel [ə], the 'clear y' of non-final syllables — a soft central 'uh', never the [i] of the final position.
-der- Voiced [d] and open-mid [ɛ] with a tapped [r], carrying the stress: Welsh words are stressed on the penultimate syllable, so the weight of the name lands here.
-i Short close [i], the unstressed final vowel, closing quietly.

Modern Approximation

'pruh-DEH-ree' — the y is a soft 'uh', the stress falls on 'der' (the Welsh penult), and the final i is a short 'ee'.

Etymological Kin

  • Middle Welsh Pryderi uab Pwyll, son of Pwyll Pen Annwn and Rhiannon, lord of Dyfed in the Four Branches of the Mabinogi.
  • Foster-name Gwri Wallt Euryn, 'Gwri of the Golden Hair' — the name Teyrnon Twrf Liant's household gave the foundling before his true name was known.
  • Welsh common noun pryder, 'care, anxiety, worry' — the living word the First Branch names him from: Rhiannon's own pryder, lifted at his return.
  • Comparative reflex Mabon ap Modron, the Welsh divine son stolen from his mother, and Gaulish Maponos — the figure-type Pryderi is read against; a parallel, not a cognate of the name.

The Accent / Script Rule

Pryderi is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the Middle Welsh form is already plain ASCII — there is no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the restoration to recover, and only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input. The name's etymology is the tale's own: the First Branch names him from Rhiannon's word pryder, 'care, anxiety', spoken when the lost child was restored — an aetiological naming the medieval author builds into the story itself, and the lexicon's gloss 'Care, anxiety' follows the text.

05

The Lord Who Spans the Four Branches

Son of Pwyll, King

Pryderi is the hinge of the whole Mabinogi: the only figure who walks through all Four Branches — stolen on the night of his birth and found outside a stable door, named from his mother's lifted anxiety, emptied out of Dyfed by an enchanter's mist, and killed at last in single combat by Gwydion's craft. Son of Pwyll and Rhiannon, lord of Dyfed, he is the king the tales keep taking things from — and the name he carries is the Welsh word for care.

The Stable Door

Outside Teyrnon's stable, where a great clawed hand had come for the newborn foal, lay an infant wrapped in silk brocade — the stolen heir of Dyfed.

The Naming

Rhiannon looks at the boy and says her pryder — her care, her anxiety — is lifted from her; from that word the child is named Pryderi.

The Golden Bowl

Hanging by chains above a marble fountain in a fortress that was never there before: when Pryderi grasps it, his hands stick, his feet root to the slab, and his voice dies.

The First Swine

The hobeu, first pigs ever brought to Wales — Arawn of Annwn's gift to the house of Pwyll — which Gwydion wins with illusory gold and pays for with Pryderi's life.

Sacred Symbols

The silk brocade wrapping Royal blood declaring itself at a stable door — the foundling child whose swaddling says what no witness can
The clawed hand The Otherworld reaching through the window on May-eve — the unseen power that takes foals and princes alike
The golden bowl on its chains Bait that looks like treasure — the trap at the heart of the enchanted fortress, and the cost of touching what is not yours
The gate-hammer about his neck What he wore out of the enchantment — the humiliation of the Third Branch, borne home and buried in silence
The swine of Annwn Otherworld wealth with a geis upon it — not to be sold or given until the herd had doubled — and the war a broken promise bought
The grave at Maen Tywynog The mound above Y Felenrhyd where the slain lord was buried — the one fixed point of a life lived under enchantment
06

Mythology

Stories of Pryderi

Pryderi's mythology is unique in Welsh prose: he is the only character who appears in all Four Branches of the Mabinogi, and his life is the thread the whole cycle is strung on. The First Branch gives his birth, theft, and naming; the Second carries him through the Irish war as one of the seven survivors; the Third empties his kingdom and takes him captive; the Fourth kills him. Every word of it is medieval Welsh prose, preserved in two great manuscripts.

First Branch · Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed

The May-Eve Vanishing

Pryderi is born to Pwyll and Rhiannon after the nobles of Dyfed have pressed for an heir, and on the night of his birth the six women set to watch all fall asleep — and the child is gone. Terrified of the blame, the women kill the pups of a hound, smear the blood on Rhiannon's face, lay the bones beside her, and swear she has destroyed her own son; her penance at the horse-block begins, and it will last until the boy comes home. The child, meanwhile, has fallen into the strangest rescue in Welsh prose. Teyrnon Twrf Liant, a lord of Gwent Is Coed by the sea, loses a foal every May-eve; keeping watch over his mare, he sees a great clawed hand reach through the window to seize the newborn foal, and strikes it off with his sword. Outside the door he finds a baby boy wrapped in silk brocade. He and his wife rear the child as their own and call him Gwri Wallt Euryn — Gwri of the Golden Hair.

First Branch · Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed

The Naming at Arberth

Gwri grows with uncanny speed — at the end of his first year sturdier than a child of three, at two like a boy of six — and year by year he bears the very likeness of Pwyll. When Teyrnon hears of Rhiannon's penance, he knows what he must do, and he and his wife bring the boy to Arberth. The court sees the likeness at once, and Rhiannon says that if this is true, her pryder — her care, her anxiety — is lifted from her. Pwyll declares that the boy should take his name from the word his mother spoke when the news was brought to her, and so the child is named Pryderi. The penance ends; Teyrnon will take no reward beyond the boy's friendship; and Pryderi is fostered with Pendaran Dyfed until he is of age, when Pwyll gives him the realm.

Second Branch · Branwen ferch Llŷr

One of the Seven

When Bendigeidfran — Brân the Blessed — leads the host of Britain into Ireland to answer the wrong done to Branwen, the war ends in near-total ruin: the Irish are destroyed, and of the host of the Island of the Mighty only seven men come back alive, bearing Brân's wounded head. The tale names them, and the first two it names are Pryderi and Manawydan, with Glifieu, Taliesin, Ynawg, Gruddieu, and Heilyn. With the others Pryderi feasts seven years at Harlech, where the Birds of Rhiannon sing, and eighty years at Gwales in Penfro in the Assembly of the Wondrous Head — a man who has sailed out of his own branch into the middle of someone else's tragedy, and who carries its grief home into the Third.

Third Branch · Manawydan fab Llŷr

The Enchantment of Dyfed

After Pwyll's death, Pryderi rules Dyfed and gives his mother Rhiannon in marriage to Manawydan fab Llŷr; with his wife Cigfa, the four come home to Arberth. There comes a peal of thunder and a fall of mist, and when it lifts, Dyfed is emptied — not a person, not a dwelling, not a beast remains. For two years the four live by hunting; then they try saddlery, shield-making, and shoemaking in the towns of England, and each time the local craftsmen drive them out for making work too good to allow. Back in empty Dyfed, a shining white boar leads Pryderi and Manawydan to a fortress that was never there before. Against Manawydan's counsel Pryderi goes in, lays hold of a golden bowl hanging by chains above a marble fountain — and his hands stick to the bowl, his feet to the slab, and speech leaves him. Rhiannon goes in after him, takes hold of the same bowl, and is caught beside him; fort, bowl, and both prisoners vanish.

Manawydan undoes the spell by patience: sowing wheat, he catches the one slow mouse stripping his third ripe field, condemns it to hang as a thief, and names his price to the bishop who comes to beg it off — Pryderi and Rhiannon released, the enchantment lifted from Dyfed, and no vengeance taken for it. The mouse is the wife of Llwyd fab Cilcoed, who had laid the spell to avenge his friend Gwawl fab Clud, the suitor Rhiannon refused. Pryderi comes home with a gate-hammer about his neck, and Rhiannon with the collars of asses about hers; the land wakes, and the Third Branch ends with the four of them feasting as they began.

Fourth Branch · Math fab Mathonwy

The Swine and the Fatal Combat

Among Pryderi's treasures are the hobeu, the first swine ever brought to Wales — a gift to his house from Arawn of Annwn, which Pryderi had sworn neither to sell nor to give until the herd had bred twice its number. Gwydion fab Dôn comes to his court in the guise of a poet, is refused the pigs, and conjures twelve stallions with golden harness, twelve greyhounds with golden collars and leashes, and twelve golden shields — apparitions good for a day and gone by morning — and trades them for the herd. When the glamour fades, Pryderi musters Dyfed and comes north, and the war that follows forces the crime Gwydion and Gilfaethwy commit against Goewin. At the peace-talks Pryderi demands single combat, for it was because of him the hosts had come; at Y Felenrhyd, the tale says, Gwydion slew him 'by strength and valour, and by magic and enchantment'. They buried Pryderi there, at Maen Tywynog above Y Felenrhyd — and his grave, the Fourth Branch says, is there.

Extended Lore

Pryderi is named out of relief. His mother has carried a false charge for years — sitting by the horse-block, offering her back to strangers — and when the lost boy is set before her she does not say vengeance, or justice, or even joy: she says her care is lifted, and the court builds the child's name out of that word. Anxiety, made into a name and then, astonishingly, into a king. The rest of his story keeps faith with the naming. His is the kingdom a mist can empty between one hour and the next; his is the hand that sticks to the golden bowl; his is the herd of otherworld pigs that a poet's illusion can buy; his is the grave above Y Felenrhyd. Nothing in the Four Branches is safe, and Pryderi is the figure through whom the Mabinogi says so — the lord who loses everything except the thread of the tale, and who walks through all four branches carrying it. Care, the Welsh word means; and also what care is for: the watch kept, the debt counted, the mother who waited at the gate.
