Mythology

Agni’s mythology is less narrative than elemental: he is born daily from the friction-sticks, ​devours his parents (the sticks that bore him) at birth, and carries the sacrifice skyward. Yet the Vedic poets gave him a full biography — hidden at birth, stolen by the gods, ​established among men — and the epics gave him a genealogy and a role in every hero’s fire-drill.

The Hidden Birth (Rigveda 10.129)

The birth-hymns tell that Agni was concealed in the ​waters and the wood, secret like a son hidden by his mother, until the ten sisters — the fingers of the kindling-priest — rubbed him forth. ‘The new-born infant licks the earth’: ‌the first flame tasting the world it will consume and consecrate.

Agni Flees the Sacrifice (Rigveda 10.51)

In the great dialogue hymn, Agni hides from the burden of the office — ​the god who must eat the offering shrinks from being eaten by his own hunger. The gods find him in the waters, the fig-tree, the plants; each offers to hide him; he ‌returns to the ritual because prayer without fire goes nowhere. It is the most psychologically modern of Vedic hymns.

The Stolen Fire of the Gods (Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa)

The Brāhmaṇas narrate how the ​gods kept Agni from men until Mātariśvan, the Vedic Prometheus, brought him down hidden in the stalk of a plant — the mythic warrant for the fire-drill itself, and for the conviction ‌that fire is heaven’s property on loan to the hearth.

Agni and the Khandava Forest (Mahābhārata)

In the epic, Agni, weakened by a surfeit of ghee, begs Krishna and Arjuna to let ​him burn the Khandava forest; fed by the conflagration, he blazes up again as of old. The episode fixes the epic’s theology of fire: indiscriminate when ravenous, a god still, and the ‌friend of heroes who understand his hunger.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Agni concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The Messenger ​— Every offering is carried to the gods through his flame — Agni is the one channel of prayer.
  • Threefold Fire — He has three births — in the heavens as sun, ‌in the waters as lightning, on earth as the hearth-flame.
  • The Divine Priest — Hotṛ of the gods, he conducts the rite he is offered at — god and officiant in one ​flame.
  • Consumer and Purifier — He eats the oblation and burns away sin; the dead pass through his mouth to the fathers.

Archaeology & Evidence

The fire-altar itself is his monument: the ​brick agnicayana altars of the Śrauta ritual, rebuilt in every performance to the geometry the Śulbasūtras prescribe. Harappan fire-pits may be his prehistoric ancestry; Southeast Asian fire-shrines and the homa-cults of esoteric ‌Buddhism carried his cult across Asia.

Across Cultures

Agni is the Vedic counterpart of the Avestan Atar, ‌and the fire-cult is the one element Zoroastrianism kept most fully from the old Indo-Iranian religion. Greek writers compared him to Hephaestus and the altar-flame; modern linguistics binds him to Latin ignis ​and English ignite. In Hindu ritual he remains unpolluted by sect — every saṃskāra from birth to cremation passes through him.

Cultural Legacy

The Agnihotra — the twice-daily milk-offering to fire — has been performed continuously for three thousand years and is still performed today, the oldest uninterrupted ritual on earth. ​His name, through Latin ignis, kindled the English language’s whole fire-vocabulary; and the ritual fire’s three forms shaped the theology of light in every Indian tradition.
