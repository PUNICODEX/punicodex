The Fleet That Would Not Sail (Aulis)

The Cypria, the lost epic of the war's beginnings, told it first: ‌the Greek fleet gathers at Aulis and the winds die. Agamemnon has angered Artemis — in the epic's account by killing a stag in the goddess's grove and boasting he could outshoot her. ​The seer Kalkhas announces the price: the fleet will sail only when the king gives his daughter Iphigéneia to the goddess. Aeschylus compresses the whole unbearable machinery into the parodos of the Agamemnon ‌(104–257): the portent of two eagles tearing a hare — 'sacrifice a suffering thing' — and the elder king, 'clothed in ruinous frenzy', willing the act with 'a wretched holiness'.

What the tradition ​insists on is not the savagery but the logic: the war against Troy begins by consuming the Greeks' own, at home, before a single ship clears the harbor. Ten years of foreign slaughter ‌are inaugurated by one deliberate domestic one.

The Deer on the Altar (The Sacrifice)

Aeschylus stages what the epic only reported, and makes it the most harrowing scene in surviving Greek poetry (Agamemnon ​228–247): the girl, 'gagged like a goat', lifted over the altar by her father's men; 'her saffron robe pouring to the ground'; one glance, one cry for her brother — and her father, ‌'after that decision, willed it', 'and so the ships sailed'. The Cypria softened the blow for its audiences: Artemis snatched the maiden from the altar, set a deer in her place, and carried Iphigéneia ​to the land of the Taurians, where she made her immortal.

Euripides, in Iphigenia at Aulis, splits the difference and finds the truth inside the contradiction: his Iphigéneia arrives deceived — promised to ‌Achilles — and then, at the last, chooses the sacrifice herself, declaring her death shall be Greece's freedom. The tradition never lets us settle whether she was murdered, rescued, or volunteered; the ambiguity ​is the point, because all three answers say the same thing about war's arithmetic.

Priestess of the Strangers (Tauris)

Euripides' Iphigenia in Tauris finds her years later on the northern Black Sea coast ​— the Crimean Chersonesos of later geography — priestess of a savage Artemis whose rite demands that any Greek cast ashore be consecrated and slaughtered at the altar. She has become, in her ‌own bitter office, the one who prepares the victims. Her opening monologue is the tradition's most direct audit of the Aulis bargain: she was sacrificed, she says, so that Helen might be recovered ‌and the Greeks might go free — and the brother she died for now stands, in her dream, dead by her own hand at that very altar.

The myth's cruelty is precise: the ‌rescued girl is installed as executioner of her own people. Survival, the play suggests, is not the opposite of sacrifice but its continuation — the gods saved her body and spent her life.
