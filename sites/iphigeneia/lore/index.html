01

Mythology

Stories of Iphigéneia

Iphigéneia's story is the price of the Trojan War paid in advance: the maiden summoned to Aulis as a bride, lifted over her father's altar as the fleet's ransom, and — in the tellings that spare her — swept to the far shore of the Black Sea, where the gods set her a second altar and made her its keeper.

Aulis

The Fleet That Would Not Sail

The Cypria, the lost epic of the war's beginnings, told it first: the Greek fleet gathers at Aulis and the winds die. Agamemnon has angered Artemis — in the epic's account by killing a stag in the goddess's grove and boasting he could outshoot her. The seer Kalkhas announces the price: the fleet will sail only when the king gives his daughter Iphigéneia to the goddess. Aeschylus compresses the whole unbearable machinery into the parodos of the Agamemnon (104–257): the portent of two eagles tearing a hare — 'sacrifice a suffering thing' — and the elder king, 'clothed in ruinous frenzy', willing the act with 'a wretched holiness'.

What the tradition insists on is not the savagery but the logic: the war against Troy begins by consuming the Greeks' own, at home, before a single ship clears the harbor. Ten years of foreign slaughter are inaugurated by one deliberate domestic one.

The Sacrifice

The Deer on the Altar

Aeschylus stages what the epic only reported, and makes it the most harrowing scene in surviving Greek poetry (Agamemnon 228–247): the girl, 'gagged like a goat', lifted over the altar by her father's men; 'her saffron robe pouring to the ground'; one glance, one cry for her brother — and her father, 'after that decision, willed it', 'and so the ships sailed'. The Cypria softened the blow for its audiences: Artemis snatched the maiden from the altar, set a deer in her place, and carried Iphigéneia to the land of the Taurians, where she made her immortal.

Euripides, in Iphigenia at Aulis, splits the difference and finds the truth inside the contradiction: his Iphigéneia arrives deceived — promised to Achilles — and then, at the last, chooses the sacrifice herself, declaring her death shall be Greece's freedom. The tradition never lets us settle whether she was murdered, rescued, or volunteered; the ambiguity is the point, because all three answers say the same thing about war's arithmetic.

Tauris

Priestess of the Strangers

Euripides' Iphigenia in Tauris finds her years later on the northern Black Sea coast — the Crimean Chersonesos of later geography — priestess of a savage Artemis whose rite demands that any Greek cast ashore be consecrated and slaughtered at the altar. She has become, in her own bitter office, the one who prepares the victims. Her opening monologue is the tradition's most direct audit of the Aulis bargain: she was sacrificed, she says, so that Helen might be recovered and the Greeks might go free — and the brother she died for now stands, in her dream, dead by her own hand at that very altar.

The myth's cruelty is precise: the rescued girl is installed as executioner of her own people. Survival, the play suggests, is not the opposite of sacrifice but its continuation — the gods saved her body and spent her life.

The Recognition

The Letter and the Statue

The escape fills the second half of the Iphigenia in Tauris: two Greek captives are brought in, and one is Orestés, sent by Apollo to steal the Taurian image of Artemis. Recognition comes by letter — the play's celebrated scene of Orestés refusing to believe the impossible, and Iphigéneia inventing on the spot the fiction of a ritual purification to save them both. They seize the goddess's statue, flee to the ship, and are caught in the shallows until Athēnâ intervenes, commanding that the image be given a new home at Halai in Attica and that the goddess receive a rite in which a sword is drawn at a man's throat in token, bloodlessly — 'so shall the goddess have her honors'. King Thoas, cheated of his victims, accepts the goddess's will with one of tragedy's most humane exits: if gods they are, they are beyond his vengeance.

The ending converts a cult of human sacrifice into a festival of symbolic remembrance — myth rewriting ritual into mercy, and an audience taught to prefer it.

Cult

The Bears of Brauron

The Athenians gave her a home and a function. At Brauron on Attica's east coast stood the sanctuary of Artemis Brauronia, where girls — the famous arktoi, 'bears' — served the goddess before marriage, dancing in saffron robes and dedicating their childhood garments on leaving (Aristophanes, Lysistrata 641–647; the rite's sacred law survives inscribed on marble). Pausanias (1.33.1) saw Iphigéneia's tomb and cult there, and the clothes of women who had died in childbirth dedicated at her shrine; Megara disputed the honor with a rival tomb of its own (Pausanias 1.43.1). Later antiquity multiplied her endings: Antoninus Liberalis (27), after Nikander, reports that Artemis made her immortal as Hekátē; and the tragedians preserve the strangest variant — that she was not Agamemnon's daughter at all but Theseus' child by Helen (Stesichorus; Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis 509–512), born of a kidnapping before the war ever had a cause.

Whichever ending a city preferred, the office is constant: the maiden sacrificed to launch a war becomes the guardian of every girl's passage into adult life.

02

Iphigeneia

Sacrifice

Iphigéneia is sacrifice, winds, aulis. The name means "Mighty-born" — ἶφι, "by might," joined to γένος, "birth, race" — and hers is the Greek tradition's most searching account of what the Trojan War cost before it ever reached Troy.

Sacred Name

The restoration Iphigéneia returns the acute of Ἰφιγένεια and the name's full weight: "Mighty-born," the girl who carried her fate in her name.

Sacrifice

Summoned to Aulis on the pretext of marriage to Achilles, she became the price of the wind that would carry a thousand ships to Troy (Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis).

Living Tradition

Priestess of Artemis among the Taurians, then guardian of girls' passage at Brauron, where Athens showed her tomb (Euripides, Iphigenia in Tauris; Pausanias 1.33.1).

Unicode Restoration

Preserved as a flagship temple despite the unregistrable plain-ASCII form.

Sacred Symbols

The saffron robe The garment that poured to the ground at the altar (Aeschylus, Agamemnon 239) — maidenhood offered up in fabric.
The deer The substitute Artemis set on the altar in the Cypria's version — the rescue the epic grants her, taken back as suddenly as given.
The letter The written word that reunited her with Orestes in the Tauris — recognition carried by text, not tokens or scars.
The bear (arktos) The little girls who served Artemis Brauronia before marriage — Iphigéneia's Athenian afterlife as guardian of the passage she never lived.
The becalmed sea Aulis's windless harbor — the fleet's stillness ended only at her altar.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

Ἰφιγένεια

The name in its original Greek form. Iphigéneia (Ἰφιγένεια) is attested in the source tradition — “Mighty-born (ἶφι "by might" + γένος "birth")”. Its aspirated consonants, diphthongs, and acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

iphigeneia

Reduced to plain iphigeneia, the name loses everything that made it specific: aspirated consonants, diphthongs, and acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Iphigéneia

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Iphigéneia restores aspirated consonants, diphthongs, and acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Iphigéneia.com → xn--iphigneia-f4a.com

The non-ASCII characters in Iphigéneia are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Iphigéneia.

Pronunciation

How Iphigéneia was spoken

/iphigeneia/ Restored scholarly form
I- Short iota — the 'ee' of 'see', lightly sounded.
-phi- Phi, the aspirated p of classical Greek — 'p' released with a breath of air, as in 'uphill' said quickly.
-gé- Gamma-epsilon with the Greek acute: the stressed syllable, and a hard 'g' as in 'go'.
-nei- Nu-epsilon-iota in hiatus — two short vowels, 'neh-ee', not a diphthong.
-a Short final alpha.

Modern Approximation

ee-fee-GEH-neh-ee-ah — five syllables, the classical p aspirated, the g hard as in 'go'; the Greek acute falls on GE.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Iphigéneia is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the acute accent preserves the inherited Greek stress, but every vowel in Ἰφιγένεια is short — the antepenult accent itself proves the ultima is light. Nothing further distinguishes the name from its ASCII form.

Extended Lore

Iphigéneia asks the question Greek tragedy never stops asking: what kind of necessity can require the death of a child, and who is responsible for obeying it? The tradition refuses a clean answer three times over. In the epic, the gods demand it and the father consents. In Euripides' Aulis, the victim consents too — and her consent, freely given for a nation's freedom, disturbs us more than any command could. In the Tauris, the one who was spared is ordered to become the executioner, and the play's mercy lies in her refusal.
