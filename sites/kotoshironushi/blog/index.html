Overview

Mythology

Kotoshironushi’s mythology is brief but pivotal: as a son of Ōkuninushi, ruler of the earthly kami, he advised the peaceful cession of the land to the ​heavenly line of Amaterasu and then withdrew from the world. His “hiding” let the imperial succession begin without the annihilation of the old order, and later ages gathered his memory into Ebisu, the ​beloved god of fishermen and merchants.

The Cession of the Land (Kojiki)

Amaterasu resolved that her grandson Ninigi should rule the central land of reed plains, and sent the warrior god Takemikazuchi to ​compel its ruler, Ōnamuchi — Ōkuninushi — to yield. Ōkuninushi’s son Takeminakata answered the embassy with defiance, was defeated in a trial of strength, and fled across the sea to Suwa. Then the ‌Kojiki slows for the decisive interview: Ōkuninushi asked his son Kotoshironushi what should be done. Kotoshironushi answered that the land should be given up to the heavenly grandchildren, and — his counsel ​accepted — he withdrew into concealment, kakuremi, and “the land was then at peace.” His mother, the Kojiki adds, was Hayaakitsuhime, a daughter of the sea deity Ōwatatsumi, which is why his disappearance ‌ran seaward. By choosing counsel over resistance, one son of the old order ended a war that his brother’s arms could not have won — and the mythology marks the cost exactly: the ​surrendering god simply vanishes from the story he made possible.

The God Who Hides (Nihon Shoki)

The Nihon Shoki’s parallel account deepens both the withdrawal and the genealogy: it names Kotoshironushi’s mother a ‌child of the sea deity, binding him to Watatsumi’s watery realm, and dwells on the hiding itself — the kakuremi by which the god of the old dispensation removes himself so that the ​new one can begin. Japanese scholarship has long read the “hiding kami” as the mythic signature of cults that withdrew from the political centre: deities who lost the argument survived by retreating into ‌oracles, mountains, and the sea, keeping a voice precisely by surrendering a throne. The theme recurs across the early chronicles — gods who conceal themselves and must be lured, cajoled, or divined back ​into relationship with the court. Kotoshironushi is the purest case: he is the patron of every defeated thing that refuses to die, and his entire myth is an exit that turned out to ‌be a survival.

The Oracle Lord of Miwa (Shrine tradition)

His very name — 事代主, “lord who substitutes for the matter” — was read by later generations as a title of the oracular ​god: the one who speaks the divine word in place of another, and so “substitutes” for the hidden deity behind the message. Shrine tradition around Ōmiwa, at the foot of Mount Miwa in ​Nara prefecture — one of the oldest sanctuaries in Japan, where the mountain itself is the sacred body of the god — links his lineage to the Miwa cult, through which the will ‌of the kami was delivered to the court by divination and trance. Some scholars have gone further and proposed identifying Kotoshironushi with Ōmononushi, the great kami of Miwa itself; the identification remains a ‌hypothesis, but a telling one. In the Nihon Shoki’s age of oracles, the troubled reign of Emperor Sujin — plagues, failed harvests, and divine warnings that had to be sought and obeyed — ‌taught that neglected gods of the land would speak until properly housed. Kotoshironushi’s specialty is precisely that: being the voice of the one who is absent.

The Smiling God of Nishinomiya (Ebisu)

By ​the medieval and early modern centuries, the hidden god had resurfaced as the most approachable of deities. The great shrine of Nishinomiya in Hyōgo prefecture identifies its kami with Kotoshironushi and calls him ​Ebisu — “the prosperous guest” — the smiling god who carries a fishing rod and a red sea bream, the tai whose name puns on medetai, “auspicious.” Every January the shrine fills with ‌merchants and fishermen for the Tōka Ebisu festival, when visitors buy lucky bamboo grass and invite the god of the new year into their shops and boats. Among the Seven Gods of Fortune, ​Ebisu is the only one worshipped purely as a native kami, without Buddhist, Daoist, or foreign pedigree. The transformation is one of the neatest survivals in Japanese religion: the son who advised his ​father to surrender an empire became the guest who blesses a shopkeeper’s ledger. The old order lost the land — and won the marketplace.

Archaeology & Evidence

Ōmiwa Jinja in Sakurai, ‌Nara prefecture — among the oldest shrine sites in Japan — faces Mount Miwa directly, the mountain itself revered as the shintai, the divine body of the god; no main hall stands between ​worshipper and peak. The site’s antiquity is supported by third-century Chinese chronicles, which attest the political prominence of the Yamato region’s great shrine cults, and by the dense concentration of kofun burial mounds ‌in the Miwa area. At the other pole of his myth, Suwa in Nagano preserves the Takeminakata cult that the land-transfer story left behind. Nishinomiya Shrine’s Ebisu identification took its present form in ​the early modern period, but the shrine claims older precedent, and the Tōka Ebisu rite is documented from the Edo period onward.

Across Cultures

Kotoshironushi is a nodal figure connecting the land-transfer myth, the oracle cults of Miwa, and the later Ebisu tradition. He represents the old order’s willing surrender and the sea’s continuing ​bounty, and his identification with Ebisu — ratified at Nishinomiya and celebrated across Japan at Tōka Ebisu — is one of the clearest cases of mythological condensation in Japanese folk religion. Within the ‌Seven Gods of Fortune he keeps company with deities of Buddhist, Daoist, and Hindu origin, yet remains the only member with a purely native genealogy. Some scholars have further proposed identifying him with ​Ōmononushi, the serpent-kami of Miwa, which would bind the surrender myth directly to Japan’s oldest oracle shrine; the identification remains a hypothesis, but it shows how completely the hidden god was absorbed into ​the living cult.

Cultural Legacy

Kotoshironushi is rarely worshipped under his own ‌name today, but his legacy is everywhere in January: Ebisu, his smiling later form, presides over the first business of the year at shrines from Nishinomiya to Osaka’s Imamiya. The Tōka Ebisu festival ‌draws millions of merchants and fishermen who buy lucky bamboo to invite prosperity into the new year. In the national myth, he is remembered as the son whose counsel spared the country a ‌war of extermination — the voice of peaceful succession on which the imperial line’s legitimacy partly rests. And at Ōmiwa, before Mount Miwa, one of Japan’s oldest sanctuaries keeps the older stratum of ‌his story: a god who hides, and speaks.

A Meditation

Kotoshironushi is the theology of the graceful exit. Every tradition must answer the question his story poses: what becomes of the old order when ​the new one arrives? His answer — counsel surrender, then withdraw — let both worlds survive. There is a distinctly Japanese lesson in the shape of his career: power that argues with history ​loses, while power that serves as its voice endures. The oracle-god who speaks for the absent deity becomes, centuries later, the guest-god who blesses the shop: both are the same office, the sacred ‌made negotiable. In an age that treats every ending as a failure, the Lord Who Substitutes reminds us that some of the most durable things in a culture are precisely the ones ​that stepped aside at the right moment.
