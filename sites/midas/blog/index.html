The name Mídas and the world it opens

A name is a door. Mídas opens onto an entire world: the domain of king of phrygia, the golden touch, a Greek tradition, and centuries of storytelling, worship, and scholarship. This post walks through that world room by room — the name and its roots, the original script, the sound of it, the myths, the symbols, the sites, the afterlife across cultures — and ends at the newest room of all: a Unicode domain that makes the whole structure addressable. midas gets you to the same building, but only the restored form tells you why it was built.

Overview

Mídas (midas) — The Gilded King · Whose Touch Turns All to Gold — belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "King of Phrygia, the Golden Touch". The name itself is not Greek at root: it is a Phrygian royal name.

Mídas is the double figure of Greek imagination: a real king of Phrygia, known to Assyrian scribes as Mitâ of Muški and to Herodotus as the first foreigner to send gifts to Delphi — and the mythic fool whose wish made the world gold and his stomach empty. In him, legend and chronicle meet like the two halves of a coin.

PuniCodex restores the name as Mídas and serves its temple at mídas.com. The original carries only one of the two prosodic features — stress, without distinctive vowel length — which places the name in Tier 2. The plain ASCII form midas survives as a modern convenience; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete.

The Name

The name is attested in Greek as Μίδας, a first-declension masculine of the -ᾱς type, genitive Μίδου. But the Greeks themselves knew it was not theirs: it was the throne-name of the kings of Phrygia, borne by more than one ruler of the dynasty at Gordion, much as 'Pharaoh' or 'Caesar' functions as a title in foreign mouths. Herodotus gives the canonical Greek form of the man — Midas son of Gordias, the first foreign king to dedicate offerings at Delphi — and the Assyrian annals of Sargon II give the same king in their own syllabary: Mitâ, king of Muški, pressing the empire's eastern marches in the late eighth century BCE.

Etymology & Roots

The name's family, as the canonical sources give it:

  • Assyrian: Mitâ, king of Muški, in the annals of Sargon II — the same historical king the Greeks called Mídas
  • Phrygian: Midas in the Old Phrygian inscriptions of Gordion and Midas City — a royal name borne by more than one Phrygian king
  • Greek: Μίδας, the Hellenized form, fixed by Herodotus and the myth

Beekes treats Μίδας as a foreign royal name naturalized in Greek — which is exactly what the epigraphy shows. The Phrygians wrote a related alphabet in their own cities, and there the name stands in native dress on the monuments themselves. The etymology lesson of this entry runs backwards: the question is not what the Greek name means, but how a Phrygian name traveled — into Assyrian record as Mitâ, into Greek story as Μίδας, and into English proverb as the touch and the ears.

The Original Script

The Greek form is Μίδας: mu with an acute accent, iota, delta, alpha, sigma. The acute is the name's only diacritic, fixing the stress on the first syllable — MÍ-das, where the English habit of MY-dus both moves the pitch and corrupts the vowel. Both Greek vowels are short, so stress is the single feature the restoration preserves: a textbook Tier 2 case. Behind the Greek letters stands the name's older spelling life — Old Phrygian characters on the rock of the 'Midas Monument' at Yazılıkaya, and cuneiform wedges in the annals of Assyria — a reminder that this name was being carved into stone long before it was typed into a browser.

Pronunciation

Mídas is Tier 2 because the Greek Μίδας carries only the acute stress; the vowels are short and no long vowel or diphthong is preserved. Behind the Greek form stands a genuine Phrygian royal name — the legend has a historical king inside it.

The reconstructed Attic sound is /mí.das/ — 'MEE-dass', stress forward, the first syllable bright and the second clipped; the English 'MY-das' flattens both. Three phonemes carry the name:

  • Mí- — bilabial nasal opening into acute-pitched iota: the name's single mark, pitched high.
  • -d- — voiced dental stop: plain and firm.
  • -as — short alpha and final sigma: the common ending of Anatolian royal names.

Mythology

The Greeks attached two great fables to Mídas — the touch and the ears — and one genuine history: the wealthy Phrygian king whose gifts reached Delphi and whose wars reached Assyria. Ovid gives the fables their classic form; Herodotus keeps the history.

Silenos Returned (The Reward)

Silenos, the drunken companion of Dionysos, strayed and was brought to Mídas — in some tellings caught in the king's own rose gardens. Mídas entertained him ten days and returned him to the god, who offered any gift in thanks. The king, thinking of his treasury, wished that everything he touched might become gold. The wish was granted on the spot.

All He Touches Turns to Gold (The Curse)

The gift revealed itself at table: the bread stiffened in his hands, the wine ran molten and would not wet his lips. Hungry and thirsty in a house of gold, Mídas begged release. Dionysos sent him to wash in the spring of the Pactolus at Sardis; the power passed from his body into the river, whose sands bore gold ever after. Later ages added a gilded daughter to the tale — antiquity was content with the bread and the wine.

The Ears of an Ass (The Judgment)

Old Tmolos judged the music-contest of Pan and Apollo for the lyre; Mídas alone declared for the pipes. Apollo would not suffer such ears to keep their human shape: they lengthened, furred, and moved. The king hid them under a Phrygian cap, but his barber knew, and whispered the secret into a hole in the ground — and the reeds that grew there have murmured 'King Mídas has ass's ears' on every wind since.

Mitâ of Muški (The King)

The Assyrian annals of Sargon II name Mitâ, king of Muški, pressing on the empire's eastern marches and then seeking its friendship — the Greek Mídas, ruler of Phrygia from Gordion in the late eighth century BCE. Herodotus preserves his splendor: the first foreign king to dedicate at Delphi, where his throne of judgment could still be seen, and the roses of his Macedonian gardens, which the Phrygians were said to have tended before they crossed into Asia.

Symbols & Iconography

The objects the tradition hangs on Mídas are the two hungers — greed and shame:

  • Gold — the substance of his wish and his punishment.
  • The ass's ears — the mark of the critic who hears wrongly.
  • The whispering reeds — grown from the hole where the barber buried the secret; they repeat it still.
  • The rose gardens — his gardens below Mount Bermion, where Silenos was taken; roses of sixty petals, Herodotus says.
  • The Pactolus — the river that took the gold from his hands into its sands.

The Phrygian Inscriptions

Phrygia was a literate kingdom, and its king's name survives in its own script. From the eighth century BCE the Phrygians wrote an alphabet adapted from the Greek in rock-cut and incised texts across central Anatolia; some 250 Old Phrygian inscriptions are known. The most famous is carved on the colossal rock façade at Yazılıkaya — the 'Midas Monument' — whose dedication names Midas in the Phrygian form Midai. The nearby citadel, still called Midas City, stands in the Phrygian highlands where his dynasty ruled. These stones are the name's oldest attested spellings, contemporary with the Assyrian Mitâ and centuries older than the first Greek telling of the golden touch.

Archaeology & Evidence

Gordion (Yassıhöyük), the Phrygian capital on the Sangarios, has been excavated by the University of Pennsylvania since 1950: the citadel's gate and megarons, and the Great Tumulus (Tumulus MM, ca. 740 BCE) with its preserved wooden chamber and hoard of bronzes — now dated a generation too early for Mídas himself and assigned to his dynasty, most plausibly Gordias his father. Old Phrygian inscriptions name Midas on the rock façade of the 'Midas Monument' at Yazılıkaya and at Midas City. The Assyrian annals of Sargon II supply the external chronicle, naming Mitâ of Muški between 718 and 709 BCE. His ivory throne at Delphi, which Herodotus saw, has not survived.

Realm & Domain

Mídas is the double figure of Greek imagination: a real king of Phrygia, known to Assyrian scribes as Mitâ of Muški and to Herodotus as the first foreigner to send gifts to Delphi — and the mythic fool whose wish made the world gold and his stomach empty. In him, legend and chronicle meet like the two halves of a coin.

The Golden Touch

Granted by Dionysos in thanks for the return of Silenos; everything his hands touched — bread, wine, roses — hardened into gold.

The Judgment of Ears

When he preferred Pan's pipes to Apollo's lyre, Apollo gave him the ears of an ass — the byword for deaf judgment ever since.

The Phrygian King

Behind the fable stands a historical ruler of Gordion, contemporary of Sargon II of Assyria, lord of the Phrygian migrations.

The River Pactolus

Where he washed away the gift at the god's command; its sands ran gold afterward, the aition of Lydia's wealth.

Across Cultures

Mídas absorbed no foreign gods; instead, the myth absorbed the man. Greek storytellers took a historical Phrygian dynasty and hung upon it their two great parables of appetite and judgment. Rome passed the fables on in Ovid's polished telling, and in time the moral inverted itself: the 'Midas touch', once a curse, became the financier's compliment. The ass's ears went the other way, remaining exactly what Apollo made them — the emblem of the critic who cannot hear.

Cultural Legacy

Few names are so thoroughly proverbial. 'The Midas touch' names effortless profit; 'Midas ears' names the opposite gift in judgment. Hawthorne's A Wonder-Book (1852) added the golden daughter, the version children now learn first; opera, satire, and economics have all mined him. The historical king endures as firmly as the fool: Gordion's excavations made him a fixed point of Iron Age chronology. To restore Mídas with its acute is to restore the Greek shape of a Phrygian name — a small act of hearing rightly, which he himself never managed.

A Meditation

The touch and the ears are one story told twice. Both are parables of misjudged value: the king who thinks gold is the best thing in the world, and the listener who thinks pipes are better than the lyre. Each wish-come-true is a famine; each judgment is a deformity. The Greeks were exact about this — wealth and taste are the two places where a man chooses what he is, and Mídas chose wrong both times, and knew it, and wept, and was not destroyed.

For the myth is gentler than its proverb. Dionysos releases him; Apollo only marks him. He washes the gold away in the river and, Ovid says, hated riches ever after — a man cured by his cure. Perhaps that is why a real king's name clung to the fable: the historical Mídas was famous for sending his treasures away, a throne across the sea to Delphi. The legend only exaggerates the gesture. He is the king forever giving gold back, and the river still pays it out, grain by grain.

The Unicode Restoration

Mídas is classified as Tier 2: the original carries only one of the two prosodic features — stress or vowel length — not both. The ASCII fallback midas still resolves everywhere, but it is the restored form that carries the name's full information. Across the 5 characters of the name, the restoration adjusts 1: 1 mark of stress (í). That is the whole thesis of this temple: the marks are the message.

The Domain Name

The restored name is live as a working domain: mídas.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--mdas-vpa.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. The visitor sees Mídas; the machines see the encoding.
