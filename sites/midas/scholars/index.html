01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Mídas (midas) — The Gilded King · Whose Touch Turns All to Gold — belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "King of Phrygia, the Golden Touch". The name means "Phrygian royal name"[1].

Mídas is the double figure of Greek imagination: a real king of Phrygia, known to Assyrian scribes as Mitâ of Muški and to Herodotus as the first foreigner to send gifts to Delphi — and the mythic fool whose wish made the world gold and his stomach empty. In him, legend and chronicle meet like the two halves of a coin.[2]

PuniCodex restores the name as Mídas and serves its temple at mídas.com. The original preserves one prosodic feature — stress or vowel length — rather than both, which places the name in Tier 2. The plain ASCII form midas survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete[3].

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name is attested in Greek as Μίδας. Etymologically it means "Phrygian royal name"[1].

The ASCII form midas survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Mídas recovers the stress accent of the original directly in the address bar. The original preserves one prosodic feature — stress or vowel length — rather than both, which places the name in Tier 2.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • m → M — M uppercase
  • i → í — Acute on i
  • d → d — d same
  • a → a — a same
  • s → s — s same

The project holds the domain mídas.com (xn--mdas-vpa.com) as the canonical home of this name[2].

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /mí.das/ — Attic Greek Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Mí- — Bilabial nasal opening into acute-pitched iota [mí] — the name's single mark, pitched high.
  • -d- — Voiced dental stop [d] — plain and firm.
  • -as — Short alpha and final sigma [as] — the common ending of Anatolian royal names.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'MEE-dass' — stress forward, the first syllable bright and the second clipped; the English 'MY-das' flattens both.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Assyrian — Mitâ, king of Muški, in the annals of Sargon II — the same historical king the Greeks called Mídas
  • Phrygian — Midas in the Old Phrygian inscriptions of Gordion and Midas City — a royal name borne by more than one Phrygian king
  • Greek — Μίδας, the Hellenized form, fixed by Herodotus and the myth

Mídas is Tier 2 because the Greek Μίδας carries only the acute stress; the vowels are short and no long vowel or diphthong is preserved. Behind the Greek form stands a genuine Phrygian royal name — the legend has a historical king inside it.

Sources

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Mídas is the double figure of Greek imagination: a real king of Phrygia, known to Assyrian scribes as Mitâ of Muški and to Herodotus as the first foreigner to send gifts to Delphi — and the mythic fool whose wish made the world gold and his stomach empty. In him, legend and chronicle meet like the two halves of a coin.[1]

The Golden Touch

Granted by Dionysos in thanks for the return of Silenos; everything his hands touched — bread, wine, roses — hardened into gold.

The Judgment of Ears

When he preferred Pan's pipes to Apollo's lyre, Apollo gave him the ears of an ass — the byword for deaf judgment ever since.

The Phrygian King

Behind the fable stands a historical ruler of Gordion, contemporary of Sargon II of Assyria, lord of the Phrygian migrations.

The River Pactolus

Where he washed away the gift at the god's command; its sands ran gold afterward, the aition of Lydia's wealth.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography associated with Mídas concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:[1]

  • Gold — The substance of his wish and his punishment
  • The ass's ears — The mark of the critic who hears wrongly
  • The whispering reeds — Grown from the hole where the barber buried the secret; they repeat it still
  • The rose gardens — His gardens below Mount Bermion, where Silenos was taken — roses of sixty petals, Herodotus says
  • The Pactolus — The river that took the gold from his hands into its sands

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

The Greeks attached two great fables to Mídas — the touch and the ears — and one genuine history: the wealthy Phrygian king whose gifts reached Delphi and whose wars reached Assyria. Ovid gives the fables their classic form; Herodotus keeps the history.[1]

Silenos Returned (The Reward)

Silenos, the drunken companion of Dionysos, strayed and was brought to Mídas — in some tellings caught in the king's own rose gardens. Mídas entertained him ten days and returned him to the god, who offered any gift in thanks. The king, thinking of his treasury, wished that everything he touched might become gold. The wish was granted on the spot.[2]

All He Touches Turns to Gold (The Curse)

The gift revealed itself at table: the bread stiffened in his hands, the wine ran molten and would not wet his lips. Hungry and thirsty in a house of gold, Mídas begged release. Dionysos sent him to wash in the spring of the Pactolus at Sardis; the power passed from his body into the river, whose sands bore gold ever after. Later ages added a gilded daughter to the tale — antiquity was content with the bread and the wine.

The Ears of an Ass (The Judgment)

Old Tmolos judged the music-contest of Pan and Apollo for the lyre; Mídas alone declared for the pipes. Apollo would not suffer such ears to keep their human shape: they lengthened, furred, and moved. The king hid them under a Phrygian cap, but his barber knew, and whispered the secret into a hole in the ground — and the reeds that grew there have murmured 'King Mídas has ass's ears' on every wind since.

Mitâ of Muški (The King)

The Assyrian annals of Sargon II name Mitâ, king of Muški, pressing on the empire's eastern marches and then seeking its friendship — the Greek Mídas, ruler of Phrygia from Gordion in the late eighth century BCE. Herodotus preserves his splendor: the first foreign king to dedicate at Delphi, where his throne of judgment could still be seen, and the roses of his Macedonian gardens, which the Phrygians were said to have tended before they crossed into Asia.

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Mídas absorbed no foreign gods; instead, the myth absorbed the man. Greek storytellers took a historical Phrygian dynasty and hung upon it their two great parables of appetite and judgment. Rome passed the fables on in Ovid's polished telling, and in time the moral inverted itself: the 'Midas touch', once a curse, became the financier's compliment. The ass's ears went the other way, remaining exactly what Apollo made them — the emblem of the critic who cannot hear.[1]

Within the Greek tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Achérōn, Achilleús, Adámas, Aḗr, Aithḗr, and Anánkē.

Sources

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗
09

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Few names are so thoroughly proverbial. 'The Midas touch' names effortless profit; 'Midas ears' names the opposite gift in judgment. Hawthorne's A Wonder-Book (1852) added the golden daughter, the version children now learn first; opera, satire, and economics have all mined him. The historical king endures as firmly as the fool: Gordion's excavations made him a fixed point of Iron Age chronology. To restore Mídas with its acute is to restore the Greek shape of a Phrygian name — a small act of hearing rightly, which he himself never managed.[1]

Sources

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Gordion (Yassıhöyük), the Phrygian capital on the Sangarios, has been excavated by the University of Pennsylvania since 1950: the citadel's gate and megarons, and the Great Tumulus (Tumulus MM, ca. 740 BCE) with its preserved wooden chamber and hoard of bronzes — now dated a generation too early for Mídas himself and assigned to his dynasty, most plausibly Gordias his father. Old Phrygian inscriptions name Midas on the rock façade of the 'Midas Monument' at Yazılıkaya and at Midas City. The Assyrian annals of Sargon II supply the external chronicle, naming Mitâ of Muški between 718 and 709 BCE. His ivory throne at Delphi, which Herodotus saw, has not survived.[1]

Sources

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗

Homeric Hymns

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No hymn addresses Mídas, and none should be expected to: the corpus sings of gods, and Mídas entered Greek song late, as a historical dynast absorbed into fable. The hymns do, however, celebrate both divinities of his two great stories. The Dionysiac hymns — above all the seventh, of the god and the pirates — hymn the power who granted the golden touch in gratitude for Silenos's return; and the Homeric Hymn to Pan sings the wild musician of the mountains, whose pipes Mídas famously preferred to the lyre of Apóllōn.[1] Between those two hymns lies his whole myth, and the corpus never notices: the contest on Tmolos and the gift of Dionysos belong to elegy, comedy, and Ovid, not to the hymned liturgy.[2] For a figure in the hymns' own world, the closest witness is Herodotus's prose — the first foreign king to send gifts to Delphi.[3]

Sources

  1. Homeric Hymn 7, to Dionysos; Homeric Hymn to Pan.
  2. Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85–193.
  3. Herodotus, Histories 1.14.

Epithets & Epicleses

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Mídas has no poetic epithet-stock — he is a dynast of record, not a hero of formulaic song — and what attaches to him are titles, relations, and proverbs:

  • Γορδίου παῖς — 'son of Gordias' — Herodotus's identification at Delphi; the historian's own genealogy, which elsewhere makes him Gordias's father, shows a dynasty alternating the two throne-names.[1]
  • ὁ Φρυγίας βασιλεύς — 'the king of Phrygia' — his plain title in Greek historians and geographers.[1]
  • šar Muški — 'king of Muški' — the Assyrian style of the same man in the annals of Sargon II, where he appears as Mitâ.[2]
  • ὁ χρυσοῦς — 'the golden' — the proverbial epithet later tradition fastened on him; antiquity preferred the fable's own terms, 'the touch' and 'the ears'.[3]

The name itself functioned as the title: a Phrygian throne-name carried by more than one king, as 'Caesar' and 'Pharaoh' would be after him.

Sources

  1. Herodotus, Histories 1.14, 1.35, 8.138.
  2. The annals of Sargon II (Mitâ, king of Muški).
  3. Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.

Oracle & Cult Sites

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Mídas belongs to oracular geography as a client, not a site. Herodotus makes him the first foreign king to dedicate at Delphi, and names the offering precisely: the throne on which he sat to give judgment, 'well worth seeing', displayed in the treasury of the Corinthians beside the gold of Gyges.[1] The gesture begins Phrygia's long courtship of the Delphic god, and it anchors the fable-king in the real economy of archaic prestige — gold sent across the sea to the oracle, not hoarded under a wish.[2] His other sacred landmark is Herodotus's own: the gardens of Mídas below Mount Bermion in Macedonia, where wild roses of sixty petals grew and where, the Macedonians said, Silenos was captured — the landscape the Phrygians were thought to have left when they crossed into Asia.[3] No oracle answered in his name and no hero-shrine received him; his sacred footprint is that of a dedicator.

Sources

  1. Herodotus, Histories 1.14 (the throne at Delphi).
  2. Herodotus, Histories 1.14; Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.137–145.
  3. Herodotus, Histories 8.138 (the gardens below Bermion).

Iconography

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ancient image of Mídas rests on two attributes. The first is the Phrygian cap — the soft, forward-falling tiara of his people — under which Ovid says he tried to hide his shame, and with which ancient art marks him as the eastern king; it is the same cap the iconographic tradition gives to Trojans and to Orpheus in his Thracian dress.[1] The second is the ass's ears themselves: Roman sarcophagi and wall paintings of the musical contest on Tmolos show the king already marked, seated between Pan with his pipes and Apollo with his lyre, the ears unmistakable above the cap's edge.[2] The golden touch proved unpaintable directly — art renders it by consequence: the averted cup, the hardened wreath — while the return of Silenos to Dionysos, the episode that earned the wish, was a favorite of Roman Dionysiac sarcophagi: the drunk old satyr led before a crowned eastern king.[3]

Sources

  1. Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.180 (the cap over the ears).
  2. Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.156–179 (the judgment of Tmolos).
  3. Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.90–105 (Silenos returned).
16

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

The touch and the ears are one story told twice. Both are parables of misjudged value: the king who thinks gold is the best thing in the world, and the listener who thinks pipes are better than the lyre. Each wish-come-true is a famine; each judgment is a deformity. The Greeks were exact about this — wealth and taste are the two places where a man chooses what he is, and Mídas chose wrong both times, and knew it, and wept, and was not destroyed.

For the myth is gentler than its proverb. Dionysos releases him; Apollo only marks him. He washes the gold away in the river and, Ovid says, hated riches ever after — a man cured by his cure. Perhaps that is why a real king's name clung to the fable: the historical Mídas was famous for sending his treasures away, a throne across the sea to Delphi. The legend only exaggerates the gesture. He is the king forever giving gold back, and the river still pays it out, grain by grain.[1]

Sources

  1. Histories, Loeb Classical Library, 440 BCE. ↗
