01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Uppsala (uppsala) ​— Upper ‌halls · Temple, ‌Freyr, Sacred Grove ​— is ​the one sanctuary ​of the ​Norse world the ​medieval record describes ‌in full: ​the golden temple ​at Ubsola where, ‌in Adam ​of Bremen's account ‌(c. 1075), ​the statues of ‌Þórr, Wodan, and ​Fricco sat ​enthroned; the sacred ​grove beside ​it, every tree ​made divine by ‌the offerings ​hung in its ‌branches; and the ​nine-day festival, ‌renewed every ninth ​year, at ‌which nine males ​of every living ‌kind were ​sacrificed to placate ​the gods.[1] ‌Snorri's Ynglingasaga gives ‌the site its ‌founding god ​— Freyr built ​the temple at ‌Uppsalir, made ​it his chief ​seat, endowed ‌it with the ​Uppsala wealth, and ​lies in ‌a mound beside ​it — ‌while the three ​royal mounds of ​Gamla Uppsala, ​cremation graves of ​sixth- and ‌seventh-century kings, stand ‌on the same ​ground as ‌archaeology's answer to ​the saga.[2]

The ​name is ‌a transparent compound: ‌upp 'up' ‌+ salir, plural ‌of salr 'hall' ​— 'the ‌upper halls'.[3] PuniCodex ​restores the ​name as Uppsala ​— identical to ‌the ASCII ​input, because normalized ​Old Norse writes ​no diacritic ‌in this name. ​The mechanical ​rule therefore places ​the name in ‌Tier 2: ​nothing distinctive is ‌lost in ​transit, a statement ‌about orthography and ​never about ‌the place. The ‌temple is domain-less: ​the plain-ASCII ​uppsala.com is registered ​to a ​third party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Uppsalir ​— a nominative ​plural, genitive ​Uppsala — a ​compound of ​upp 'up' and ‌salir, the ​plural of salr ​'hall': 'the ‌upper halls'. The ​plural form ‌and the elevated ​site on ‌the ridge above ​the Fyris ​plain have long ​been read ​together: the halls ​up above, ‌royal and divine ​at once.[1] Adam ‌of Bremen, ​the name's earliest ‌literary witness, ​Latinizes it as ‌Ubsola; the ​modern Swedish form ‌Uppsala descends ​from the Old ​Norse genitive, ‌and the name ‌has never ‌died out.

The ​ASCII form ​is not a ‌fallback here ​but the form ​itself: normalized ‌Old Norse writes ​seven plain ​Latin letters and ‌a capital. ​The Unicode restoration ‌Uppsala therefore ​preserves nothing the ​ASCII alphabet ​loses, and the ​mechanical rule ‌classifies the name ‌Tier 2 ​— an orthographic ‌fact, not ​a judgment on ​the sanctuary.

‌The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • ‌u → U ‌— Same, ​capitalized
  • p ‌→ p ​— Same
  • ​p → ​p — Same

‌- s → ​s — ​Same

  • a ​→ a ‌— Same
  • ​l → ​l — Same

​- a ‌→ a — ​Same

The ‌project holds no ​domain for ‌this name. The ​plain-ASCII uppsala.com ‌is registered to ‌a third ​party outside the ​collection and ​is watched for ​release; this ‌temple claims nothing ‌it does ​not hold.[2]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the Old Norse ‌form is ​/ˈupː.sɑ.lir/ — Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- U- — ​Short high ‌back rounded [u], ​stressed on ​the first syllable ‌as in ​every Old Norse ‌word, held brief ​and fully ‌rounded

  • -pp- ​— The ​geminate voiceless bilabial ​stop [pː] ​held audibly long ​across the ‌syllable boundary, as ​in modern Swedish

‌- -sa- ​— Voiceless alveolar ‌[s] and ​short open back ‌unrounded [ɑ]
​- -lir — ‌Light alveolar ​[l], short close ​[i], and a ‌trilled final ‌[r]: the plural ‌ending -ir, ​fully sounded

For ​the modern ‌speaker, the closest ​approximation is: ​'OOP-sah-lir' — three ‌beats, the double ​p held ​a fraction long, ‌the final ​r trilled. Modern ‌Swedish Uppsala ​('OOP-sah-lah') has shed ​the old plural ​ending.

Kindred ​and historical forms ‌of the ‌name:

  • Old ​Norse — ‌Uppsalir (nominative plural), ​genitive Uppsala: ​'the upper halls'

‌- Medieval Latin ‌— Ubsola, ‌Adam of Bremen's ‌spelling (c. ​1075), the name's ‌earliest literary ​witness[2]

  • Younger ​Futhark — ​ᚢᛒᛒᛋᛅᛚᛅ (ubbsala), a ‌normalized phonetic reconstruction; ​the sixteen-rune ​row writes no ​b/p or ‌vowel-length distinctions
  • ​Modern Swedish ​— Uppsala, the ​unbroken living ‌name of the ​city, its cathedral, ‌and its ​university

Uppsala is ‌Tier 2 ​because the restoration ‌preserves nothing ‌the ASCII form ​loses: no ​diacritic, no distinctive ​letter, no vowel ​length. English ‌and Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis ecclesiae pontificum 4.26, trans. Tschan, rev. Reuter (Columbia University Press, 2002).

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval manuscripts ‌in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Uppsalir (genitive ​Uppsala) — ​Old Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ‌in the ​Icelandic and Latin ​textual record ‌of the twelfth ​and thirteenth ‌centuries, and Latinized ​as Ubsola ‌by Adam ​of Bremen around ​1075, the ​name's earliest witness.[1] ​Before the ​Latin bookhand, Old ‌Norse names ​were carved ‌in the Younger ​Futhark; no ‌runic attestation of ​the place-name ‌survives, so ​the manuscript form ‌is the ​primary witness. The ​runic rendering ‌is ᚢᛒᛒᛋᛅᛚᛅ (ubbsala).

‌The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ​step by step:

​- The ‌name is a ​compound, not ​a simplex: upp ‌'up' + ​salir, plural ​of salr 'hall' ‌— 'the ​upper halls'.

  • ‌ᚢ (úr) ​writes the ​rounded back vowels ​/u, o, ​ø, ǫ, y/ ‌and /w/; ‌the initial u ​needs no ‌diacritic.
  • ​ᛒ (bjarkan) writes ​both /b/ ‌and /p/, so ‌the carving ‌cannot mark the ‌geminate voiceless ​stop the ‌manuscripts write pp.

​- ᛋ ​(sól) writes /s/; ​ᛅ (ár) ‌writes /a/; ​ᛚ (lögr) writes ​/l/ — ​and the sixteen-rune ‌row separates ​neither short from ​long vowels ​nor voiced ‌from voiceless stops.
​- The ‌spelling ubbsala is ​therefore a ‌normalized phonetic reconstruction, ​and the ‌Unicode restoration ‌Uppsala is identical ​to the ​ASCII form — ​the restoration ​is a ‌matter of canonical ‌reference, not ​rescued orthography.[2]

Sources

  1. Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis ecclesiae pontificum 4.26, trans. Tschan, rev. Reuter (2002); Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. uppsalir; Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. uppsalir.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Uppsala is ​catalogued under Temple, ‌Freyr, Sacred Grove ‌— the three ​facts on ​which every witness ​agrees, and the ​three registers in ​which the site ‌operated: cult house, ​god's seat, and ​holy ground.[1]

‌### Temple

Adam ​of Bremen describes ‌the temple at ​Ubsola as entirely ‌decked with gold, ​a golden chain ​hanging from ​its gable, and ​within it the ​enthroned statues of ‌the three gods ​— Þórr central ‌with his sceptre, ​Wodan armed, Fricco ‌shaped for ​peace and pleasure ‌— each served ​by his own ‌priests. It is ​the fullest description ​of a Norse ‌cult building ‌that survives.[1]

‌Freyr

Snorri's Ynglingasaga ​makes the site ​the seat of ‌Freyr himself: the ​god built the ​temple at ‌Uppsalir, endowed it ​with all his ​revenues — the ‌origin of the ​Uppsala wealth, the ‌crown estate of ​the Swedish kings ​— and ​was buried in ​a mound on ‌the same ground. ‌Cult and kingship ​at Uppsala are ‌one inheritance.[2]

​Sacred Grove

​Beside the temple ‌stood the grove ‌in which the ‌nine-year sacrifice was ‌hung — men, ​horses, and dogs ‌among the ​branches, every tree ​held divine because ​of the death ‌and decay of ​the victims. A ​spring beside the ​temple received a ‌living man; ​if he was ​not found again, ​the people's wish ‌would be granted.[1]

​### Kingship

The ‌three royal mounds ​of Gamla ‌Uppsala and the ​dynasty the poems ‌call Ynglings or ‌Scylfings bind the ​sanctuary to the ​crown: the site ​where Óðinn's ​priestly legacy, Freyr's ‌temple, and the ‌kings' graves occupy ​one ridge.[2]

Sources

  1. Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis ecclesiae pontificum 4.26–27 with scholia, trans. Tschan, rev. Reuter (2002).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglingasaga 10, trans. Hollander (1964).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Uppsala is ‌architectural and ​ritual ​rather than ​mythic — ​the ​corpus fixes ‌around the ​sanctuary a ​small ‌set of ​objects no ‌account can ​omit:[1]

‌- The ​gilded temple ​— ​the gold-decked ​hall where ​the triad ‌sat ​enthroned: the ‌sacral core ​of Swedish ‌kingship, ​visible wealth ‌offered to ​the ‌gods

  • ​The enthroned ​triad — ‌Þórr ‌central with ‌his sceptre, ​Wodan armed ​like ‌Mars, Fricco ​shaped for ​peace ‌and pleasure: ​sovereignty, war, ​and fertility ‌enshrined ​in one ‌room
  • ​The sacred ​grove ​— trees ​made divine ‌by ‌sacrifice: the ​boundary where ‌offering becomes ​landscape ​and the ‌forest itself ‌turns temple

‌- ‌The hanging ​offerings — ‌men, ​horses, and ​dogs suspended ​in the ‌branches, ​nine of ​every kind: ​the commonwealth ‌renewed ​at the ​price of ​nine-year ‌blood

  • ​The royal ‌mound — ​the ‌barrow of ​kings and, ‌by Snorri's ‌account, ​of Freyr ​himself: dynasty ​made ​continuous with ‌the god ‌who endowed ​it[2]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Uppsala's mythology is ​a sanctuary's record rather than a ‌god's biography — the fullest any ‌Norse holy place receives, carried by ​four witnesses.

The Golden Temple ​(Adam of Bremen 4.26)

Adam of ​Bremen, writing about 1075 from the ​reports of the Danish king ​Sven Estridsen, describes the famous temple ‌called Ubsola near Sigtuna, entirely decked ​with gold. Within it the people ​worship the statues of three gods: ‌the most powerful, Thor, occupies the ​throne in the middle of the ‌hall, with Wodan and Fricco ​on either side. Thor governs the ‌air, thunder, lightning, winds, rains, fair ​weather and crops, and holds his ​sceptre like Jove; Wodan — 'that ​is, Frenzy' — rules war and ​is carved armed like Mars; ​Fricco, bestower of peace and pleasure, ‌is shaped with an immense phallus. ​Each god has his priests; in ‌plague or famine the people sacrifice ​to Thor, for war to Wodan, ‌for weddings to Fricco.[1]

The ​Nine-Day Sacrifice (Adam of Bremen ‌4.27)

Every nine years, Adam records, ​a solemn assembly of all the ‌provinces of Sweden is held at ​Uppsala, from which no one may ​purchase exemption. Of every living creature ‌that is male, nine head are ‌offered, and the bodies are ‌hung in the grove adjoining the ​temple — a grove so sacred ​that each tree is believed divine ‌because of the death and putrefaction ​of the victims. Dogs and horses ​hang there with the men; one ‌of the Christians told Adam ​he had seen seventy-two bodies hanging ​together. The incantations sung at the ‌rite, Adam adds, are so disgraceful ​that it is better to say ‌nothing of them. A scholion adds ​the spring beside the temple ​in which a living man was ​drowned.[2]

Freyr's Temple and the ​Uppsala Wealth (Ynglingasaga 10)

Snorri gives ‌the site its founding god. Freyr, ‌succeeding his father Njörðr, built a ​great temple at Uppsalir, made it ‌his chief seat, and endowed ​it with lands, livestock, and money ​— the origin of the Uppsala ‌auðr, the crown estate of the ‌Swedish kings. In his days began ‌the peace and good seasons the ‌Swedes remembered as the best of ​times; his other name was ‌Yngvi, and from him the royal ​house took its name, the Ynglings. ​When Freyr died, his men hid ​the news, raised a mound with ‌three holes, and received the people's ​offerings of gold, silver, and copper ​through them for three years ​— the god of the temple ‌buried on his own ground, cult ​and kingship one estate.[3]

Kraki's ​Seed on the Fyrisvellir (Skáldskaparmál / ​Hrólfs saga kraka)

Snorri, explaining in ‌Skáldskaparmál why gold may be ​called 'Kraki's seed', tells how Hrólfr ‌kraki of the Danes fled Uppsala ​with treasure taken from King Aðils ‌and sowed the gold along the ​Fyrisvellir, the plain of the river ‌Fyris, so that the pursuing Swedes ‌stopped to gather it. Aðils ​himself stooped from his horse for ​the ring Svíagriss — and Hrólfr ​declared he had now bent low ​the mightiest man in Sweden. The ‌same dynasty rides through Beowulf, where ‌Eadgils son of Ohthere is king ​of the Scylfings.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Uppsala's closest ​kin are not ‌gods but holy ‌places. Thietmar of ​Merseburg, writing two ​generations before Adam, ​describes the ​nine-year sacrifice at ​Lejre in Denmark ‌— ninety-nine men, ​horses, dogs and ​cocks offered to ‌placate the gods ​— the Danish ‌counterpart of the ​Uppsala rite, and ‌the proof that ​Adam's nine-year cycle ​was pan-Scandinavian grammar ​rather than ​local invention.[1] Behind ​both stands the ‌deepest stratum: Tacitus' ​Germania records the ‌sacred grove of ​the Semnones, entered ‌bound and widened ​by human sacrifice, ‌and the grove ​and wagon of ‌Nerthus — the ​goddess whose ​name is the ‌feminine counterpart of ‌Njörðr, father of ‌the Freyr whose ​seat was Uppsala.[2]

​The enthroned triad ‌itself has been ​read since Dumézil ​against the archaic ‌Roman triad of ​Jupiter, Mars and ​Quirinus — sovereignty, ‌war, and ​the fertile commons ‌— though Adam's ​own glosses (Thor ​as Jove, Wodan ​as Mars, Fricco ​as peace and ‌pleasure) show the ‌comparison began with ​the medieval churchman, ‌not the modern ​comparatist. Modern handbooks ​urge matching caution: ‌Adam's testimony ‌is secondhand, channelled ‌through Danish informants ‌a kingdom away ​from the Swedish ‌cult he wanted ​abolished, and Simek, ​Orchard, and Lindow ​treat the account ‌as the fullest ​and most dangerous ​source for Norse ​religion.[3] What ‌remains undisputed is ​the place: no ​other site in ​the North is ‌so continuously written ​about, from the ‌royal mounds' century ​to the last ‌generation of the ​old religion. Within ‌the corpus, its ‌related figures are ​the triad ​it enshrined — ​Þórr, Óðinn, Freyr ​— and the ‌Vanir homeland Vanaheimr ‌from which Freyr's ​house came.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Uppsala never ​stopped being ‌the stage ‌on which ​Sweden remembers ​itself. The ​old cult ​centre became ​the church's: ‌Gamla Uppsala ​was the ​seat of Sweden's ‌first archbishopric ​from 1164 ‌until the ​see moved ‌to Östra ​Aros — ​the modern ​city of ​Uppsala — ​in 1273, ‌and the medieval ​church still ‌stands on ​the artificial ‌plateau where ​Adam's temple ‌is sought.[1]

​Nineteenth-century romantic ‌nationalism made ​the mounds ​the nation's ‌ancestral shrine; their ‌excavation in ‌1846–47, ordered ​to still ​public argument ‌about what ​they contained, ​yielded the ‌cremated kings ​of the ​Vendel era ‌and made Old ​Uppsala the ‌birthplace of ​Swedish field ​archaeology. The ​university at ​Uppsala, founded ‌in 1477, ‌gave the ​name a ‌second, learned ​fame that now ​outruns the ‌temple's. For ‌modern scholarship ‌the site ‌remains the ​test case ‌for the ​union of ​cult and ​kingship in ‌the North — ​Olof Sundqvist's ​Freyr's Offspring ​is built ‌on its ​ground — ​and for ​modern Heathen ‌and Ásatrú ​communities the ‌grove and ​the mounds are ‌the north's ​most numinous ‌standing landscape, ‌the one ​address where ​the old ​religion can ​still be ‌visited rather ‌than merely ​read about.[2]

Sources

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Uppsala is ​the rare ‌Norse holy place ‌where the ​texts can ​be tested against ​the ground. ​The three royal ​mounds of ‌Gamla Uppsala — ​the largest ​barrows in ‌Sweden — were ​opened in ‌1846–47 under Bror ​Emil Hildebrand: ‌beneath each lay ​a cremation ​of the ​sixth or seventh ​century, burned ​in its finery ‌and sealed ​under birch bark, ‌with weapons, ​game equipment, and ‌sacrificed animals ​— the ‌graves of the ​dynasty the ‌poems call Ynglings ​or Scylfings.[1]

​Beside them the ‌medieval church ‌— seat ‌of Sweden's first ​archbishop from ​1164 — stands ‌on a ​great artificial plateau ​of levelled ‌terraces, the ​one construction on ​the site ‌that matches Adam's ​scale. Sune ‌Lindqvist's excavation beneath ​the church ​floor in ​1926 revealed the ​postholes of ‌a very large ‌wooden building, ​and the argument ‌whether it ​was the ​pagan temple, a ‌royal feasting ‌hall, or both ‌has run ‌ever since; Olof ​Sundqvist's work ‌on the ​Svea cult-and-kingship complex ​is the ​standard modern frame.[2] ‌The surrounding ​landscape is dense ​with the ​dynasty's furniture: boat-graves ‌at Valsgärde ​a few ​miles north, the ​Vendel graves ‌across the river, ​hundreds of ‌smaller mounds on ​the Fyris ‌plain. No ​runestone carves the ‌place-name itself; ‌the Younger Futhark ​rendering ᚢᛒᛒᛋᛅᛚᛅ ​(ubbsala) is a ​normalized reconstruction, ​though Uppland ‌is the richest ‌runestone province ​in Scandinavia.[3]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​surviving eddic ‌poem names ‌Uppsala: the ​Codex Regius ​knows the ​gods Adam ​saw ​enthroned there, ‌but not ​the temple ​itself — ‌a silence ​that reminds ‌us ​the poems ‌were Iceland's ​inheritance, while ​the sanctuary ​was Sweden's. ​What ​the poems ‌supply is ​the frame ‌of the ​cult. Grímnismál, ‌in its ​numbered ‌survey of ​heaven's dwellings, ‌gives Freyr ​his hall: ​the gods ‌gave him ‌Álfheimr ‌as a ​tooth-gift in ​days of ‌old — ​the eddic ​anchor for ‌the ​god whose ​earthly seat, ‌by Snorri's ​account, was ‌the temple ​at ​Uppsalir.[1]

The ​deeper eddic ​context is ‌the Vanir ‌theology the ​Uppsala triad ‌embodied. ​Lokasenna stages ​Freyja and ‌Freyr among ‌the Æsir ‌as hostages ‌of the ​older ‌divine family, ​and the ​myth of ​Vanaheimr — ‌the Vanir ​homeland from ​which ​Freyr's father ‌Njörðr came ​— stands ​behind every ​account of ‌the ​fertility cult ‌that Adam ​describes enshrined ‌at the ​Upper Halls. ‌The poems ‌do ​not name ​the place; ​they name ​the gods ‌the place ‌was built ​for.[2]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 5 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Uppsala, Freyr.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌names Sweden ‌but not ​the temple: ​Snorri's euhemerized ​account in ​Gylfaginning brings ​Óðinn north ‌to settle ​among the ​Swedes and ‌founds the ​northern priesthood ‌at Sigtuna, ​while the ‌great temple ​account of ​the Swedish ​centre belongs ​to Adam ​of Bremen ‌and to ​Snorri's own ‌Heimskringla.[1] What ​the Edda ‌does give ​Uppsala is ‌one of ​its ‌most famous ​kennings. In ​Skáldskaparmál's catalogue ‌of the ‌names of ‌gold, Snorri ​explains why ​gold may ‌be called ​'Kraki's seed': ​King Aðils ‌at Uppsala ​pursued the ​fleeing Hrólfr ‌kraki across ​the Fyrisvellir, ‌and Hrólfr ​sowed the ​stolen gold ​on the ​plain so ‌that the ‌Swedes stopped ​to gather ‌it — ​the seed ​of the ‌Danish king ‌scattered under ‌the ‌walls of ​the Upper ‌Halls.[2]

The ​pairing is ​characteristic: the ​Prose Edda, ‌a poet's ​handbook, remembers ​Uppsala as ​the place ‌where gold ​fell like ​grain; Heimskringla, ​a king's ‌history, remembers ​it as ‌the place ​where a ‌god became ​an estate. ‌Between the ‌two Snorris ​the site ​holds its ​double identity ​— sanctuary ‌and treasury, ‌temple and ​throne.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning, trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the kenning 'Kraki's seed'), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​Viking-Age runestone ‌carves ‌the place-name ​Uppsalir in ​any ​attested spelling. ​The ​runic corpus ‌of Uppland ​— ​the richest ‌runestone ​province in ‌Scandinavia, ​with well ‌over a ​thousand ​inscriptions — ​records ​the dead, ​their ‌bridges, and ​their estates, ‌but ​not the ‌sanctuary ​on the ‌ridge; the ​name's ‌runic rendering ​is ​therefore a ‌normalized ‌reconstruction rather ‌than a ​witnessed ​form.[1]

What ‌can ​be said ​runologically ‌is the ​rendering itself: ​in ‌the Younger ​Futhark ‌the name ​would have ​been ​carved ᚢᛒᛒᛋᛅᛚᛅ ​(ubbsala). ‌The sixteen-rune ‌row ​underspecifies heavily ‌here: ᚢ ​(úr) ​writes all ‌the ‌rounded back ‌vowels ‌and /w/; ​ᛒ (bjarkan) ‌writes ​both /b/ ​and ​/p/, so ‌the carving ​cannot ​distinguish the ​doubled ‌voiceless stop ​the ​manuscripts write ​pp; ᛋ ‌(sól) ​writes /s/; ‌and ​the row ‌marks ​no vowel ‌length at ‌all. ​The manuscript ​spelling ​of the ​medieval texts ‌remains ‌the primary ​witness.[2]

Sources

  1. Jansson, Sven B. F., Runes in Sweden, trans. Peter Foote (Stockholm: Gidlunds, 1987).
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Uppsala's ‌medieval afterlife ‌belongs to ​the kings' ​sagas and the ​legendary cycle. ​Snorri's Ynglingasaga ​makes the ‌site the sacral ​capital of ​the Swedish ‌dynasty: Freyr ​builds the ‌temple, endows the ​Uppsala wealth, ‌and lies ​in a ​mound there; ​his descendants the ​Ynglings rule ​from the ‌ridge for ​generations, and the ‌phrase 'the ​Uppsala wealth' ‌becomes shorthand ​for the ‌crown estate itself.[1]

​Hrólfs saga ‌kraka sets ​the dynasty's ​most famous feud ‌on the ‌same ground: ‌King Aðils ​at Uppsala, ​his Danish rival ‌Hrólfr kraki, ​and the ​flight across ‌the Fyrisvellir ​with the gold ​sown behind ‌— the ​saga that ‌Snorri's kenning 'Kraki's ​seed' presupposes. ​Aðils keeps ​the hall ​at Uppsala ‌to the end ‌of his ​strange life, ‌a king ​remembered for ​sorcery, sacrifice, and ‌the ring ‌Svíagriss.[2]

The ‌same royal ‌house rides through ​Beowulf, where ‌the Swedish ​kings of ​the Scylfing ​line — Ongenþeow, ‌Onela, Eadgils ​— feud ​with the ​Geats in the ‌generation the ​mounds were ​raised; the ​identification of ‌Beowulf's Eadgils with ​the sagas' ‌Aðils of ​Uppsala is ‌one of ​the oldest convictions ‌of northern ‌scholarship, and ​the one ​that ties the ​Upper Halls ​to the ‌greatest poem ‌of the ​Germanic North.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Heimskringla, Ynglingasaga 10 and passim, trans. Lee M. Hollander (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1964).
  2. Hrólfs saga kraka, trans. Jesse L. Byock (London: Penguin, 1998).
  3. Beowulf (Eadgils, king of the Scylfings), trans. Seamus Heaney (London: Faber, 1999); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Uppsala.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Every other ​sanctuary of ‌the Norse ‌world survives ​as a ​sentence or ​a stone; ​Uppsala survives ​as a ‌chapter. Adam ​of Bremen, ​wanting it ‌torn down, ​preserved it ‌— the ​gold on ‌the beams, ​the sceptre ​in Thor's fist, ​the disgraceful ​songs he ​would not ‌quote, the ​seventy-two bodies ‌a Christian ​eyewitness counted ‌in the ​grove. Snorri, ‌wanting kings, ​gave it ‌a god ​for a ​founder and ‌a mound ‌for the ‌god. The excavators, ​wanting proof, ​found postholes ‌under the ​church floor ​and a ‌hall where ​the temple ​should be. ‌Each witness ​buries the ‌last, and ​the place ​absorbs them ​all, as ​it absorbed ‌its offerings.[1]

‌The Upper ​Halls are the ‌North's one ​complete lesson ​in how ‌a holy ‌place works: ‌the land, ‌the wealth, ​the king, ‌and the ​god are ​a single ​estate, renewed ‌every ninth ​year in ​nine-day blood, ​and written ‌down only at ​the moment ​of its ​ending. To ‌read the ​Uppsala record ‌is to ​watch a ‌whole religion ​hold its ‌last festival ‌— and ​to find, ​eighteen centuries ​on, that ​the grove ‌has not ‌entirely stopped ​being visited.[1]

Sources

  1. Adam of Bremen, Gesta Hammaburgensis ecclesiae pontificum 4.26–27 with scholia, trans. Tschan, rev. Reuter (2002).
