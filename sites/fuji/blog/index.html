Overview

Fuji (fuji) — Sacred Mountain — belongs to the Japanese tradition. ​The name means "Wealthy warrior".

Mount Fuji is the sacred mountain of Japan, an object of worship since at least the Nara period and the most reproduced image of the country.

PuniCodex ​restores the name as Fuji. The plain ASCII form fuji is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual ​form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is treated as a plain form. The Unicode form ​remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 富士. Etymologically it means "Wealthy ‌warrior".

The name Fuji is of uncertain etymology. One influential theory derives it from the Ainu fuchi or huchi, meaning "fire" or "old woman," referring to the volcano's fire and the fire ​goddess. Another derives it from Old Japanese fushi or fuji, a wisteria-like plant, or from a phrase meaning "wealthy warrior." The mountain has been an object of worship since at least the ​Nara period.

The ASCII form fuji survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration ‌Fuji gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is a simpler restoration because the restoration does not ​add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 富士 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical ​Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Fuji (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //ɸɯ.ʑi//.

The characters ‌富士 are ateji, now read for their auspicious meanings: 富 (wealth) and 士 (warrior, gentleman). The mountain's name predates the writing system and was assigned these graphs because of their sound and ​favorable associations.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /ɸɯ.ʑi/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'FUJI' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid ​English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Fuji is a simpler restoration because the ASCII form fuji already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length ​mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short syllable receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as ​separate morae. The name Fuji therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English speaker stresses one syllable heavily. Mythology

The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki connect the volcano to the fire deity Kagutsuchi: when Izanagi killed Kagutsuchi with his sword, the blood that fell on the rocks created other ‌kami, and the fire god's body was said to be buried or transformed into mountains. Later tradition identifies the mountain itself as the abode of the kami Konohana-sakuya-hime and Ōyamatsumi, and the ​object of the Asama or Sengen cult. The Konjaku Monogatari and Fuji-kō literature describe ecstatic ascents and visions of paradise from the summit.

The narrative of Fuji participates in the larger mythic ‌program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles ​were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of fuji illustrates ‌how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

The Name and Its Power (Name lore)

Folk etymology explains the name ​as fushi, 'without equal,' or fuji, 'inexhaustible' — both appropriate to a mountain with no rival on the horizon. In the early chronicles the peak's deity was treated as female, a kami ‌of fire, and in the medieval period she was identified with Sengen (Asama-no-kami) and with Konohanasakuya-hime, the blossom princess of the Kojiki, whose marriage to the heavenly grandson Ninigi bound heaven's line ​to the earth. When Fuji smoked or rumbled, the interpretation was immediate: the goddess of the volcano was stirring, and the province looked to its Sengen shrines. The shrine cult kept the ​mountain's divinity local and specific — not an abstraction like 'nature,' but a named power with festivals, priests, and a genealogy reaching back to the age of the gods. The mountain's own ‌name became the nation's shorthand for what is highest, purest, and most alone.

Fuji as Lotus Mandala (Fuji-kō)

In the medieval imagination Fuji was not climbed so much as entered. Buddhist exegetes ‌mapped the mountain as a lotus mandala, its ridges the lotus petals, its crater the heart of the world; ascending it was circumambulation and pilgrimage at once. In the sixteenth century the ‌ascetic Hasegawa Kakugyō is credited with founding the Fuji-kō confraternities, and by the Edo period tens of thousands of pilgrims climbed in white robes reciting sutras, carrying miniature shrines up the stations ​and building fujizuka — small replica Fujis — in the cities, so that those who could not travel could still 'climb' the mountain. Some confraternities worshipped the crater itself as the Pure ​Land. The practice reveals the mountain's genius as an image: Fuji is endlessly reproducible, and every reproduction is still Fuji — a mandala you can ascend with your feet, a paradise with ‌a weather problem.

The Floating World's Mountain (Hokusai / Hiroshige)

Between about 1830 and 1850 the ukiyo-e masters fixed Fuji's image for the world. Hokusai's Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji placed the ​same snow-cap behind the Great Wave, a lumberyard, a tea-house balcony, a thunderstorm — proving the mountain was a measuring stick for every human activity. Hiroshige answered with travel series in which ​Fuji appears at a roadside halt, over a river, beyond a station bell. The prints used the newly imported Prussian blue so lavishly that the pigment itself became a fashion, and when ‌Japan opened, these images were among the first things the West saw and imitated. The lesson of the series is visual relativism: Fuji never changes, but every viewpoint is a different picture. ​The mountain became the constant against which a whole floating world arranges and rearranges itself — and the proof that a sacred image, printed a hundred thousand times, loses none of its ​sanctity.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Snow-capped cone — the symmetrical volcanic form, icon of Japan par excellence.
  • Sakura and wisteria — floral symbols associated with the Fuji cult and spring pilgrimage.
  • Lava caves ‌and springs — the mountain's fire and water are both sacred; the Oshino Hakkai springs are thought to be melted snow from Fuji.
  • Red torii and sōmen — pilgrims wear white ​robes and conical hats, carrying a staff and bell.
  • Torii and shrine markers — The network of Sengen shrines, of which Fujisan Hongū Sengen Taisha is the head, that manages the mountain's ‌cult
  • Crater and rising sun — The summit caldera and the dawn viewed from it — the goraikō, the honored arrival of the sun, central to pilgrimage
  • Pilgrim's staff — The walking ​stick and stamped robe of those who climbed the sacred slopes, from Fuji-kō confraternities to today

Archaeology & Evidence

The summit of Fuji preserves more than a hundred okunoin sacred sites, shrines, ​and the Konohana-sakuya-hime shrine rebuilt in the modern period. The Yoshida-guchi and Subashiri trail systems, the Ōmuro sect headquarters, and numerous Fujizuka mounds in the Kantō plain provide a dense archaeological and ​landscape record of the Fuji cult.

Realm & Domain

Mount Fuji is the sacred mountain of Japan, the highest peak in the country, and a shintai — a natural body in which ​a kami dwells. Its domain encompasses fire, water, fertility, and the boundary between this world and the other world. Pilgrimage (Fuji-kō) and the veneration of the mountain as a deity (Sengen) have ‌shaped Japanese religion, art, and literature for more than a thousand years.

Volcano Deity

As a living volcano, Fuji has been feared and worshipped as a source of both destruction and fertility.

‌Pilgrimage Peak

For centuries pilgrims climbed to the summit, where shrines and lodges marked the path.

National Symbol

Its image identifies Japan in art, currency, passports, and global imagination.

Artistic Muse

Hokusai's ​Thirty-Six Views made Fuji an icon of world art.

Across Cultures

Fuji worship blends Shinto (Sengen or Asama shrines), Buddhism (Fuji-san cults and the founder Hasegawa Kakugyō), and folk asceticism. The Fuji-kō ‌confraternities of the Edo period, founded by Kakugyō and later systematized by Jikigyō Miroku, organized mass pilgrimages and built miniature Fujis (Fujizuka) in towns across Japan. The mountain also attracted Shugendō practitioners, ​who saw it as an axis mundi.

Cultural Legacy

Mount Fuji is a UNESCO World Heritage site and the most reproduced image in Japanese art, from Hokusai's Thirty-Six Views to contemporary postcards ​and corporate logos. It continues to be climbed by hundreds of thousands of pilgrims and tourists each summer and remains the national symbol of Japan.
