01

Mythology

Stories of Manannán

Manannán moves through Irish myth like the tide: now distant, now suddenly present, always at the boundary between worlds. He is not a creator or a warrior king but the guardian of passages — between island and island, between the visible Ireland and the hidden one — and the keeper of the treasures that let a hero cross. His dossier spans the voyage-tale, the adventure-tale, the fosterage-tale, and the weapon-lore of the whole cycle.

The Voyage

The Chariot on the Wave-Plain

In Immram Brain, the Voyage of Bran, the god meets a mortal expedition on its own element. Bran mac Febail had heard music behind him until he slept, and woke to find a silver branch with white blossoms at his hand; a strange woman sang to him of the Land of Women until he put to sea with three companies of nine. Out on the ocean a chariot came toward the boat, and the charioteer hailed Bran across the water — it was Manannán mac Lir, driving over what Bran took for the sea and the god took for a flowery plain. His chariot wheels were the chariot-wheels of the plain; the speckled salmon in the wave were calves and lambs to him; and from the driver's seat he sang the poem of the crossing, naming the islands Bran would pass and promising the harbour ahead.

The scene is the whole of his theology in one image: the crossing is not a change of place but of sight. What is sea to the mortal is meadow to the god, and the boat that labours through the wave is, from the far side, a chariot travelling home. Every immram is a lesson in looking twice at the water.

The Branch

The Silver Apples of the Land of Promise

Echtra Cormaic maic Airt i Tír Tairngiri sends him into Tara itself. A warrior appeared at Cormac mac Airt's court bearing a branch with three silver apples that played sweet music; for it the king gave, one by one, his wife and his son and his daughter — and then grieved. A grey-haired man with a shining branch led him through a mist into the Land of Promise, where four palaces stood and a pig was roasted and renewed every night, and where an aged couple kept an ever-full cauldron: 'the world is a wave,' the old man told him, 'ebbing and flowing.' Manannán showed him the cup of truth that split into three pieces when three lies were told beneath it, and when Cormac's own tale of loss proved true the cup healed whole again; then the god kept the true cup for himself and sent the king home with its likeness, his family riding behind him.

The tale is a merchant's ledger written as myth: every exchange cost exactly what it revealed. Cormac traded his dearest three for a music he could not keep, and was paid back only when his word was tested and found true — Manannán's commerce is never in gold but in the truth of the bargain.

The Fosterage

The Keeper of the Hollow Hills

Altram Tige Dá Medar, the Fosterage of the House of the Two Methers, gives him his strangest office: landlord of the gods. When the sons of Míl divided Ireland among mortals, Manannán counselled the Túatha Dé Danann not to contest the division but to take the sídhe — the hollow hills beneath the land — and he himself allotted each god his mound: the Dagda his Brug, Bodb Derg the síd of Munster, Midir his Brí Léith. He became foster-father to the children of the gods — [[aengus|Óengus]] among them — and his mist, the féth fíada, hid the hidden Ireland from human eyes. His own dwelling he kept apart, at Emain Ablach, the Isle of Apple-trees, in the sea to the west where no storm dies down.

The sea-god turns out to be the manager of the transition between sovereignties — the one power both peoples can deal with. Mortals inherit the surface; the gods retire to the hills; and Manannán, who belongs to neither element entirely, keeps the paperwork of both. He is the diplomat of the two Irelands, and the mist is his treaty.

The Treasures

The Answerer, the Wave-Sweeper, and the Seven Pigs

His wealth is passage, and the cycle spends it everywhere. To Lugh, the tradition says, he gave the sword Fragarach, the Answerer, from whose point no one escaped and which opened every gate; his horse Aonbharr ran over land and sea alike; his boat Sguaba Tuinne answered thought and needed no sail; his crane-bag opened only at the full of the tide. In the late tale Oidheadh Chlainne Tuireann, Lugh's eric against his father's killers demands still more from the god's household: the seven pigs of Manannán, killed and eaten each night and found whole each morning, went on the quest-list, and the three sons of Tuireann won them with the rest. When the sons returned broken and died at Cian's grave, the treasures stayed in the world; the god's purse was never the poorer, because everything he lends comes home.

Read the inventory and the doctrine appears: there is no treasure of Manannán's that does not carry somebody somewhere. Sword, horse, boat, bag, pigs — even the cup of truth is a vessel. A god of the threshold gives only instruments of crossing, and keeps nothing but the sea.

02

Lord of the Sea and Mist

Guardian of the Irish Otherworld

Manannán mac Lir — 'son of the Sea', glossed so in Sanas Cormaic — is the great god of the wave, the mist, and the threshold. He rules the waters and weather around Ireland, keeps the Land of Promise hidden behind the féth fíada, and acts as foster-father and quartermaster to the Túatha Dé Danann — appearing where the known world ends to explain what lies beyond it.

Sea Lord

The sea took its name from him or he from it — Sanas Cormaic glosses Manannán as mac mara, 'son of the sea' — and the waters and weather around Ireland answer to him.

Otherworld Guardian

The féth fíada, the cloak of mist, hides Tír Tairngire and the hollow hills from mortal eyes — and it is his.

Treasure-Giver

He arms the heroes: Fragarach the Answerer, the horse Aonbharr, the boat Sguaba Tuinne, the crane-bag that opens only at the full of the tide.

Shape-Shifter

He comes as warrior, fisherman, and grey-haired beggar — in Echtra Cormaic an old man with a staff leads the king through the mist to the Land of Promise.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

Manannán mac Lir

The name in its original Celtic form. Manannán (Manannán mac Lir) is attested in the source tradition — “Son of the sea (from Old Irish Manannán)”. Its acute stress marks carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

manannan

Reduced to plain manannan, the name loses everything that made it specific: acute stress marks. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Manannán

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Manannán restores acute stress marks, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Manannán.com → xn--manannn-mwa.com

The non-ASCII characters in Manannán are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Manannán.

Pronunciation

How Manannán was spoken

/ˈmanˠənˠaːnˠ/ Old Irish Reconstruction
M- Voiced bilabial nasal [m], broad — the plain 'm' of English 'man'.
-a- Short open [a], the unstressed first vowel; Old Irish carries its stress at the front of the word.
-nann- Broad nasal [n̪ˠ], short open [a], and a second broad [n̪ˠ] — the doubled letter the medieval spelling keeps.
-án Long open [aː] marked by the síneadh fada, closing the name with a broad [n̪ˠ] — the length the temple's Tier 1 rests on.

Modern Approximation

'MAH-nuh-nawn' — stress the first syllable, keep the middle vowels short and plain, and lengthen the final 'awn'. The Anglicized retellings say 'man-ə-NAWN'; Old Irish would not have.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Manannan is Tier 1 by the mechanical rule: the temple form Manannán carries a síneadh fada on the final á, a distinctive feature plain ASCII strips. The name is traditionally explained as 'son of the sea' (mac Lir), and the reconstruction marks initial stress, as Old Irish demands; the double n simply belongs to the medieval spelling.

Etymology

Irish Manannán, traditionally 'son of the sea' (mac Lir); the first element may connect to the Isle of Man.

Meaning

Sea, otherworld, mist, and liminality; a guide between worlds.

From original to transliteration

  1. Old Irish was written in the Latin alphabet using Insular minuscule from the early medieval period.
  2. The name appears in the Ulster Cycle and Mythological Cycle as Manannán mac Lir, 'son of Lir (the sea)'.
  3. The acute accent on the final á marks a long vowel in modern scholarly convention.
  4. The Unicode restoration Manannán preserves the long vowel; the ASCII form flattens the name.

Extended Lore

Manannán is the god of the horizon. He does not rule the land; he rules the mist that hides the land beyond the land, and his household — the Answerer, the Wave-Sweeper, the horse Aonbharr, the crane-bag — is an inventory of crossings. To meet him is to discover that the boundary you thought was an end is actually a door: the sea that separates Ireland from Tír na nÓg is the same sea that brings the fog, the fish, and the stranger.
