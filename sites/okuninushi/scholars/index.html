01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ōkuninushi (okuninushi) — Nation-building, Marriage · Great land master — belongs to the Japanese tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Nation-building, Marriage". The name means "Great land master"[1].

Ōkuninushi — the Great Land-Master — is the earthly sovereign of Japanese myth: the heir of Susanoo who subdued wild Izumo, built it into a nation with the dwarf-god Sukunabikona, and codified medicine, agriculture and the rites of marriage. His was the visible world — Ashihara no Nakatsukuni, the Central Land of Reed-Plains — until he ceded it to the heavenly line of Amaterasu in the act of statecraft the chronicles call kuni-yuzuri, the transfer of the land.[2]

PuniCodex restores the name as Ōkuninushi and serves its temple at ōkuninushi.com. The restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses, and exactly one historically valid Unicode restoration exists, which places the name in Tier 1. The plain ASCII form okuninushi survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete[2].

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 大国主. Etymologically it means "Great land master"[1].

The ASCII form okuninushi survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Ōkuninushi recovers the vowel length of the original directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses, and exactly one historically valid Unicode restoration exists, which places the name in Tier 1.

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /oːkɯnʲiɲɯɕi/ — Japanese Reconstruction (Old Japanese Opo-kuni-nusi).[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ō- — from Old Japanese opo-, 'great, large' — the coalesced long vowel the macron preserves
  • -kuni- — 'land, province, realm' — written 国 in the chronicles, phonetic 久邇 in older spellings
  • -nushi — 主, 'lord, master, owner' — a standing title in divine names (cf. Kotoshironushi)

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'OH-koo-nee-NOO-shee' — five even morae; the long Ō is a doubled plain O, never an English diphthong; 'shi' is the soft Japanese shibilant.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Japanese — opo-kuni-nusi, 'great-land-master' — the 8th-century pre-coalescence form attested in the Kojiki
  • Alternate name — Ōnamuchi-no-kami (大己貴命), 'Great-Name-Possessor' — his second primary name in the Kojiki cycle
  • Folk reading — Daikoku-sama — the vernacular reading of 大国 that later fused him with the Buddhist Daikokuten

Tier-1: the restoration Ōkuninushi preserves the long vowel (macron Ō) that the ASCII Okuninushi erases — the very feature that marks Ō- 'great' as distinct from a plain o-. One preserved feature, mechanically verifiable: a single-tier Tier-1 name.

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗

06

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography associated with Ōkuninushi concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:[1]

  • White hare of Inaba — His first patient and his prophet: the flayed hare of Cape Keta whose healing proved Ōkuninushi's medicine and foretold his marriage to Yakami-hime; emblem of the province of Inaba.
  • The great shimenawa — The colossal sacred rope hung across the halls of Izumo-taisha — among the largest in Japan — marking the boundary of his enshrined presence.
  • Susanoo's sword, bow and lute — The regalia of lordship he carried out of Ne-no-Kuni with Susanoo's blessing — the storm-god's own charter for his rule over the land.
  • The tiny boat of Sukunabikona — The skiff of Metaplexis pod-husks (ama-no-kagami-no-fune) and the moth-skin cloak of the dwarf-god, emblem of the small partner in nation-building.
  • Herbs and the mortar — The pharmacology he and Sukunabikona devised for gods and men — beginning with the remedy of fresh water and katami (cattail) pollen prescribed for the hare.
  • The high pillars of Izumo — The towering hall promised at the kuni-yuzuri, to be raised with pillars 'high as the heaven of the gods' — his eternal seat after the surrender of the land.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ōkuninushi's cycle, preserved in the Kojiki (712) and the Nihon Shoki (720), is the longest and most novelistic narrative in Japanese myth — a story of escape, ordeal, sovereignty and renunciation at the meeting-point of earth and heaven.[1]

The White Hare of Inaba (The First Prescription)

In the Kojiki (Book I), a hare from the isle of Oki tricks the wani sea-beasts into forming a bridge, is flayed for the deception, and meets the eighty cruel brothers of Ōnamuji, who mockingly prescribe a bath in salt sea-water — agony for its wounds. The youngest brother, Ōnamuji (later Ōkuninushi), advises instead a rinse in fresh river water and a roll in the pollen of katami, the cattail rush; the hare is healed. Grateful, the hare prophesies that Ōnamuji — not his eighty brothers — will win the hand of the princess Yakami-hime of Inaba. It is the first medical prescription in Japanese myth, and the deed that marks the future land-master out from his rivals.[1]

The Trials of Ne-no-Kuni (The Ordeals Below)

Fleeing his brothers' repeated attempts to kill him, Ōnamuji descends to Ne-no-Kuni, the root-land of Susanoo, as the Kojiki (Book I) relates. There he loves Susanoo's daughter Suseribime, and the storm-god tests him: a night in a hall of snakes, a night in a hall of centipedes and wasps — each survived through Suseribime's protective scarves — and a burning meadow where a mouse leads him to safety underground and retrieves the arrow Susanoo had fired. When Ōnamuji at last washes the great god's hair and steals away with Suseribime, Susanoo's sword, bow and heavenly lute, the pursuing god yields and proclaims him Ōkuninushi-no-kami, Great Land-Master, bidding him drive out his brothers and rule the land.

The Dwarf from Across the Sea (Nation-Builders)

While Ōkuninushi labours to order the land, the Kojiki (Book I) tells, a tiny god arrives across the waves in a skiff of kagami pod-husks, wearing the skin of a moth: Sukunabikona, son of the great producer Kamimusubi (the Nihon Shoki variants name Takamimusubi). The two make and consolidate the land together, and devise the arts of medicine and protective magic for gods, men and beasts. When the work is done, Sukunabikona departs for Tokoyo no kuni, the far land beyond the sea, leaving Ōkuninushi to guard the realm they built.

Kuni-yuzuri — the Transfer of the Land (The Great Abdication)

When Amaterasu resolves that her line shall rule the Central Land of Reed-Plains, the Kojiki (Book I; cf. the Nihon Shoki, Age of the Gods scrolls) records that the warrior-god Takemikazuchi is sent to Izumo, where he thrusts his sword into the shore and demands Ōkuninushi's answer. The land-master defers to his sons: Kotoshironushi consents at once, while Ajisuki-takahikone resists and is subdued in a divine wrestling-bout. Ōkuninushi then cedes the land — on terms: a grand palace is to be built for him with pillars raised high and crossbeams thick, and his worship is to be perpetuated by the heavenly line itself. That promised palace is Izumo no Ōyashiro, the Grand Shrine of Izumo, and the treaty-in-myth is remembered as kuni-yuzuri.

  1. Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), Translated by W. G. Aston (excerpts), 720. ↗
08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Japan's gods met no Roman pantheon; Ōkuninushi's later travels ran through Buddhism instead. Through the folk reading Daikoku-sama of the characters 大国, he was identified with Daikokuten, the Japanese form of Mahākāla, becoming the smiling god of wealth and the household hearth — one of the Seven Gods of Fortune. At Izumo the older identity survived intact: there he remains the great matchmaker of en-musubi, the binding of marriage fates, and in the month the rest of Japan calls Kannazuki, 'the godless month', Izumo calls it Kami-ari-zuki — 'the month the gods are here', when the eight million kami convene at his shrine.[1]

Within the Japanese tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Fūjin, Hokkaidō, Honshū, Jizō, Kōbe, and Kyōto.

09

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Ōkuninushi still anchors the Izumo region of Shimane, where Izumo-taisha draws millions of pilgrims and couples across Japan pray to him as the nation's matchmaker. The white hare of Inaba endures as the emblem of Tottori prefecture, and his son Kotoshironushi presides over the great pilgrimage shrine of Kotohira in Shikoku. The word kuni-yuzuri itself remains the standard term for the peaceful cession of sovereignty — the god's abdication absorbed into the political vocabulary of Japan.[1]

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Izumo-taisha (Taisha-machi, Izumo, Shimane) is among the oldest and grandest shrines of Japan; the present main hall dates to 1744, but excavations in 2000 uncovered massive clustered pillar bases confirming medieval records of a towering earlier hall — the 'high pillars' the kuni-yuzuri myth demands. The region's cultic antiquity is independently attested by the 1984 Kojindani discovery, a ritual cache of 358 bronze swords, the largest single find of its kind in Japan.[1]

Kojiki & Nihon Shoki

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Ōnamuchi — the god who becomes Ōkuninushi, 'Master of the Great Land' — enters the Kojiki as the bullied youngest of the eighty brothers, carrying their baggage on the road to Inaba to woo Yakami-hime. His kindness to the flayed hare wins him the hare's prophecy and the bride; his brothers answer by killing him twice, and twice his mother wins his restoration from Kamimusubi.[1] In Susanoo's realm of Ne-no-Katasu-Kuni he survives the storm-god's trials — the snake chamber, the bees, the burning plain where a mouse shows him refuge — and escapes with Susanoo's life-sword, bow, and speaking koto, taking Suseri-hime with him.[2] With the dwarf god Sukunabikona he then 'made and solidified' the Central Land of Reed-Plains, establishing medicine.[2] The cycle closes in the kuni-yuzuri: Takemikazuchi, sent by Amaterasu, overawes his sons Kotoshironushi and Takeminakata, who flees to Suwa — and Ōnamuchi yields the visible realm in exchange for a great palace at Izumo and governance of hidden affairs.[3]

Sources

  1. Kojiki (712), Scroll 1 (Kamitsumaki): the hare of Inaba, the eighty brothers, and Ōnamuchi's two deaths and restorations through Kamimusubi.
  2. Kojiki, Scroll 1: the trials in Ne-no-Katasu-Kuni and the nation-building (kuni-zukuri) with Sukunabikona. Trans. Donald L. Philippi, Kojiki (University of Tokyo Press, 1968).
  3. Nihon Shoki (720), Scrolls 1–2 (Jindai no maki), with variants: the kuni-yuzuri, Futsunushi accompanying Takemikazuchi. Trans. W. G. Aston, Nihongi (1896).

Shinto Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Izumo-taisha — formally Izumo Ōyashiro, the 'Great Shrine' — stands in Taisha-machi, Shimane, and presents itself as the palace promised in the kuni-yuzuri, its taisha-zukuri hall held to preserve Japan's oldest shrine architecture.[1] Its calendar inverts the nation's: the tenth lunar month, elsewhere Kannazuki, 'the month without gods,' is at Izumo Kamiarizuki, 'the month with the gods,' when the eight million kami gather to deliberate the year's bonds.[2] That assembly grounds Ōkuninushi's most famous modern cult: en-musubi, the 'tying of bonds.' Because his myth braids marriage (Suseri-hime, Nunakawa-hime), alliance, and the compact between seen and unseen worlds, Izumo-taisha is Japan's premier pilgrimage for matchmaking, its votive cords and plaques sought by petitioners of every tie.[3] The white hare of Inaba serves as the shrine's emblem, and the hereditary Senge priesthood claims descent from Amenohohi, the heavenly envoy who, the Nihon Shoki says, stayed at Izumo rather than return.[4]

Sources

  1. Izumo-taisha (Izumo Ōyashiro), Taisha-machi, Shimane: shrine tradition of the taisha-zukuri honden as the palace promised in the kuni-yuzuri.
  2. Kamiarizuki ('the month with the gods'), the tenth lunar month at Izumo, against the national Kannazuki — documented shrine calendar tradition.
  3. The en-musubi ('tying of bonds') cult of Izumo-taisha, Japan's premier matchmaking pilgrimage — documented shrine tradition.
  4. Nihon Shoki, Jindai no maki (Scrolls 1–2): Amenohohi's mission to Izumo; the Senge hereditary priesthood of Izumo-taisha claiming his descent.

Japanese Buddhist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The identification of Ōkuninushi with Daikokuten — Japan's form of the Indian Mahākāla — rests on a pun: 大黒, read Daikoku in the fortune-god's name, can equally be read Ōkuni, 'Great Land.' The syncretism is medieval and popular, not scriptural — the chronicles know no Daikoku — yet it redrew the god's face for a millennium.[1] Daikoku of the Seven Lucky Gods stands on rice bales, the wish-granting mallet uchide no kozuchi in hand, a rat at his side; the rat is no accident, for in the Kojiki a mouse saves Ōnamuchi from Susanoo's burning plain.[2] The bridge was half-built already in the Nihon Shoki, whose variant text names the plague-god Ōmononushi of Miwa as none other than Ōnamuchi — the chroniclers identifying gods within their own pantheon long before Buddhism arrived.[3] Under the doubled identity Ōkuninushi rules the unseen order while his Daikoku face dispenses very visible fortune — a division of labor the kuni-yuzuri had already scripted.

Sources

  1. The Daikokuten–Ōkuninushi identification: documented medieval syncretism on the Daikoku/Ōkuni reading of 大黒; Daikokuten (Mahākāla) among the Seven Lucky Gods (shichifukujin).
  2. Kojiki, Scroll 1: the burning-plain trial, in which a mouse guides Ōnamuchi to the refuge underground.
  3. Nihon Shoki, reign of Emperor Sujin (variant text, arufumi): 'Ōmononushi-no-kami is none other than Ōnamuchi-no-kami.'

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

The Ōkuninushi cycle is a treaty written as myth. The 8th-century mythographers of the Yamato court preserved, almost lovingly, a rival sovereignty — an earth-line descended from Susanoo that owned the visible world — and then sanctified its surrender to the sun-line of Amaterasu. What Ōkuninushi received in exchange is telling: not power but cult, the towering shrine and the empire's perpetual worship. He is therefore the god of everything the heavenly line does not do — healing, farming, matchmaking, the pragmatic order of the reed-plain. Many scholars read the cycle as encoding real negotiations between Yamato and the old Izumo region; whether charter or memory, it makes Ōkuninushi the most humane of sovereigns: a ruler who learned kingship through ordeals beneath the earth, and who gave up his kingdom so that his land — and his god — could endure.[1]

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗
