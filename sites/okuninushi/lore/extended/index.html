Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Ōkuninushi in Later Traditions

Japan's gods met no Roman pantheon; Ōkuninushi's later travels ran through Buddhism instead. Through the folk reading Daikoku-sama of the characters 大国, he was identified with Daikokuten, the Japanese form of Mahākāla, becoming the smiling god of wealth and the household hearth — one of the Seven Gods of Fortune. At Izumo the older identity survived intact: there he remains the great matchmaker of en-musubi, the binding of marriage fates, and in the month the rest of Japan calls Kannazuki, 'the godless month', Izumo calls it Kami-ari-zuki — 'the month the gods are here', when the eight million kami convene at his shrine.

Modern Legacy

Ōkuninushi still anchors the Izumo region of Shimane, where Izumo-taisha draws millions of pilgrims and couples across Japan pray to him as the nation's great matchmaker of en-musubi. The white hare of Inaba endures as the emblem of Tottori prefecture; his son Kotoshironushi presides over the great pilgrimage shrine of Kotohira in Shikoku; and his name survives in the Daikokuten of household luck and the Seven Gods of Fortune. The word kuni-yuzuri itself remains the standard Japanese term for the peaceful cession of sovereignty — a god's abdication absorbed whole into the political vocabulary of the nation.

In the Earth

Izumo-taisha (Taisha-machi, Izumo, Shimane) is among the oldest and grandest shrines of Japan; the present main hall dates to 1744, but excavations in 2000 uncovered massive clustered pillar bases confirming medieval records of a towering earlier hall — the 'high pillars' the kuni-yuzuri myth demands. The region's cultic antiquity is independently attested by the 1984 Kojindani discovery, a ritual cache of 358 bronze swords, the largest single find of its kind in Japan.

The Meditation

What Ōkuninushi means — beyond the dictionary

The Ōkuninushi cycle is a treaty written as myth. The 8th-century mythographers of the Yamato court preserved, almost lovingly, a rival sovereignty — an earth-line descended from Susanoo that owned the visible world — and then sanctified its surrender to the sun-line of Amaterasu. What Ōkuninushi received in exchange is telling: not power but cult, the towering shrine and the empire's perpetual worship. He is therefore the god of everything the heavenly line does not do — healing, farming, matchmaking, the pragmatic order of the reed-plain. Many scholars read the cycle as encoding real negotiations between Yamato and the old Izumo region; whether charter or memory, it makes Ōkuninushi the most humane of sovereigns: a ruler who learned kingship through ordeals beneath the earth, and who gave up his kingdom so that his land — and his god — could endure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Ōkuninushi, Nation-building, Marriage, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Ōkuninushi?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Ōkuninushi is /oːkɯnʲiɲɯɕi/ — approximately 'OH-koo-nee-NOO-shee' — five even morae; the long Ō is a doubled plain O, never an English diphthong; 'shi' is the soft Japanese sibilant.

02What does Ōkuninushi mean?

Ōkuninushi means Great land master in the japanese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Ōkuninushi?

Ōkuninushi is associated with White hare of Inaba (His first patient and his prophet: the flayed hare of Cape Keta whose healing proved Ōkuninushi's medicine and foretold his marriage to Yakami-hime; emblem of the province of Inaba.), The great shimenawa (The colossal sacred rope hung across the halls of Izumo-taisha — among the largest in Japan — marking the boundary of his enshrined presence.), Susanoo's sword, bow and lute (The regalia of lordship he carried out of Ne-no-Kuni with Susanoo's blessing — the storm-god's own charter for his rule over the land.), The tiny boat of Sukunabikona (The skiff of Metaplexis pod-husks (ama-no-kagami-no-fune) and the moth-skin cloak of the dwarf-god, emblem of the small partner in nation-building.), Herbs and the mortar (The pharmacology he and Sukunabikona devised for gods and men — beginning with the remedy of fresh water and katami (cattail) pollen prescribed for the hare.).

04Why restore Ōkuninushi in Unicode?

Plain ASCII okuninushi strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Ōkuninushi?

“The White Hare of Inaba” — Ōkuninushi's cycle, preserved in the Kojiki (712) and the Nihon Shoki (720), is the longest and most novelistic narrative in Japanese myth — a story of escape, ordeal, sovereignty and renunciation at the meeting-point of earth and heaven. Every stage of it, from the healed hare to the final treaty, teaches one art of rule. The full telling lives on the lore page.
