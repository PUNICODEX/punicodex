01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ymir (ymir) ​— Primordial ‌Giant, Chaos · ‌Scream / ​Roar — is ​the first ​living being of ​the Norse ​cosmos: the frost-giant ‌who quickened ​out of the ​dripping rime in ‌Ginnungagap, the ​ancestor of all ‌giants, and ​finally the raw ‌material of ​the world itself, ​slain by ​Óðinn, Vili, and ​Vé and ​made into earth, ‌sea, mountains, ​and sky.[1]

Old ‌Norse Ymir ​is most strongly ‌connected with ​the verb ymja, ‌'to scream, ​roar, resound' — ‌the Roarer ​— while a ​second line, ‌old and still ‌defended, derives the ‌name from ​Indo-European yemo-, 'twin', ​comparing Sanskrit ‌Yama; both Simek ​and de ​Vries register the ‌dispute, and ​this edition does ​not pretend ‌it is settled.[2] ​His mythology ‌is cosmogony entire: ​Völuspá opens ​its memory 'in ​early days, ​when Ymir dwelled', ‌Vafþrúðnismál gives ‌his birth from ​Élivágar's poisoned ‌rime and his ​self-begetting brood, Grímnismál ​catalogues the ‌world made from ‌his dismembered ‌body, and Hyndluljóð ‌states the ​doctrine that all ‌giants descend ​from him.[3]

PuniCodex ​restores the ​name as Ymir ‌— identical ​to the ASCII ​input, because ​normalized Old Norse ‌writes no ​diacritic in this ​name — ​which the project's ‌mechanical rule ​places in Tier ‌2. The ​plain-ASCII domain ymir.com ‌is registered to ​a third ‌party outside the ‌PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ​for release; ​this temple claims ​no domain ‌and lives at ‌its canonical ​lexicon address.[4]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ‌Ymir (nominative; genitive ​Ymis). Its etymology ​is genuinely contested ​between two defensible ​lines, and honesty ​requires both. The ‌stronger philological line ​derives it from ​the Old Norse ‌verb ymja, 'to ​scream, roar, resound' ‌— the name ​would mean ‌'the Roarer, the ​Screamer', a fitting ​name for the ​bellowing primordial being ​the giants call ​Aurgelmir, 'mud-bellow'.[1] The ‌alternative, favored in ​comparative scholarship, connects ‌Ymir with Indo-European ​yemo-, 'twin' — ‌Sanskrit Yama, Latin ​geminus — which ‌would suit the ​self-begetting, two-sexed primordial ‌from whose own ​body his children ​grew; de Vries, ‌Simek, and Lindow ‌all record the ‌dispute rather than ​resolve it.[2] The ​given gloss 'Twin, ‌screamer (from *ymijaz)' ​follows the first ​line; the second ‌remains a live ​scholarly option.

The ​ASCII form is ‌not a ​compromise here: normalized ‌Old Norse Ymir ​is already written ​in plain ASCII ​letters. There is ​no diacritic, no ‌thorn or eth, ‌no vowel-length mark ​for the early ‌domain-name system to ​have stripped. The ​name is classified ‌Tier 2 by ‌the project's mechanical ‌rule — the ‌restoration preserves nothing ​the ASCII form ‌loses — which ​is a statement ​about orthography, never ​about the being ‌who fills the ​world's first chapter.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • y ‌→ Y — ​Same, capitalized
  • ​m → m ​— Same

‌- i → ​i — Same
‌- r → ​r — Same

‌The project holds ​no domain for ‌this name: ymir.com ‌is registered to ​a third party ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈy.mir/ — ​Old Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • Y- ​— Close ​front rounded [y], ‌the vowel ​of German ü ​or French ‌u: lips pursed ​as for ‌'oo' while the ​tongue positions ‌for 'ee'; stressed, ​as every ​Old Norse word ​is, on ​the first syllable

​- -mir ‌— Voiced ​bilabial nasal [m], ‌short close ​front [i] as ‌in 'bit', ​and the trilled ‌alveolar [r] ​of the nominative ‌ending, sounded ​fully

For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest approximation ‌is: 'EE-meer' ‌spoken with rounded ​lips — ​form the mouth ‌for 'oo', ​say 'ee' inside ​it, then ‌a short 'i' ​and a ​lightly trilled final ‌r, as ​in modern Icelandic ‌Ymir.

Kindred ​and historical forms ​of the ​name:

  • Old ​Norse — ‌Ymir; the þulur ‌also name ​him Aurgelmir ('mud-bellow'), ‌Brimir, and ​Bláinn[2]
  • Old ​Norse (verb) ‌— ymja, 'to ‌scream, roar, ‌resound', the root ‌of the ​leading etymology
  • ‌Sanskrit — ​Yama, 'Twin', first ​mortal and ​king of the ‌dead, the ​comparandum of ​the alternative etymology[3]

​- Modern ‌Icelandic — Ymir, ​the name's ​unbroken living form

​Ymir is ‌Tier 2 by ​the mechanical ‌rule: the normalized ​Old Norse ‌form is already ​plain ASCII, ‌so the restoration ‌loses nothing ​in transit. The ​god-sized being ​outruns his small ​name — ‌a statement about ‌spelling, never ​about scale.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as Ymir ​— Old ​Norse (North Germanic), ​attested 13th ‌c. CE ​in the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Poetic Edda ​(GKS 2365 ‌4to) and ​in the Prose ‌Edda codices, ​recording material centuries ​older.[1] Before ​the Latin ​bookhand, Old Norse ​names were ‌carved in the ​Younger Futhark; ‌no runic attestation ​of this ‌name survives, ​so the manuscript ‌form is ​the primary witness, ‌and the ​reconstructed carving ​ᚢᛘᛁᛦ (úr for ‌y, maðr, ‌íss, and ýr ‌ᛦ for ​the nominative ​-r) is a ‌normalization, not ​an inscription.[2]

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

  • ​The name ‌is a simplex, ​not a ‌compound: Ymir, genitive ​Ymis, with ​the regular ​masculine nominative ending ​-r.
  • ‌The leading etymology ‌connects it ​to the ‌verb ymja, 'to ​scream, roar, ​resound' — the ‌Roarer; the ‌alternative derives ‌it from Indo-European ‌yemo-, 'twin', ​with Sanskrit Yama ‌as comparandum. ​The dispute ​is genuine and ​recorded in ‌the handbooks.[3]
  • ​Old Norse ​orthography writes the ​name with ‌four plain ​Latin letters; there ​is no ​diacritic for the ‌ASCII alphabet ​to have ‌lost.
  • The ​Unicode restoration ‌Ymir is therefore ​identical to ‌the ASCII ‌input — an ​honest Tier ​2 by the ​mechanical rule, ​and a ‌statement about orthography, ‌never about ​the being.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957); The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington (2014), introduction (manuscripts).
  2. Barnes, Michael P., Runes: A Handbook (Boydell Press, 2012).
  3. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Brill, 1962), s.v. Ymir; Cleasby & Vigfússon, s.v. Ymir, ymja.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ymir ​governs the ‌three headings ‌of his ​own existence: ​the primordial ​first-being, the ​frost out ​of which ‌he came, ​and the ​creation his ‌body became.[1]

​### The ‌Primordial

Before ​earth, sea, ‌or sky ​there was ​Ymir — ​Völuspá's first ​named being, ​the datum ‌from which ​the whole ‌Norse account ​of time ‌unfolds. He ​is not ‌a character ​with adventures; ‌he is ​the world's ​first fact.

‌### Frost

‌He condenses ‌from the ​rime of ​Élivágar where ‌it met ​the sparks ​of Múspell ‌in Ginnungagap ​— frost ​made flesh, ‌the ice-world ​given a ‌body, and ​the ancestor ​of the ​frost-giants who ​keep his ‌nature.

‌Creation

Slain ​by Óðinn, ‌Vili, and ​Vé, he ​becomes the ‌cosmos: flesh ‌earth, blood ‌sea, bones ‌mountains, hair ​trees, skull ‌sky, eyelashes ​the wall ​of Miðgarðr, ​brains the ‌driving clouds.[2]

​### The ​Giants' Ancestor

​Hyndluljóð states ‌it flatly ​— all ​giants descend ​from Ymir. ‌His brood, ​begotten from ‌his own ​body, is ‌the older ​generation of ‌the world's ‌substance, the ​kin the ​gods both ​fight and ​marry: Óðinn's ‌own grandmother ‌is the ​giantess Bestla.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 5–8, trans. Faulkes (1987); Hyndluljóð 33 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Ymir ​has no ‌cult regalia; ‌his 'symbols' ​are the ​fixed furniture ​of the ​cosmogony itself ​— the ‌places and ​beings the ​tradition cannot ‌tell his ​story without:[1]

‌- Ginnungagap ​— The ‌yawning void ​between the ​ice of ​Niflheimr and ​the fire ​of Múspell, ‌the ​unmade space ‌in which ​the first ‌being quickened
​- Élivágar ‌— 'The ​ice-waves', the ‌rivers whose ​poisoned drops ​froze and ‌grew until ‌a jotunn ‌arose from ​them (Vafþrúðnismál ​31)

  • ‌Auðhumla — ​The primeval ​cow whose ‌four rivers ​of milk ​fed Ymir, ‌and ​whose licking ‌of the ​salty rime-stones ​freed Búri, ​grandfather of ​the gods

‌- The ‌dismembered body ​— The ‌map of ​the world: ​flesh earth, ‌blood sea, ‌bones mountains, ‌skull sky ‌— anatomy ​as geography[2]
‌- The ​four sky-dwarfs ​— Norðri, ​Suðri, Austri, ‌and ​Vestri, set ​by the ​gods to ‌bear up ​the skull-vault ​of heaven ​at its ‌four corners
​- The ‌lúðr — ​The vessel ‌(chest or ​boat, the ‌word is ‌debated) on ​which Bergelmir ​and his ​wife rode ​out the ‌blood-flood that ‌drowned the ​frost-giants[3]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ymir's mythology is ​the Norse creation account entire: ‌he exists to be ‌first — first life, first ​giant, first death.[1]

Out ​of the Rime (Gylfaginning ​5–6; Vafþrúðnismál 20–21)

Before the ​world there was Ginnungagap, the ​yawning void, with the frozen ‌rivers of Niflheimr to ​the north and the sparks ​of Múspell to the south. ‌Where heat met rime, the ​hoar-frost melted and dripped, ‌and the drops quickened into ​the shape of a man: ‌Ymir, whom the frost-giants ​call Aurgelmir. Vafþrúðnismál gives the ​verse version — from Élivágar's ​waves shot poison-drops that froze ​and grew until a ​jotunn arose.[1]

The Self-Begetting ‌Giant (Vafþrúðnismál 29, 32–33)

Asked ​how Ymir got children when ‌he knew no giantess, ​Vafþrúðnir answers with the strangest ‌genealogy in the corpus: from ​under the giant's arm ‌grew a man and a ​woman together, and one leg ‌begot a six-headed son on ​the other. Hyndluljóð states ​the doctrine flatly — all ‌giants descend from Ymir.[2]

‌Auðhumla and Búri (Gylfaginning 6)

‌From the same dripping ​rime came the cow Auðhumla; ​four rivers of milk fed ‌the giant, and she ​licked the salty rime-stones until ​Búri emerged — hair the ‌first day, head the second, ​the whole man the ​third. Búri begot Borr, Borr ‌married the giantess Bestla, and ​their sons were Óðinn, Vili, ‌and Vé. Gods and ​giants rise from the same ​frost within days of one ​another.[1]

The Making ​of the World (Grímnismál 40–41; ‌Gylfaginning 8)

The three brothers ‌kill Ymir, and from his ​body they make the ‌world: from his flesh the ​earth, from his blood the ​sea, from his bones the ‌mountains, from his hair ‌the trees, from his skull ‌the sky; from his eyelashes ‌they built Miðgarðr as ​a defence against the giants, ‌and from his brains the ​heavy clouds. Snorri adds the ​maggots in the flesh, ​made rational as the dwarfs, ‌and the four dwarfs set ​to hold up the skull-vault. ​So much blood ran ​from the wound that the ‌frost-giants drowned in it — ​all but Bergelmir and ​his wife, who rode out ​the flood on their lúðr, ‌and from them the jotnar ​were born again.[3]

‌'When Ymir Dwelled' (Völuspá 3–5)

​The völva of Völuspá opens ‌her memory with him: 'It ​was in early days, ‌when Ymir dwelled; there was ‌no sand nor sea nor ​cool waves.' The poem ​never stages the killing; it ​places Ymir on the far ​side of a before and ‌an after — the ‌name Norse tradition gives the ​raw, unworked world.[4]

  2. Vafþrúðnismál 29, 32–33; Hyndluljóð 33 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014); Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 8, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  4. Völuspá 3–5 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Ymir stands ​at the ‌centre of ‌the best-known ​comparative debate ​in Germanic ​mythology: the ​Indo-European dismemberment ​cosmogony. The ‌Sanskrit parallel ​is exact ​in structure ‌— Rigveda ​10.90, the ‌Puruṣasūkta, makes ​the world ‌from the ​sacrifice of ​the primeval man ​Puruṣa, whose ​breath became ​wind, head ‌sky, feet ​earth — ‌and Bruce ​Lincoln's comparative ‌studies read ​Ymir as ‌the Norse ​reflex of ‌the same ​inherited creation ​pattern, complete ‌with the ‌'twin' and ‌'first sacrifice' motifs.[1]

​The name ​itself is ‌the debate's ​second front. ​The 'twin' ‌etymology pairs ​Ymir with ​Sanskrit Yama ‌— first ​mortal, son ‌of the ​sun, king ​of the ​dead, whose ​name means ‌'twin' — ‌and both ​figures anchor cosmogonies; ‌the 'roarer' ​etymology from ​ymja is ‌philologically safer ‌and semantically ‌apt for ‌the bellowing ​Aurgelmir.[2] Tacitus ‌supplies the ​Germanic corroboration: ​Germania chapter ​2 records ‌that the ​peoples sing ​of Tuisto, ​'a god ‌born of the ​earth', and ​his son ​Mannus as ‌their ancestors ​— and ‌Tuisto's name ​is regularly ‌read as ​'twin, two-fold'. ‌The pattern ‌is consistent: ​a primordial, ​doubled being ​at the ​root of ‌the world, ‌divided to ​make it.[3]

Sources

  1. Lincoln, Bruce, Myth, Cosmos, and Society: Indo-European Themes of Creation and Destruction (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1986); Rigveda 10.90 (Puruṣasūkta).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. Ymir; Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (1993), s.v. Ymir.
  3. Tacitus, Germania 2, ed. and trans. J. B. Rives (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1999).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Ymir's ​afterlife runs ‌wherever Norse ‌myth is ​retold at ​scale, ​usually under ​his own ​name. Robert ‌E. Howard's ​'The ​Frost-Giant's Daughter' ‌(1934) made ​its icy ‌heroine the ​daughter of ‌Ymir ​and carried ​the name ​into heroic ​fantasy; Marvel ​Comics ‌has kept ​Ymir the ‌frost giant ​in print ‌since ​1962 as ‌an antagonist ​of Thor; ‌and the ​name ​recurs across ‌fantasy games ‌wherever frost-giants ‌are wanted, ​from ​Dungeons & ‌Dragons onward.[1]

​In scholarship ​the figure ‌anchors ​the comparative ​project: Lincoln's ‌account of ​Indo-European creation ‌ideology is ​built ​around the ​Ymir material, ​and every ‌handbook — ‌Simek, ​Orchard, Lindow ‌— treats ​the 'roarer' ​versus 'twin' ‌question ‌as the ‌standard entry ‌on the ​etymology.[2] Modern ‌Heathenry ​reads him ​as the ​necessary first ‌matter of ​the ​cosmos: not ​a villain ‌erased from ​the world ​but ​the body ‌the world ​is still ‌made of ​— flesh ‌underfoot, ​weather overhead. ‌The name ‌itself never ​left the ​North: ​Icelandic Ymir ​remains a ‌living, if ‌rare, given ​name.

Sources

  1. Howard, Robert E., 'The Frost-Giant's Daughter' (1934); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ymir (modern reception).
  2. Lincoln, Bruce, Myth, Cosmos, and Society (Harvard University Press, 1986); Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (1993), s.v. Ymir.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Ymir ​is an ‌entirely textual ‌being: no ​runestone invokes ​him, no ​votive deposit ​names him, ​and no ‌image in ​the Norse ​visual record ‌can be ​securely ‌identified with ​him — ‌a silence ​that fits ​a cosmogonic ​first-being rather ​than a ​cult figure.[1] ‌The material ​record of ‌his mythology ​is ‌the manuscript ​record. Vafþrúðnismál ‌and Grímnismál ​survive in ‌the thirteenth-century ​Codex Regius ​of the ‌Poetic Edda ‌(GKS 2365 ‌4to); Völuspá ​is preserved ​both there ‌and ​in the ​fourteenth-century Hauksbók; ‌Snorri's creation ​account rests ​on the ‌Codex Regius ​of the ‌Prose Edda ​and its ​related codices.[2]

​Skaldic practice ​keeps ‌the giant ‌in the ​working vocabulary ‌of the ​poets: the ​þulur catalogued ‌in Skáldskaparmál ‌list Aurgelmir, ‌Brimir, and ‌Bláinn among ​the giant-names, ‌and kennings ​for ​giants and ​for the ‌sky (Ymir's ​skull) presuppose ​the cosmogony's ​currency in ‌pre-Christian verse. ​The comparative ​witnesses are ​themselves textual: ‌Rigveda 10.90 ​in ‌the Sanskrit ​manuscript tradition ‌and Tacitus' ​Germania in ‌the Latin ‌one — ​the dismemberment ​pattern preserved ​in writing ​on three ‌edges of ‌the Indo-European ​world.[3]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda gives ‌Ymir four ‌distinct voices ​of testimony. ​Völuspá opens its ​vision with ​him — ​'it was ‌in early ​days, when Ymir ​dwelled; there ‌was no ​sand nor ‌sea nor ​cool waves' — ‌placing the ​giant as ​the poem's ​first named ​being and the ​marker of ‌the world's ​before.[1] Vafþrúðnismál ‌is his fullest ​verse dossier: ‌Óðinn, disguised ​as Gangráðr, ‌draws from ​the wise giant ‌Vafþrúðnir the ​whole genealogy ​— Ymir's ‌rise from ‌Élivágar's poisoned rime, ‌the man ​and woman ​grown under ‌his arm, ​the six-headed son ​begotten leg ‌on leg, ​and Bergelmir's ​survival of ‌the flood on ​his lúðr ‌'countless winters ​before the ​earth was ​made'.[2]

Grímnismál supplies ​the creation ‌catalogue in ‌its purest ​form: 'From ‌Ymir's flesh the ​earth was ​shaped, and ‌from his ‌blood the ‌sea, mountains from ‌his bones, ​trees from ‌his hair, ​and from ​his skull the ​sky; and ‌from his ​eyelashes the ​cheerful gods made ​Miðgarðr for ‌men's sons; ​and from ​his brains ​the hard-minded clouds ‌were all ​created.'[3] Hyndluljóð ‌closes the ​account with ‌the doctrine of ​descent — ‌all the ‌giants descend ​from Ymir ​— making him ​not merely ​the first ‌giant but ‌the race's ​common ancestor.[4]

Sources

  1. Völuspá 3–5 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Vafþrúðnismál 20–21, 29–35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  4. Hyndluljóð 33 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Ymir's ‌narrative home. ​Gylfaginning 5–8 ​builds the ​cosmogony as ​continuous prose: ​Ginnungagap and ‌the ​eleven rivers ​of Élivágar ‌flowing with ​poison; the ‌meeting of ​Niflheimr's rime ‌and Múspell's ​sparks; the ​dripping ​that quickened ​into Ymir; ​the cow ‌Auðhumla and ​her four ‌rivers of ​milk; the ‌freeing ​of Búri ‌from the ​licked rime-stones; ‌the marriage ​of Borr ​and Bestla; ‌and the ‌three brothers' ‌killing ​of the ​giant.[1]

Snorri's ‌craft shows ​in the ​ordering of ‌the aftermath. ​The blood-flood ​that drowns ‌the ​frost-giants comes ‌first — ​catastrophe before ​construction — ​and only ​then the ‌making: earth ‌carried into ​the ‌middle of ​Ginnungagap, the ​sea ringed ‌around it, ‌the skull-vault ‌lifted by ‌the four ​dwarfs, the ‌sparks ​of Múspell ​fixed as ​stars, the ‌maggots in ​the flesh ​made rational ​as the ‌dwarfs, ​Miðgarðr walled ​with the ​eyelashes. Skáldskaparmál ‌then preserves ​the poetic ‌residue: the ​þulur name ‌the giant ​Aurgelmir, ‌Brimir, and ‌Bláinn, and ​the kennings ​for sky ​and giants ​keep the ‌cosmogony alive ‌as working ​imagery.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 5–8, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur and kennings, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of ​Ymir. No ​runestone ​names him, ​no ‌amulet carries ​him, ​and no ‌inscription ​yet found ‌preserves ​any of ‌his ​bynames — ​a ​silence he ​shares ​with the ‌whole ​cosmogonic stratum ‌of ​Norse myth ‌and ​one that ‌marks ​him as ‌a ​figure of ​poetry ‌and learned ‌prose ‌rather than ​of ​dedicatory practice.[1]

‌What ​the runic ​tradition ‌does preserve ​is ​the alphabet ‌in ​which the ‌name ​would have ​been ​carved: the ​Younger ‌Futhark, in ‌use ​from the ‌eighth ​century, would ​write ‌Ymir as ‌ᚢᛘᛁᛦ ‌— the ‌úr-rune ​ᚢ doing ‌duty ​for y ​as ​it does ‌for ​u, then ​maðr ​ᛘ, íss ‌ᛁ, ​and the ​ýr-rune ​ᛦ writing ‌the ​final nominative ‌-r. ​The reconstructed ‌carving ​is a ‌normalization, ‌not an ​attestation; ​the name's ​real ​inscriptional life ‌is ‌in the ​manuscripts.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ymir.
  2. Barnes, Michael P., Runes: A Handbook (Woodbridge: Boydell Press, 2012), on the Younger Futhark inventory.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Ymir's medieval ‌afterlife is ​genealogical ​and poetic ​rather than ​narrative. ​Snorri opens ‌the Prose ​Edda's frame ​with ‌the cosmogony ​because the ‌whole ​system of ‌skaldic kennings ​depends on ​it ​— the ​sky is ​'Ymir's ‌skull', the ​earth 'Ymir's ‌flesh', and ​the ‌sea 'Ymir's ​blood' in ‌the ​poets' working ‌metaphors, so ​that every ​recited ‌verse re-performs ‌the dismemberment.[1]

‌The ​learned tradition ​kept the ‌giant in ​its ​genealogies as ‌well: the ​Edda's ​euhemerizing prologue ‌and the ​þulur treat ‌Aurgelmir ​as the ​root-name of ​the ​jotnar, and ‌Saxo Grammaticus, ‌though he ​never ‌stages Ymir's ​death, begins ​his ‌Gesta Danorum ‌with a ‌Danish past ‌in ​which giants ‌are the ​oldest ​inhabitants of ​the North ‌— the ​same ​doctrine of ​the giants' ‌priority ​that Hyndluljóð ​states in ​verse.[2] The ‌skaldic ​record, not ‌the saga ​narrative, ‌is where ​Ymir lives ‌on: a ‌cosmogony ​compressed into ​kennings, carried ​in ​every ship- ‌and sky-metaphor ‌the poets ​made.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for sky, earth, sea), trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Preface and Book 1, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Every ​cosmogony answers ‌the question ‌of what ​the world ​is made ​from; the ​Norse ​answer is ‌a body. ​Before there ​was matter ‌there was ​already a ‌being — ​and ‌so creation, ​in this ​telling, is ​never innocent. ​The ground ​is flesh, ‌the ​sea is ‌blood, the ​mountains are ‌bones that ​once walked. ‌The myth ​refuses ‌the comfort ​of an ​empty beginning: ‌someone had ‌to die ‌for there ​to be ​anywhere ‌at all.[1]

​Yet the ​Edda does ‌not linger ​on the ​killing's guilt; ‌it ​lingers on ‌the fitting. ​Flesh to ​earth, blood ​to sea, ​skull to ‌sky ‌— the ​world is ‌a translation, ​part for ​part, of ‌the first ‌body into ‌the ‌lasting one. ​To live ‌in Miðgarðr ​is to ​live inside ​Ymir: behind ‌a ​wall of ​his eyelashes, ​under a ‌vault of ​his skull, ​walking his ​flesh. ‌The old ​name for ‌that intimacy ​is one ‌the modern ​world is ‌relearning — ‌that ​the environment ​is not ​around us ​but is ‌what we ‌are made ​of.

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014); Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 8, trans. Faulkes (1987).
