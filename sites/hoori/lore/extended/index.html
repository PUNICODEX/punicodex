The name Hoori reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the japanese tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

Hoori has no shrine of his own, and the evidence for any independent cult is nil — but the geography of his story is real and still kept. In Nichinan, Miyazaki Prefecture, Udo Jingū venerates his son Ugayafukiaezu in a seaside cave identified with Toyotama's parturition hut: the 'breast rock' (ochichi-iwa) receives ladles of offered water, and visitors test the omokaru-ishi, stones whose felt weight answers the purity of a wish. Nearby Aoshima, ringed by its wave-cut 'devil's washboard,' is tied to the dragon-palace cycle in local tradition. His own cult is absorbed upward into the imperial ancestral rites: the Kashihara plain at the foot of Mount Unebi, from which his great-grandson Jimmu is said to have set out east, is watched by Kashihara Jingū (founded 1890). What survives for Hoori himself is the narrative's place in the liturgy of origins — the sea-bride and the tide-jewels retold wherever the imperial genealogy is recited.

In the Earth

Hoori has no shrine and no image, but his story is anchored in real ground. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki place the action in the Yamato basin: Jimmu's traditional tomb, the Unebi kofun, lies by Kashihara in Nara Prefecture, and Kashihara Jingū — founded in 1890 — marks the traditional site of Kashihara-no-miya, the palace from which Jimmu set out eastward. His narrative survives materially in the Shinpukuji-bon, the early thirteenth-century manuscript of the Kojiki kept at Shinpuku-ji in Nagoya, the sole complete copy of the lost 712 original. At the foot of Mount Miwa, the Ōmiwa shrine preserves the sacred landscape in which the Jimmu-cycle myths were first localized. The evidence is commemorative rather than contemporary — but for an eighth-century state charter, that is precisely the point.

Iconography

How Hoori was imaged across the ages

The Image

No ancient image of Hoori exists, and the sources give him no fixed form — only objects: the bow of his inheritance, the borrowed fishhook, the tide-flowing and tide-ebbing jewels, and the cormorant-feather roof of the parturition hut his wife built. Medieval Japan did not paint him; Edo printmakers preferred his story's later avatars, and a prince's genealogy is harder to draw than a palace under the sea. What modern iconography he has is borrowed from his descendants' pageantry: the mirror, sword, and jewel of the regalia, which tradition hangs on his line. He remains, visually, a text — an ancestor whom two histories forgot to portray.

06

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

Hoori's transmission is the transmission of the Kojiki itself. The tale was set down in 712, variant in 720; its oldest surviving manuscript, the Shinpukuji-bon, carried it through the medieval centuries, and the court kept the genealogy in living use — recited at enthronements, cited in every dispute about the line's legitimacy. Edo nativist scholarship made him a hinge: Norinaga's Kojiki-den lingered over the dragon-palace cycle as proof that the age of the gods was history, not fancy. The Meiji state made the same text national charter. The West received him through translations — Chamberlain's 1882 Ko-ji-ki, Aston's Nihongi (1896), Philippi's 1969 Princeton edition — and twentieth-century folklorists filed his voyage among the international tale-types of the animal bride and the wonder-voyage, making an imperial ancestor a comparative case. Through every channel the sea-prince kept his function: the story that explains how the line of emperors came to stand on land with the sea's blessing.

Identifications Across Traditions

Hoori's story fuses motifs common across East Asia: the mortal who journeys to the dragon palace (parallels in Chinese and Korean legend), the animal bride whose true form must never be seen, and the younger brother whose misfortune ripens into sovereignty. Within Japan the dragon-palace voyage lived on most famously in the folktale of Urashima Tarō, where the same palace, the same time-slip, and the same broken promise end far more cruelly. Buddhist Japan had no need to give Hoori an avatar — his tale was too useful to the state to remain folklore. Written into the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720), it became the founding charter of the imperial house, read at court and cited by every dynasty that traced itself to Jimmu. The motifs are pan-Asian; the genealogy is specifically Yamato.

Modern Legacy

Hoori is remembered almost entirely as an ancestor, but his story has never stopped being retold. The dragon palace resurfaces in Heian tales, in otogizōshi storybooks, in puppet-theatre and Kabuki adaptations, and in modern novels, manga, and anime — the sea-bride and the undersea court are among Japan's most durable images. Politically his line still matters: the traditional date of Jimmu's accession, the first day of the first month of 660 BCE, became Kigensetsu in 1872 and survives as National Foundation Day (11 February). The imperial regalia — mirror, sword, and jewel — descend through this very genealogy, so that Hoori's tide-jewels and his descendants' legitimacy remain, ritually, in the same family.

07

The Meditation

What Hoori means — beyond the dictionary

Hoori is the one who looked. His whole career turns on small acts with enormous consequences: he borrows a hook and loses an empire's worth of trouble; he accepts a bride and gains the sea; he peeks through a feathered wall and loses her — and founds a dynasty. The Kojiki does not condemn him. Instead it makes curiosity the very mechanism by which the imperial line comes to land: no broken taboo, no Jimmu. It is a strikingly unsentimental theology of legitimacy, closer to a political founding document than a moral tale: the state begins not in obedience but in transgression survived. Hoori also teaches a subtler lesson about rivalry. He never defeats his brother by force; he wins by patience, by the tide-jewels, and by a mocking song. The victor in this myth is the one who can wait on the beach while his brother's rage exhausts itself against the sea.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Hoori, Agriculture, Hunter, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Hoori?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Hoori is /hoː.ɾi/ — approximately 'HOH-ree' — hold the first syllable long; the 'r' is a light tap.

02What does Hoori mean?

Hoori means Fire-subduing in the japanese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Hoori?

Hoori is associated with Bow (The weapon Ninigi gave him, symbolizing rule over the mountains), Fishhook (The lost and recovered hook that brought his brother Hoderi to submission), Sea palace (Ryūgū) (The undersea realm where he won Toyotama), Cormorant feather robe (The gift that allowed him to travel between land and sea).

04Why restore Hoori in Unicode?

Hoori is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.
