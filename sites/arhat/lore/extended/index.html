Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script अर्हत् Arhat — "an Arhat or superior divinity with the Jainas"
Unicode Restoration Arhat Canonical scholarly spelling — ASCII-identical
Modern ASCII arhat Plain-ASCII fallback

The name Arhat reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the buddhist tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Sanskrit — √arh 'to deserve, to be worthy' + present-participle -at — arhat, 'the worthy one' (Monier-Williams)
  • Pāli — arahant — the canonical title the Buddha applies to himself: arahaṃ sammāsambuddho
  • Jaina Prakrit — arahanta — the Jinas are 'worthy ones,' parallel title in the sister śramaṇa tradition (Ācārāṅga Sūtra)

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Arhat in Later Traditions

The Jaina sister-tradition used the same title in parallel: every tīrthaṅkara is an arhat, and the two śramaṇa movements argued over who deserved the word — evidence that it belonged to the age, not to one sect. Mahāyāna sūtras, building the rival bodhisattva ideal, chastised the arhat's "selfish" release (the Lotus Sūtra likens the arhat vehicle to a burning house escaped too soon), yet kept the title for the Buddha himself and for Śāriputra, whose "future Buddhahood" the Lotus explicitly prophesies. In China the arhat became the lohan — an immortal in Taoist dress — and in Tibet the great arhats of the canon were painted as the Buddha's original saṅgha, the lineage every ordination renews. Modern vipassanā movements restored the word to practice: the arhat is again a goal, not a museum piece.

Modern Legacy

The arhat's afterlife is the afterlife of the Theragāthā — a book of verses by the first saints, still chanted in Pāli lands, among the oldest autobiographies in the world. Chinese temples fill halls with lohan: the Five Hundred Arhats of Beijing's Biyun Si and the sixteen of Hangzhou's Jingshan Si are national treasures; Guanxiu's originals survive in Japan, designated national treasures there too. In Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia the arhat remains the living summit of the path — every forest monastery's kammaṭṭhāna tradition claims the title's ladder, rung by rung. And in the modern West, where "saint" had grown vague, "arhat" arrived with its etymology intact: the worthy one — holiness defined as a task you can finish.

In the Earth

The arhat's archaeology is the archaeology of the aniconic saṅgha. The Bharhut and Sanchi reliefs (2nd–1st c. BCE) show the great disciples flanking the empty throne — identifiable by label in Brahmi inscriptions: Sāriputa, Mogalāna, Maha Kāsava. The earliest anthropomorphic saṅgha images come from Gandhāra and Mathurā (1st–3rd c. CE): monks in patched robes, each with his attribute. The lohans' gallery in Chinese art is documented from the Tang — Guanxiu's sixteen (c. 891–901) survive in the Tokyo Seikado and Imperial Household collections — and the Five Hundred Lohans halls of the Song and Ming (Jingshan, Biyun, Baoguang monasteries) preserve the type at monumental scale. In Pāli lands, the arhat leaves no relic-cults of his own by design: the finished one, the tradition says, leaves nothing to enshrine but the teaching.

05

The Meditation

What Arhat means — beyond the dictionary

The arhat is the most austere ideal any major religion has proposed: not love, not justice, not obedience — completion. The canonical formula is brutal in its economy: done is what had to be done; there is no more of this. No god approves it, no history rewards it, no heaven awaits as payment. It is simply over.

That is why the Mahāyāna found it both admirable and insufficient, and why modern readers find it quietly devastating. The arhat does not save the world; the arhat leaves. Yet the tradition kept the begging bowl — the one object that binds the finished saint back to the unfinished crowd, who must feed him and so plant their own merit. Perfection, the bowl insists, is not private. The worthy one stands at the door precisely so that someone else can practice giving. The ideal of having nothing, it turns out, was always also a technology of connection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Arhat, Worthy One, Saint, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Arhat?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Arhat is /ˈɐɾ.ɦɐt̪/ — approximately 'UHR-hut' — short vowels throughout; the h is a voiced breath between the syllables, and the final t is pure dental, not English 't'.

02What does Arhat mean?

Arhat means an Arhat or superior divinity with the Jainas in the buddhist tradition.

03What are the symbols of Arhat?

Arhat is associated with The begging bowl (The arhat's only possession — and the reason the laity feed him: the worthy one is a field of merit, and a donation ripens), The crutch and long eyebrows (The lohans' immortal attributes in Chinese art — longevity so extreme it needs support, and sight so deep it overgrows), The flame of the taints extinguished (Nirvāṇa as a fire gone out — the arhat is the one whose fuel (rāga, dosa, moha) is spent; nothing more burns), The lion's roar of assurance (The arhat's declaration 'there is no more coming to any state of being' — certainty spoken from completion, not faith), The assembly of elders (Sāriputta and Moggallāna flanking the Buddha — wisdom and powers as the twin pillars of the arhat ideal).

04Why restore Arhat in Unicode?

Arhat is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Arhat?

“Worthy of Gifts” — The arhat has no biography because the arhat is a hundred biographies — the finished saints of the Theragāthā and Therīgāthā, the chief disciples of the canon, and in China a band of sixteen immortals commanded to wait out the ages for the next Buddha. The full telling lives on the lore page.
