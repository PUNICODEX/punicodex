Worthy ​of Gifts (The Word)

Arhat comes from √arh, "to deserve, to be worthy," with the participle -at — "the worthy one," one deserving of the alms of gods and men. The Buddha claimed ‌it as his own foremost title: in the canonical litany he is arahaṃ sammāsambuddho, "arahant, fully self-awakened" — a claim whose audacity the Udāna records being tested in his lifetime, when rivals demanded ​he prove it. The commentarial definition sharpens the word into a checklist: one who has destroyed the taints (āsava), lived the holy life, done what had to be done, laid down the burden, ‌and knows "there is no more of this." The Jaina tradition uses the same word in parallel: its twenty-four tīrthaṅkaras are arhats, worthy ones who ford the flood of rebirth. The mythic weight of ​a title: Buddhism made holiness a completed task, not a birth — and gave it a word anyone could check.

Sāriputta and Moggallāna (The Chiefs)

The Buddha named his two greatest arahants in ‌the Etadagga verses (AN 1): Sāriputta, foremost in great wisdom — the Dhamma's marshal, who could unfold a single line into a hundred meanings and who, questioned on his deathbed in the Sāriputta ​Sutta (SN 47.19), replied only that there was nowhere he had ever clung; and Moggallāna, foremost in psychic powers, who humbled Brahmā's pride by shaking the Vejayanta palace with a toe (DN 11, ‌Kevaṭṭa Sutta). Both were murdered — Moggallāna beaten to death by brigands — the Buddha predicting that the pair would die as the twin sāla trees fall. The Theragāthā lets the arahants speak ​in their own voices: "Cut down the whole forest of craving, not just one tree; from the jungle of craving, fear is born." The ideal is given a human face twice — the ‌thinker and the wonder-worker — and both faces end in the one death arahants cannot refuse.

Four Gates to the Deathless (The Path)

Arahantship is the fourth and last gate. The canon maps ​the road precisely: the stream-enterer (sotāpanna), certain of awakening, never again reborn lower than human; the once-returner (sakadāgāmin), who comes back once to this world; the non-returner (anāgāmin), who awakens in a higher ​heaven and does not return at all; and the arahant, freed here and now. The decisive faculty is stated everywhere and nowhere better than the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (MN 10): mindfulness of body, feelings, ‌mind, and dhammas — "the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow." Fifteen centuries later Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga (5th c. CE) welded the whole road into one manual, still ‌the map of Theravāda practice. The interpretive core: holiness is gradual, inspectable, and irreversible — a ladder whose rungs can be named, and whose top rung is the end of needing anything, forever.
