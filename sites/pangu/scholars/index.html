Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Pángǔ ​(pangu) — ‌The Giant ‌Between Heaven ​and Earth ​— belongs ​to ​the Chinese ​tradition, where ‌it is ​catalogued under ​the domain ‌"Creation, ​Separation, Foundation". ‌The name ​means "'Coiled ‌antiquity' (盤古), ​the giant ​who ​split heaven ​from earth"[1].

​Pángǔ is ‌China's creation ​made ‌flesh: the ​giant who ‌grew for ​eighteen thousand ‌years inside ​the ‌cosmic egg, ​pushed heaven ​and earth ‌apart with ‌his own ‌body, ​and — ​dying at ‌last — ​became the ​world itself: ‌his ​eyes the ​sun and ‌moon, his ​breath the ‌wind, his ​voice ​the thunder.[2]

​PuniCodex restores ​the name ‌as Pángǔ ‌and serves ​its ‌temple at ​pángǔ.com. The ​restoration preserves ‌the Pinyin ‌tone marks ‌— ‌the acute ​on á ‌and the ​caron on ​ǔ — ​distinctive ‌features the ​ASCII form ​loses, which ​places the ‌name ​in Tier ​1. The ​plain ASCII ‌form pangu ​survives as ‌a ​modern convenience ‌imposed by ​the early ‌domain-name system; ‌the restoration, ​not ​the fallback, ​is the ​form the ‌project defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

Sources

  1. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji (Historical Records of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors), as preserved in later encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan), 260.
  2. Anne Birrell, Chinese Mythology: An Introduction, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993.
  3. Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian), Sima Qian; Chinese Text Project, 91 BCE. ↗

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

No ​indigenous written ‌attestation survives ‌for this ​name; ​Pángǔ is ​a scholarly ​transliteration of ​the ‌reconstructed spoken ​form. Etymologically ​the name ‌means "'Coiled ​antiquity' ‌(盤古), the ​giant who ‌split heaven ​from ​earth"[1].

The ​ASCII form ​pangu survives ​only ‌because the ​early domain-name ‌system could ​not ‌carry diacritics; ​it is ‌a technological ​compromise, ‌not an ​ancient spelling. ​The Unicode ‌restoration Pángǔ ‌recovers ‌the stress ​accent of ​the original ‌directly ​in the ​address bar. ‌The restoration ​preserves ​the Pinyin ‌tone marks ​— the ‌acute ​on á ​and the ​caron on ​ǔ ‌— distinctive ‌features the ​ASCII form ‌loses, ​which places ​the name ‌in Tier ‌1.

The ‌letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

​- p ‌→ P ​— ​Same letter
​- a ‌→ á ​— ​Stress mark ​(acute/tone)

  • ‌n → ​n ​— Same ​letter
  • ‌g → ​g ‌— Same ​letter
  • ‌u → ​ǔ — ‌same

‌The project ​holds the ​domain pángǔ.com ​(xn--png-ela13j.com) ​as the ‌canonical home ‌of this ​name[2].

Sources

  1. Chen Shou, Sanguozhi (Records of the Three Kingdoms), Zhonghua Shuju critical edition, 289.
  2. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji (Historical Records of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors), as preserved in later encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan), 260.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed ‌pronunciation ‌of ​the name ​is ​/pʰa˧˥ ​ku˨˩˦/ ​— ‌Mandarin ​(Hanyu ​Pinyin ‌with ​tones).[1]

For ‌the ​modern ‌speaker, ​the ​closest ​approximation ​is: ​pahng-goo, ‌rising tone ​on ‌Pang, ​low-dipping ‌on ​Gu.

‌Kindred ​and ‌historical ​forms of ​the ‌name:

‌- ‌Chinese ​— ​盤古
‌- ​Owned ​form — ‌Pángǔ

​Pángǔ ​盘古 ‌carries ​the ‌rising ​tone ​on páng ​and ​the ‌dipping ‌third ​tone ‌on ​gǔ ​("antiquity"); ‌the name ‌pairs ‌"coiling" ‌with ​"ancient" ‌— ​the ​coiled ​one ‌of the ​primordial ​egg. ​Written ‌盤古 ​in ​traditional ​characters, ‌it ​is Chinese ‌myth's ​youngest ‌great ​name: ‌the ‌giant ​is ​attested ​only from ​the ‌3rd ‌century ​CE.

Sources

  1. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji (Historical Records of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors), as preserved in later encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan), 260.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​original ‌script ‌for ​this ​chinese ​name ​has ​not ‌yet ​been ​added ‌to ​PUNICODEX. ‌The ​form ‌shown ​is ​a ​scholarly ​transliteration.[1]

​The ‌form Pángǔ ​is ‌therefore ​a ‌scholarly ​transliteration ‌rather ​than ‌an ​attested ​ancient ‌spelling; ‌it ‌encodes ​the ​reconstructed ‌sound ​of ​the ‌name ​for ​modern ‌use, ​and ‌no ​mark ​in ​it ​is ‌decorative.

‌Written ​盘古 ‌(盤古 ​traditional): ​pán ‌(coil, ‌tray, ‌the ‌great ​basin) ‌plus ​gǔ ​(ancient). ​The ‌graphs describe ​him ​exactly ​— ‌the ​coiled ​giant ​of ‌antiquity, ​curled ‌inside ​the ‌cosmic ​egg ‌before ‌there ​was ​a ​world ​to ‌be ‌ancient ​in.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Pángǔ's myth ​exists in two ‌sentences of immense ‌consequence, recorded by ​Xú Zhěng in the ​3rd century CE ​— and expanded ​by a civilization ​into its canonical ‌account of how ​the world began.[1]

​### Three-Five Calendar Record ‌(The Egg)

Xú ​Zhěng's Sānwǔ lìjì ‌gives the founding ​text: "Heaven and ‌earth were chaos ​like a hen's ​egg, and Pángǔ was ​born within it. ​After eighteen thousand ​years, heaven and ‌earth opened: the ​bright, light qì ‌rose to become ​heaven; the dark, ‌heavy qì sank to ​become earth. Pángǔ ‌stood between them, ​changing nine times ‌daily — heaven ​rising ten feet ​a day, earth ‌thickening ten feet, and ‌Pángǔ growing ten ‌feet — for ​eighteen thousand years ​more."[2]

The ‌Body Becomes the ​World (The Death)

​When the separation was ‌fixed, Pángǔ died ​— and the ​transformation began: his ‌breath became wind ​and cloud, his ‌voice thunder, his ​left eye the ​sun and his right ​the moon, his ​four limbs the ‌four sacred mountains, ‌his blood the ​rivers, his veins ‌the roads, his ​flesh the soil, his ​hair the stars, ‌his skin and ‌body-hair grass and ‌trees, his sweat ‌rain — and, ​in the most ‌anthropologically piquant line, his ​fleas and lice, ​quickened by the ​wind, became the ‌black-haired people.

​China's Youngest Creator ​(The Late Arrival)

​The myth's strangest ‌fact is its date: ​no pre-Han text ​mentions Pángǔ, and ​scholars trace his ‌entry through southern ​traditions — the ‌Yao and Miao ​genealogies of the dog-ancestor ‌Pán Hù, phonetically ​adjacent — absorbed ‌and cosmicized in ‌the Wei-Jin centuries. ​China's canonical creation ​is thus a ​relative newcomer, adopted so ​completely that every ‌schoolchild now learns ‌it as the ​oldest story.

Sources

  1. Anne Birrell, Chinese Mythology: An Introduction, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993.
  2. Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian), Sima Qian; Chinese Text Project, 91 BCE. ↗

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The ​Pángǔ–Pán ‌Hù ‌debate ​is ​one ​of ​Chinese mythology's ​famous ‌problems: ​the ​southern ‌dog-ancestor ​of ‌the ​Yao ‌and ​She ​peoples ​and ​the ​cosmic ‌giant share ​a ‌syllable ​and ‌a ​region, ‌and ​scholars ‌from ​the ​Republican ‌era ‌onward ‌have ​argued ​transmission in ‌both ​directions. ​Comparative ‌mythology ​places ​him ‌with ​the ‌dismembered ​primordial ​beings ​— ​Vedic ‌Púruṣa, Norse ‌Ymir ​— ‌whose ​bodies ​become ‌worlds: ‌China's ‌version ‌is ​distinctive ‌for ​making ​the ​giant's ‌death not ​a ​murder ​but ‌a ​completion.[1]

​Within ​the ‌Chinese ​tradition, ‌closely ​related ‌names ​in ‌the ‌corpus include ​Bāguà, ​Fúxī, ​Guānyīn, ​Lóng, ‌Méngpó, ‌and ​Nǚwā.

Sources

  1. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji (Historical Records of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors), as preserved in later encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan), 260.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

"Since ​Pángǔ ‌‌separated ​heaven ​and ​earth" ​​is ‌the ​standard ​Chinese ‌​formula ‌for ​"since ‌​the ​beginning ​of ​time," ​‌and ​his ‌myth ​‌opens ​every ‌children's ​account ‌​of ​Chinese ‌prehistory. ‌The ‌​transformation ​— ‌body ​​to ‌landscape ​— ​gave ‌​Chinese ‌poetry ​one ​of ​​its ‌deepest ‌image-families, ​‌the ​mountain-as-body. ​UNESCO-listed ‌school ‌‌textbooks, ‌animated ​films, ‌and ​​the ​giant's ‌statues ​​across ​southern ‌China ​(Henan's ​​Tongbai ‌county ​claims ‌​his ‌birthplace) ​keep ‌the ‌​youngest ​creator ​as ​the ‌‌most ​familiar.[1]

Sources

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

No cult-temple ​of Pángǔ ‌predates the ‌myth's literary ​arrival, but ​his material ​afterlife is ​real: the ​Pángǔ temples ‌of Tongbai ​(Henan) and ​the southern ‌shrines of ​Guangdong and ‌Guangxi mark ​the festival ‌of his ​"birth" in ​the tenth ​lunar month. ​The textual ​archaeology is ‌exact — Xú ​Zhěng's Sānwǔ ‌lìjì and ​the Wǔyùn ‌lìnián jì ​(both 3rd ‌century, preserved ​in encyclopedias) ‌are the ​two struts ​of the ‌whole tradition, ‌and the ‌history of ​their quotation ​is the ‌history of ​the myth ​itself.[1]

His ‌cult is ​young, like ​his myth: ‌the Pángǔ temple ​of Tongbai ‌county (Henan) ​anchors the ​tradition's claimed ​birthplace, with ​his festival ‌in the ‌tenth lunar ​month drawing ‌the county's ​pilgrimage. In ​the south ‌— Guangdong ‌and Guangxi, ‌where scholars ‌trace the ​myth's Yao ‌and Miao ​ancestry — ​village shrines ​to Pángǔ ‌dàrén, 'the great ​man Pangu,' ​predate the ​literary fame. ‌The Museum ​of Chinese ​creation mythology ​tradition apart, ‌his monuments ​are the ‌mountains themselves: ​the myth ‌names them ​his limbs, ‌and the ‌pilgrim reads ​the landscape ​as the ​giant's body, ​which is ‌the oldest ‌way of ​reading it.

Sources

  1. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji (Historical Records of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors), as preserved in later encyclopedias (Taiping Yulan), 260.
  2. Birrell, Chinese Mythology; the Tongbai temple records.

Daoist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Daoist ​cosmology ‌adopted ‌him ​wholesale: ​the ​Yunji ​Qiqian ​and ‌the ​Daoist ​‌creation ​treatises ‌retell ​the ‌egg ​and ​the ​eighteen ​thousand ​years ‌within ​the ‌vocabulary ​of ‌qì ​— ‌the ​light ‌and ​heavy ​vapors ‌‌he ‌separates ​are ​the ‌tradition's ​own. ​Some ‌registers ​deify ​him ‌further ​as ‌a ​primordial ​lord ​of ​the ‌first ‌era, ​and ‌the ​​flood-and-egg ‌cosmogonies ‌of ‌the ‌southern ​textual ‌traditions ​(where ​scholars ​trace ‌his ​Yao ​and ​Miao ‌ancestry) ​circulate ​in ​Daoist-liturgical ‌editions. ​China's ‌youngest ​‌creator ​became, ‌in ‌the ​canon, ​simply ​the ​name ‌for ‌the ​beginning.

  1. Yunji Qiqian (creation treatises); Birrell, Chinese Mythology.

Buddhist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Buddhist ​cosmologies, ‌rich ‌​in ​their ​own ​world-cycles, ​‌took ​him ​up ‌late ​‌and ​locally: ‌vernacular ​Buddhist ​​storytelling ​syncretizes ​the ‌egg ​‌with ​the ‌kalpa's ​re-formation ‌​after ‌the ​cosmic ​winds ‌destroy ‌‌the ​old ​world, ‌and ​​temple-wall ‌narratives ​in ​the ‌​south ‌place ​him ​before ​​the ‌Buddha's ‌eras ​as ‌​the ​previous ‌formation's ‌giant. ‌The ‌​scholarly ‌engagement ​is ​equally ​‌real: ​the ​Buddhist ​encyclopedias' ‌​discussions ​of ​non-Buddhist ‌origins ​‌cite ​the ‌Pángǔ ​myth ‌‌as ​China's ​native ​answer ​‌to ‌the ​kalpa.

Calligraphy & Script

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

盘古 ​(盤古): ‌pán ‌(盤) ​is ​the ​great ​basin ​— ‌dish ​with ​the ‌boat-phonetic ​— ‌evoking ​the ‌coiled ​container ​of ​chaos; ​gǔ ​(古) ‌is ​"ancient," ‌ten ​mouths, ‌the ​tradition ‌handed ​down. ‌Together: ​the ​coiled ‌one ‌of ‌antiquity. ​The ​name ‌is ​young ​as ‌names ​go ​— ‌third ​century ‌CE ​— ​and ​its ​graphs ‌are ‌transparent ​where ‌older ​gods' ​are ‌opaque, ‌a ‌name ‌written ​to ‌describe. ​Temple ​steles ​of ‌the ​Tongbai ​and ​southern ‌shrines ​carve ​the ​pair ‌in ​the ‌standard ​script ‌of ​their ‌centuries: ‌a ​creation ​story ​told, ​fittingly, ‌in ‌plain ​characters.

Sources

  1. Xu Zheng, Sanwu Liji; the Tongbai temple steles.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Pángǔ ​is the ‌god ‌of the ​long ​work: ​eighteen thousand ​years ​of pushing, ‌unnoticed, ​until the ​sky ‌stayed up ​on ‌its own. ​He ‌never ​saw the ​world ​he made ​— ​he became ‌it. ​He asks ‌of ​everyone engaged ‌in ​slow, ‌unglamorous creation: ​what ‌are you ​growing ​between the ‌heaven ‌and the ‌earth ​of your ​time ‌— ​and will ​it ‌stand when ​you ​are done?

‌The ​flea-born people ‌of ​the oldest ​version ​keep ​the myth ‌honest: ‌humanity is ​not ‌the giant's ​masterpiece ​but his ‌smallest ‌parasite, quickened ‌by ‌the ​wind. It ‌is ​the humblest ​creation-line ​in world ‌mythology ​— and ​somehow ​the most ‌accurate: ​we ​are the ​detail, ‌not the ​design, ‌and the ​world ‌was not ​made ‌for us ‌but ​of ​something that ​once ​stood up ‌and ‌held it ​apart.[1]
