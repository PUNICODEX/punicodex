Overview

Amida (amida) — Infinite Light, Pure Land — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The ​name means "Immeasurable light".

Amida is the Japanese contraction of the Sanskrit Buddha Amitābha, the Buddha of Measureless Light, and the central figure of the Pure Land schools of Japan.

PuniCodex restores the name ​as Amida. The plain ASCII form amida is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed ​in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is treated as a plain form. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, ​while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 阿弥陀. Etymologically it means "Immeasurable light".

Amida is the Japanese contraction ‌of Amitābha (Sanskrit अमिताभ), the Buddha of Measureless Light, and is used interchangeably with Amitāyus, the Buddha of Measureless Life. The Chinese transcription 阿弥陀 (Ēmítuó) was adopted into Japanese as Amida, becoming the ​focus of the Pure Land schools.

The ASCII form amida survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. ​The Unicode restoration Amida gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 阿弥陀 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where ‌attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Amida (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized ​reading //a.mi.da//.

The three characters 阿弥 (a-mi) and 陀 (da) transcribe phonetically the Sanskrit name Amitābha. The form is a classic Buddhist ateji: the characters were chosen for their sound rather than their meaning, ‌though later exegetes read symbolic significance into each graph.

Mythology

The mythology of Amida is the mythology of a vow. In the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra the monk Dharmākara, after five aeons of contemplation, pronounced forty-eight vows before the Buddha Lokeśvararāja, ​pledging to create a Buddha-field where all beings could be reborn through faith. The eighteenth vow is foundational: beings of the ten directions who sincerely entrust themselves to him and call his name ‌even ten times will be born in Sukhāvatī. Upon fulfilment of these vows Dharmākara became Amida, and his paradise has received the faithful for ten aeons.

The narrative of Amida participates in the larger ​mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles ‌were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of amida illustrates how ​Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

The Forty-Eight Vows of Dharmākara (The Primal Vow)

In the Larger Sukhāvatīvyūha Sūtra the ‌Buddha tells how, in an inconceivably remote past, the monk Dharmākara knelt before the Buddha Lokeśvararāja and set himself a task no being had dared: to survey every Buddha-field in the ten directions, ​gather the best of each, and build from them a single land where no one who trusted him would fail to reach enlightenment. After five aeons of contemplation he pronounced forty-eight vows. The ‌eighteenth — the Primal Vow (hongan) — promises that anyone who, with sincere entrusting, desires rebirth in his land and calls his name even ten times will be born there. Dharmākara then practiced ​for countless kalpas until the vows stood fulfilled, and he became Amida, whose Sukhāvatī lies to the west like a shore beyond every storm. The sūtra's logic is startling: enlightenment is not climbed ​but received, because someone else finished the climbing first.

Hōnen Selects the Single Practice (The Nembutsu)

By the twelfth century the Tendai establishment taught that every practice could lead to awakening — and ‌therefore, argued the monk Hōnen, none was being done wholeheartedly by ordinary people mired in the latter days of the Dharma. In 1198 he set down his answer in the Senchakushū: select the ‌one practice the Buddha himself had promised would suffice — the nenbutsu, the calling of Amida's name, Namu Amida Butsu — and let every other good act adorn it rather than compete with it. ‌His disciples carried the teaching from the capital to the fishing villages, until peasants and soldiers who had never read a sūtra were reciting the Buddha's name in fields and on battlefields. The ​authorities proscribed the movement in 1207 and exiled Hōnen, but the nenbutsu had already escaped every gate: the single practice made the highest path the commonest road.

Shinran and the Tannishō (Other Power)

​Hōnen's disciple Shinran pushed the nenbutsu one step further — and into a new religion. If salvation depended on Amida's Primal Vow, then even the recitation must be the Buddha's own working through ‌the believer; even faith was a gift, not an achievement. The saying attributed to him in the Tannishō became the movement's signature: even the good person attains birth in the Pure Land, how ​much more so the evil person — because the good still secretly trust their own goodness, while the evil have nothing left to trust but the vow. Driven from Kyoto and settled among ​the peasants of Kantō, Shinran married, raised a family, and wrote the Kyōgyōshinshō in huts and borrowed halls, founding what became Jōdo Shinshū, Japan's largest Buddhist school. The scandal of his teaching is ‌its mercy: it works precisely for those the other paths screen out.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Dhyāna mudrā — the meditation gesture, hands in the lap, emblem of the receptive calm of the Pure ​Land.
  • Welcoming descent (raigō) — standing Amida with hand raised, descending to meet the dying faithful.
  • Lotus throne — birth in Sukhāvatī occurs upon a lotus blossom.
  • Red or crimson body — in ​the five-Buddha system Amida heads the lotus family of the west.
  • Nembutsu — the invocation Namu Amida Butsu is itself the central symbol and practice.
  • The Amida triad — Amida flanked by Kannon ‌(Avalokiteśvara) and Seishi (Mahāsthāmaprāpta) — compassion and wisdom attending every descent
  • The nenbutsu rosary (juzu) — Beads counting Namu Amida Butsu; worn on the wrist of every Pure Land devotee since Hōnen's day
  • The ​western direction — He faces west — toward the setting sun and toward Sukhāvatī; the dying were laid with their heads west to meet his coming
  • The mudrā of welcome — The raised ‌hand in raigō images, palm open — the gesture of one arriving to receive, not to judge
  • Halos of light — Radiant aureoles of immeasurable light — in Amida's case the body itself ​is the sermon

Archaeology & Evidence

The earliest Indian epigraphic reference to Amitābha is the Huviṣka-era pedestal inscription from Mathura (second century CE). In Japan the Phoenix Hall (Hōōdō) at Byōdō-in, a UNESCO ​World Heritage site, houses the seated wooden Amida carved by Jōchō in 1053, the supreme example of Heian yosegi-zukuri joined-block construction. The Great Buddha of Kamakura at Kōtoku-in, cast c. 1252, is another ​monumental Amida image.

Realm & Domain

Amida presides over Sukhāvatī, the Pure Land of Bliss in the western direction. His soteriology is founded on the hongan, the primal vow made as the bodhisattva ​Dharmākara, that any being who calls his name with sincere faith will be reborn in his land. In Japan this devotion became the dominant form of Buddhist practice among laypeople from the late ‌Heian period onward.

The domain of Amida is understood within the broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, buddhas, and local spirits. ‌Shrines, temples, and household rites all invoke the figure in ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Amida therefore names not only a mythic person but a living ​field of ritual activity: pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. This combinatory or "syncretic" context is essential for interpreting the domains listed above.

Across Cultures

Amida ‌is the Japanese form of the pan-Asian Buddha Amitābha. In China he is Ēmítuófó; in Korea Amit'a-bul; in Tibet ʼOd dpag med (Öpakmé). The Japanese Pure Land schools — Jōdo-shū founded by Hōnen ‌and Jōdo Shinshū founded by Shinran — made exclusive reliance on Amida's Other-power (tariki) a distinctive path. Shinran's teaching that the nembutsu saves "even the good person, how much more the evil one" ‌(akunin shōki) remains one of the most influential formulations of Japanese Buddhism.

Cultural Legacy

Pure Land devotion centred on Amida is one of the largest living Buddhist traditions in Japan. The nembutsu (Namu ‌Amida Butsu) is recited in homes, temples, and funeral rites across the country. Artistic masterpieces such as the Amida of the Phoenix Hall at Byōdō-in (1053) and the Great Buddha of Kamakura (c. ​1252) testify to his visual prominence. The phrase "Namu Amida Butsu" has entered Japanese popular culture as an expression of gratitude, resignation, and hope.
