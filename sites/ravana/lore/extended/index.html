Sanskrit Rāvaṇa, from the causative of rū 'to roar, to cry'; the ten-headed king of Lanka.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Sanskrit root — √ru 'to roar, cry out' — rāvaṇa is 'the Roarer'; the epic explains the name at the shaking of Kailāsa (Monier-Williams s.v.; Rāmāyaṇa, Uttarakāṇḍa 16).
  • Prakrit — Rayaṇa — the form of the name in Vimalasūri's Paümacariya, the Jain Rāmāyaṇa in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit.
  • Tamil — Iṟāvaṇaṉ — the Tamil form, with prothetic i-, of the Cilappatikāram and the later Kamba Rāmāyaṇam tradition.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Rāvaṇa in Later Traditions

The Jains refused the epic's verdict. In Vimalasūri's Prakrit Paümacariya (c. 3rd–5th century CE), the villain of Vālmīki becomes the noble King Rayaṇa of Laṅgka, a Jain layman of scrupulous conduct — it is Lakṣmaṇa who is the ruthless one, and the virtuous Rayaṇa who attains heaven. Southeast Asia recast him again: in the Thai Ramakien he is Thotsakan, the ten-faced titan of Ayutthaya drama, still the arch-villain but magnificent; in Cambodia's Reamker he is Krong Reap, the giant king of Laṅkā. In parts of South Asia the demon became a deity: villages in Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh keep Rāvaṇa shrines, and the Gonds of Bastar claim descent from a 'Rāvaṇa king' of old. His Śaiva devotion also survived independently of his epic crimes — the Śivatāṇḍava is still recited at Śiva's morning worship, and Ayurvedic tradition names him among its legendary physician-authors. The traditions disagree on whether he is to be burned or bowed to; both have been done, in different places, for two thousand years.

Modern Legacy

Every autumn at Dussehra, across India and the diaspora, towering effigies of Rāvaṇa — ten-headed, twenty-armed, stuffed with firecrackers — are burned to cheers, reenacting the epic's climax on the festival that celebrates Rāma's victory. The ritual has its dissenters: in Bisrakh, the village near Delhi that claims his birthplace, some families keep mourning on the burning-day, and in Bastar, Gond tradition counts him an ancestor to be honored. His name lives in music through the rāvaṇahatthā, the spike-fiddle of Rajasthani folk players long described as his invention and famous in the West since the 1960s as the ravanastron, a claimed ancestor of the violin family. Modern retellings — from Bhavabhūti's sympathetic eighth-century Uttararāmacarita to television's 1987 Rāmāyaṇ and twenty-first-century novels narrated from Laṅkā's side — keep returning to him: the villain whose learning, grief, and grandeur make the hero's victory cost something.

In the Earth

There is no material record of Rāvaṇa — no inscription, coin, or image survives from any period that names him, and the giant belongs entirely to the textual tradition, whose oldest witnesses are the medieval manuscripts of the Rāmāyaṇa itself. The landscape, however, has been thoroughly claimed by the epic. In Sri Lanka — Laṅkā of the tradition — the rock fortress of Sigīrya is popularly his palace (in fact the work of King Kāśyapa I, fifth century CE), and the cave above the falls at Ella is toured as Rāvaṇa's own. Between India and the island runs Ādām's Bridge, the chain of shoals the epic calls Nala's bridge (Yuddhakāṇḍa 22), and at Rāmeśvaram in Tamil Nadu the Rāmanāthaswāmy temple stands at the epic's launching point, its corridors among the longest in India. The archaeology of Rāvaṇa is thus the archaeology of reception — geology, temples, and place-names retelling the story in stone.

05

The Meditation

What Rāvaṇa means — beyond the dictionary

Rāvaṇa is the epic's portrait of a mind that knows everything except how to yield. Ten heads — later glossed as the four Vedas and the six śāstras — and twenty arms; a singer whose hymn still opens Śaiva mornings; a conqueror who rode the Pushpaka to the ends of the three worlds; a king whose council scenes are a seminar on statecraft that he consistently fails to heed. The Rāmāyaṇa's deepest teaching is spoken by his own brother: give Sītā back, and live. He cannot, and the epic spends six books showing precisely what it costs a civilization when its most capable man cannot take advice. In this he is disturbingly modern — the expert, the genius, the tenfold mind that has read everything and changed nothing in itself. Restoring his name matters the way the epic's balance does: Rāvaṇa, with its long ā and curled ṇ, stands for a tradition that made even its demons learned and refused to write any man off as merely evil.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Rāvaṇa, Ten-Headed King, Lanka, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Rāvaṇa?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Rāvaṇa is /raː.ʋɐ.ɳɐ/ — approximately 'RAA-vuh-nuh' — long open first syllable, a soft w-like v, and a tongue-curled n for the ṇ.

02What does Rāvaṇa mean?

Rāvaṇa means The ruler of Laṅkā and chief of the Rākṣasas, whose destruction by Rāmacandra forms the subject of the Rāmāyaṇa in the sanskrit tradition.

03What are the symbols of Rāvaṇa?

Rāvaṇa is associated with Ten heads (daśānana) (The multiplied mind; later tradition glosses the ten as the four Vedas and six śāstras — mastery worn as anatomy.), The Pushpaka vimāna (His sky-going chariot, won from his brother Kubera — the vehicle of the abduction and, at the epic's end, of Rāma's triumphal return.), The Śivatāṇḍava stotra (The hymn he sang with crushed arms beneath Kailāsa — the one language, devotion, that he never lost.), Brahmā's boon (Near-invulnerability to gods, gandharvas, and demons — and the fatal contempt that left men out of the list.), The golden city of Laṅkā (The island fortress his brother Kubera ruled before him, built of gold by the divine architect Viśvakarman.).

04Why restore Rāvaṇa in Unicode?

Plain ASCII ravana strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Rāvaṇa?

“Born of Sage and Demoness” — The Rāmāyaṇa builds Rāvaṇa as a complete king — scholar, musician, ascetic, conqueror of the three worlds — whose single failure of self-command draws Rāma across the ocean. His story spans the epic's frame: born in its seventh book, he dies in its sixth. The full telling lives on the lore page.
