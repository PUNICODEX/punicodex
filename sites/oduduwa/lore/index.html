01

Mythology

Stories of Odùduwà

Odùduwà's story is the Yoruba Genesis: the descent from heaven, the making of dry land, the founding of Ilé-Ifẹ̀, and the fathering of dynasties. It lives in the Ifá corpus and in oral tradition rather than any fixed book, and the tellings disagree with one another — the tradition itself treats the variants as paths to the same city.

The Delegation

Seventeen from Ọ̀run

In the beginning the world was water: Olódùmarè, the supreme God, ruled from Ọ̀run, the sky, while Ayé, the earth below, was soft marsh without a place to stand. The orishas petitioned to build a dry world, and Olódùmarè chose Ọbàtálá — Oríṣàńlá, the most senior among them — to lead the mission down, with Odùduwà in the company: in some tellings his younger brother, in others simply the keeper of the god's bag of materials. The delegation received what creation required — a handful of earth, a five-toed cockerel, and a chain long enough to climb from heaven to the waves. The number of the delegates shifts between tellings, sixteen or seventeen, as oral tradition allows. The story is recorded in the tellings collected by Bascom, Johnson, and Idowu from the Ifá corpus and Ifẹ̀ priests.

The myth's quiet claim is that creation in Yoruba thought is delegated: God entrusts the founding of the world to a team, and seniority alone does not finish the job. The one who climbs down will matter more than the one who was chosen to lead — and the tradition already knows it.

Palm Wine

The Sleep of Ọbàtálá

Before the descent, Ọbàtálá grew thirsty and drank palm wine — and the wine dulled him. He staggered, fell, and slept beside the work he had been sent to do. Odùduwà took the bag from his sleeping brother, climbed down the chain to the primordial waters, scattered the handful of earth onto the waves, and set the five-toed cockerel to scratching; the fowl spread the dust outward into wide dry land, beginning at the place that became Ifẹ̀. When Ọbàtálá awoke and came down to find the creation finished by another hand, the rivalry between the two worship-lines was born. Tradition adds the ritual moral: Ọbàtálá therefore forbade his priests the palm wine that undid him.

The myth explains two lasting facts at once — the founding of Ifẹ̀ and a living taboo in Ọbàtálá's cult — and its judgement is characteristic of Yoruba narrative: creation passes not to the most senior but to the most steadfast, and the instrument of failure is never an enemy, only a pleasure taken at the wrong hour.

Ilé-Ifẹ̀

Where the World Began

The spot where Odùduwà landed and made his camp became Ilé-Ifẹ̀ — the name is usually explained as 'the house of expansion' (ifẹ̀, breadth). Here he planted his staff and raised the first city; from here the world was peopled and the kingship of the earth was administered. Ifẹ̀ tradition points to the ground of the descent to this day, and some tellings carry the quarrel upward: Ọbàtálá lodged his claim to the work with Olódùmarè, and the settlement divided the realms between them — the earth and its rule to Odùduwà, the fashioning of human bodies to Ọbàtálá. The version is old and not the only one; the tradition keeps several and argues from all of them.

Ifẹ̀'s claim to be the place where the world began is not geography but memory: the city is a monument to the act of landing. In Yoruba religious geography every road of consequence runs back to this one settlement, and the mythology exists to say why the road is there.

The Seven Sons

The Kings Go Out

From Ifẹ̀, kingship radiated outward. Ifẹ̀ tradition names seven sons of Odùduwà who went out from the city to become kings of the Yoruba towns, the royal lines of the entire people descending from this single household; the youngest, Ọ̀rànmíyàn, was the heir who stayed at home longest and set out last of all. He went north and founded Ọ̀yọ́, whose Aláàfin — 'owner of the palace' — ruled the greatest of the Yoruba kingdoms. And Benin's own court tradition tells that when Benin needed a king, an embassy walked the forest road to Ifẹ̀ and was given Ọ̀rànmíyàn, whose son became the first Ọba of Benin — making Odùduwà, through his son, an ancestor of the Benin dynasty too.

The two traditions differ in detail, and modern historians debate how much of the migration narrative is memory and how much charter-myth; what no account disputes is the direction of the claim. Every dynasty wants its road to lead back to Ifẹ̀. The myth is a map of Yoruba political order, with all paths drawn to one father.

Olojo

The Crown That Came Down

At Ifẹ̀, Odùduwà is not only remembered; he is worshipped. His shrine stands in the sacred city, and at the Olojo festival — the feast of 'the owner of the day' — the Oòni, held to be his direct descendant, withdraws and then emerges wearing the Ade Aare, the ancient crown that tradition holds to have come down from heaven with Odùduwà himself. On this one occasion in the year the king is seen crowned, and Ifẹ̀ fills with pilgrims, chiefs, and the priests of a hundred lineages. The rite ties the city's foundation story to its living calendar: creation is not past tense at Ifẹ̀ but an annual appointment.

Each Olojo is the descent from Ọ̀run restaged in cloth, bead, and procession — the myth worn rather than told. That is the deepest sense in which Odùduwà is progenitor: the crown, the city, and the festival are one continuous inheritance, and the living king is its current syllable.

02

Progenitor

Progenitor, Ile-Ife, First King

Odùduwà is the founder: the heaven-sent ancestor who made the earth firm, founded Ilé-Ifẹ̀, and fathered the dynasties of the Yoruba world. Every Yoruba king claims descent from him, and the Oòni of Ifẹ̀, his living heir, is the acknowledged spiritual head of the tradition.

Progenitor

The primordial ancestor of Yoruba kingship — Ifẹ̀ tradition names his sons and grandsons as the founders of the Yoruba towns and their dynasties.

Maker of the Firm Earth

He took the materials of creation — a handful of earth, a five-toed cockerel, a chain — and made the dry land on which the world stands (Ifá corpus; oral tradition).

Unicode Restoration

The restoration Odùduwà recovers the low tones that plain ASCII oduduwa flattens — in Yoruba, pitch is meaning.

Ifá and Oral Attestation

Attested in the Ifá corpus and Yoruba oral tradition; Yoruba's Latin orthography is a nineteenth-century standardization, so the true attestation is tonal and performative, not alphabetic.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

Òdùduwà

The name in its original Yoruba form. Odùduwà (Òdùduwà) is attested in the source tradition — “Creator of the earth; primordial ancestor of the Yoruba”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

oduduwa

Reduced to plain oduduwa, the name loses everything that made it specific: original diacritics and script distinctions. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Odùduwà

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Odùduwà restores original diacritics and script distinctions, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Odùduwà.com → xn--odduw-vqa5o.com

The non-ASCII characters in Odùduwà are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Odùduwà.

Pronunciation

How Odùduwà was spoken

/o.dù.du.wà/ Yoruba Reconstruction
O- Open-mid back [ɔ] at mid tone — Yoruba's unmarked, level pitch, the calm opening of the name.
-dù- Voiced alveolar [d] plus low-tone [ù]: the pitch drops on the marked syllable and stays down.
-du- A second, mid-tone syllable — the repetition gives the name its drum-like fall and return.
-wà The labio-velar approximant [w] closing on low-tone [à]; the name ends where the pitch comes to rest.

Modern Approximation

'oh-doo-DOO-wah' — four even syllables; the grave-marked syllables ù and à are low in pitch, sung below the others, not stressed harder.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Odùduwà is Tier 1: the restored form carries the Yoruba tone marks — low ù and à — that the ASCII oduduwa cannot write. The marks are the name: a Yoruba speaker reads pitch, not spelling.

Etymology

Yoruba theonym of the primordial ancestor orisha Òdùduwà, founder of Ile-Ife and progenitor of Yoruba kingship.

Meaning

Progenitor, kingship, Ile-Ife, sacred authority; the ancestor of the Yoruba royal dynasties.

From original to transliteration

  1. Yoruba has been written in the Latin alphabet since the 1840s, with diacritics for tone and vowel quality.
  2. The underdot marks the open vowels /ɛ, ɔ/ and the postalveolar fricative /ʃ/; these are the obligatory phonemic marks.
  3. Tone marks (grave low, acute high, mid unmarked) are fully written in dictionaries but routinely omitted in everyday Yoruba writing.
  4. The owned Unicode form preserves the underdots and the tone marks the domain could carry; the fully tone-marked dictionary form is documented here.

Extended Lore

Odùduwà is what it means to be from somewhere. Most peoples have a founder, but few have made the founder do such concentrated work: in one figure, Yoruba tradition fuses the creation of the earth, the founding of the first city, the origin of kingship, and the common ancestry of a nation of millions. To belong, in this scheme, is not to hold a passport but to trace a road — and every king's legitimacy is measured by how convincingly his lineage walks back to Ifẹ̀.
