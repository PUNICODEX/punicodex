01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Sif (sif) ​— Earth, Harvest ‌· Relation ‌by marriage — ​is the golden-haired ​wife of ​Þórr, mother of ​Ullr, and the ​still centre ‌of the busiest ​forge-story in the ​Edda: when ‌Loki sheared off ​her hair in ‌mischief, the redress ​Þórr exacted ‌sent Loki to ​the dwarfs, and ​the smithying ​that followed produced ​not only her ​new hair ‌of living gold ​but Gungnir, Skíðblaðnir, ‌Draupnir, Gullinbursti, ​and Mjölnir — ‌the gods' entire ​great arsenal, all ‌descended from ​one act of ‌domestic malice.[1]

Her ​name is ​the Old Norse ‌common noun sif ‌(plural sifjar), ‌'affinity, relation by ​marriage' — cognate ​with Old ‌English sibb and ​Old High German ​sippa, and so ‌the ancestor ​of English sib, ​sibling, and gossip. ‌The theonym ​is kinship itself, ‌made personal.[2] In ​the Poetic ​Edda she appears ​twice: pouring Loki ​mead in a ‌crystal cup ‌and asking to ​be counted blameless ‌(Lokasenna 53), ​and as the ​target of a ‌taunt flung ‌at Þórr across ‌the water (Hárbarðsljóð ‌48) — ​the corpus's whole ‌record of her ​voice.[3]

PuniCodex restores ​the name ​as Sif — ‌identical to the ​ASCII input, ​because normalized Old ​Norse writes no ‌diacritic in ​this name. The ​mechanical rule therefore ​places the ‌name in Tier ​2: nothing distinctive ‌is lost in ​transit, a ‌statement about orthography ​and never about ‌the goddess. ‌The temple is ​domain-less: the plain-ASCII ​sif.com is ​registered to a ​third party outside ‌the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the shearing of Sif's hair and the making of the treasures), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. sif; de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. sif.
  3. Lokasenna 53–54 and Hárbarðsljóð 48 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old ‌Norse as Sif ​— the ​goddess's name and, ​beside it, ​the common ​noun sif (plural ‌sifjar), 'affinity, ​kinship, relation ​by marriage'. The ‌etymology is ​among the clearest ‌in the ​onomasticon: the ‌theonym is the ​abstract noun ​of marriage-bond ​made personal, from ​the Germanic ​root behind Old ‌English sibb ​'kinship, peace', ‌Old High German ​sippa, and ‌Gothic sibja ​— the same ‌root that ​gives modern English ‌sib, sibling, ​and, through ​god-sibb 'baptismal sponsor', ‌gossip.[1] Cleasby–Vigfusson ‌gloss the ‌goddess's name with ​the noun ​it is, and ‌neither de ​Vries nor ​the handbooks record ‌any serious ​rival derivation.[2]

​The ASCII form ‌is not ​a fallback here ‌but the ​form itself: ​normalized Old Norse ​writes three ​plain Latin ‌letters and a ‌capital. The ​Unicode restoration Sif ‌therefore preserves ​nothing the ​ASCII alphabet loses, ‌and the ‌mechanical rule ‌classifies the name ‌Tier 2 ​— an orthographic ‌fact, not ​a judgment ​on the goddess.

​The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

​- s → ​S — ​Same, capitalized

  • ‌i → ​i — ​Same
  • f ​→ f ‌— Same

​The project holds ‌no domain ​for this name. ‌The plain-ASCII ​sif.com is ‌registered to a ‌third party ​outside the ​collection and is ​watched for ​release; this temple ‌claims nothing ‌it does ​not hold.[3]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈsiv/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​S- — Voiceless ​alveolar sibilant ‌[s], as ​in English 'see'; ​Old Norse ‌initial s is ​plain and ‌unaspirated
  • ​-i- — Short ‌close front ​[i], held brief ​— Old ​Norse short ​i, not the ​long í

‌- -f — ​Written f ‌but sounded [v]: ​Old Norse ‌f after ​a vowel was ‌the voiced ​fricative, exactly as ‌in modern ​Icelandic, where ​the name is ‌still pronounced ‌'seev'

For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest ​approximation is: 'SEEV' ‌— one ​syllable, the vowel ​short and ‌bright, the ​final f voiced; ​never an ‌English 'siff'.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of the ​name:

  • ​Old Norse ​— Sif, wife ​of Þórr; ‌beside the common ‌noun sif ​(plural sifjar), ‌'affinity, relation by ​marriage'
  • ​Younger Futhark — ‌ᛋᛁᚠ (s-i-f), ‌the Viking-Age ‌runic rendering
  • ‌Modern Icelandic ​— Sif, a ‌living given ​name, pronounced ​with the voiced ​final fricative ‌to this day

​- English ​cognates — sib, ​sibling, and ‌gossip (from ​Old English god-sibb): ​the same ​inherited root of ‌kinship, worn ​down into ‌ordinary words[2]

Sif ​is Tier ‌2 because the ​restoration preserves ‌nothing the ‌ASCII form loses: ​no diacritic, ​no distinctive letter, ​no vowel ​length. English ‌and Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology (the voiced value of medial and final f).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. sif.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet as ​Sif — ​Old Norse ​(North Germanic), ‌attested 13th ​c. CE in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the Poetic ‌Edda (Lokasenna, ​Hárbarðsljóð) and ‌the codices of ​Snorri's Edda, ​recording material ​centuries older ​— beside ​the common noun ‌sif (plural ​sifjar), 'affinity, ‌relation by ​marriage'.[1] Before ‌the Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ‌names were ​carved in ‌the Younger ​Futhark; no runic ​attestation of ‌this theonym ‌survives, so ‌the manuscript ​form is the ​primary witness. ‌The runic ​rendering is ​ᛋᛁᚠ (s-i-f).

‌The scholarly rendering ​proceeds step ​by step:

‌- ᛋ ​(sól) writes ‌/s/.

  • ​ᛁ (ís) writes ​both /i/ ​and /e/; ​here /i/.

‌- ᚠ ‌(fé) writes both ​/f/ and ‌/v/; the ​manuscripts' f ​stood for ‌the voiced fricative ‌heard in ‌modern Icelandic ‌Sif.

  • ​The spelling ‌sif is a ​normalized phonetic ​reconstruction; Younger ​Futhark does ‌not distinguish ​voiced/voiceless fricatives or ​separate short ​and long ‌vowels.
  • ​The Unicode ​restoration Sif is ​therefore identical ‌to the ​ASCII form ‌— the ​restoration is ‌a matter of ​canonical reference, ‌not rescued ‌orthography. The ​name's etymology ​is the common ​noun itself, ​registered in ‌the lexica ‌and never ​seriously disputed.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. sif; de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Brill, 1962), s.v. sif.
05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Sif is ​catalogued under Earth, ‌Harvest — a ‌heading that is ​half attestation and half ​interpretation, and this ​edition keeps the ​two apart. The ​sources themselves give ‌her no office: ​no hall named ​hers, no cult, no ‌domain assigned in ​any stanza. What ‌the tradition attests ​is the household ‌at the centre ​of Þórr's world; ​what modern readers, following ​Grimm, have added ​is the harvest.[1]

​### Earth

Grimm ‌read the golden ​hair as the ‌ripe corn of ​the field — ‌shorn in harvest, regrown ​in gold — ‌and the reading ​made Sif the ‌Germanic earth-and-grain goddess ​beside the corn-mothers ​of folklore. The ‌sources never call her ‌so; but the ‌pairing with Þórr, ​the farmer's weather-god, ​has kept the ‌interpretation alive in ​the handbooks.[2]

​Harvest

The harvest belongs ‌to the same ​interpretive line: the ​shearing and the ‌golden regrowth read ​as the agricultural ‌year in miniature. ​Simek and Turville-Petre ​record the reading while ​warning that the ​myth explains itself ‌well enough as ‌the aetiology of ​the gods' treasures.[3]

‌### Kinship by ​Marriage

The one domain ​the name itself ‌attests. Sif is ‌the Old Norse ‌noun for affinity ‌— the bond ​created by marriage ‌— and the corpus ​honours it: she ​presides over the ​Edda's most blended ‌household, stepmother and ​mother in one, ​and her one ​speech is an ‌act of hospitality.[1]

​The Thunder-God's House

​Snorri's kennings define ​her by the ‌household: Þórr is ​'husband of Sif', ‌and from that ​house come Ullr the ‌archer, and Þrúðr ​of the strength-name. ‌Her sphere in ‌the tradition is ​not a force ​of nature but ​a relation — the ​bond that holds ‌the strongest of ‌the gods to ​a home.[4]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Sif's ​iconography is ‌narrative rather ‌than ​cultic — ​the corpus ​gives ​her no ​temple regalia, ‌but ​fixed around ​her few ‌scenes ​a small ‌set of ​objects ‌no retelling ​can omit:[1]

​- ​The golden ​hair — ​her ‌defining attribute: ​shorn by ‌Loki, ​remade in ‌living gold ​by ‌the sons ​of Ívaldi, ‌and ​read since ​Grimm as ‌the ‌ripe corn ‌of the ​harvest ​field

  • ‌The crystal ​cup ​of mead ‌— her ​single ​speech in ‌Lokasenna: hospitality ​offered to ‌the ​one guest ​no one ​else ​would serve[2]

‌- Mjölnir ‌— ​the hammer ‌that exists ​because ​her hair ‌was cut: ‌the ‌wager's greatest ‌prize, and ​the ‌Æsir's surest ​defence[1]

  • ​Gungnir ​and Skíðblaðnir ‌— Óðinn's ​spear ​and Freyr's ​ship, made ‌in ​the same ​commission that ​remade ‌her hair: ​her restoration ‌underwriting ​the gods' ‌whole arsenal[1]

​- ‌Þrúðheimr — ‌the 'world ​of ​strength', Þórr's ​realm, where ​her ‌household stands ‌among its ​fields[3]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Sif's mythology is short ​in line-count and enormous in consequence: one act ‌of malice against her sets the forges ‌of the dwarfs to work, and the ​gods' six greatest treasures come out of the ​fire. Snorri tells the hair-myth whole; the ​Poetic Edda gives her two smaller scenes; ​Gylfaginning names her son.

The Shearing of ​the Golden Hair (Skáldskaparmál)

Loki Laufeyjarson, Snorri ‌begins, did this for mischief: he cut ​off all Sif's hair. When Þórr learned it, ​he seized Loki and would have broken ‌every bone in him, had Loki not ​sworn to go to the svartálfar and have ‌them make Sif a head of hair ​from gold that would grow like other ‌hair. Loki went to the dwarfs called the ​sons of Ívaldi; they made the hair, ​and it grew to her head the ​moment it was set there, as if it ​were her own. The episode runs to ​a handful of sentences, and Sif does ‌not speak in it — the shearing, the ​oath, and the making all happen around ‌her.[1]

The Wager That Forged Mjölnir ​(Skáldskaparmál)

The sons of Ívaldi made more than ‌the hair: in the same commission came ​Gungnir, the spear that never misses, for ‌Óðinn, and Skíðblaðnir, the best of ships, for ​Freyr. Then Loki wagered his head with ‌the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri ​could not match those three. Sindri laid gold, ​a boar's hide, and iron in the ‌fire in turn while Loki, in fly-shape, ‌stung the bellows-worker — and out came Draupnir ‌the dripping ring, Gullinbursti the golden-bristled boar, ​and Mjölnir, its handle short only because ​the fly stung Brokkr's eyelid at the last ‌draw. The Æsir judged Mjölnir the best ​of all, and Loki saved his head ​on the quibble that his neck was not ‌in the wager. Count the consequence of ​one shorn head of hair: the spear, ​the ship, the ring, the boar, and the ‌hammer all exist because Sif lost hers.[1]

​### The Crystal Cup (Lokasenna 53–54)

In Ægir's ‌hall, as Loki's slander works down the ​company, Sif alone rises and pours him ​mead in a crystal cup: hail to thee, ​Loki — take the cup, and say ​that she alone among the Æsir's kin ‌is without blame. It is the flyting's one ‌courteous stanza, and Loki's answer is its ​coldest: he grants she would be blameless ‌indeed, were it not that he knows one ​who drew the mighty Þórr's wife from ​home — 'and that was I.' The ‌poem offers the accusation and no verdict; Sif ‌speaks no second stanza.[2]

The Taunt ‌on the Shore (Hárbarðsljóð 48)

Óðinn in ‌ferryman's disguise keeps Þórr waiting on the wrong ​side of the sound, and among the ‌gibes flung across the water is one ​aimed at the home Þórr cannot reach: 'Sif ​has a lover at home; him you ​will want to meet.' The poem gives ‌the charge no support and no sequel — ​one dart in a long volley, leaning ​on the same rumour Lokasenna spent a ​stanza on. The scholarly verdict is uniform: the ‌eddic poets twice insinuate and never narrate, ​and the taunts are evidence about the ​sport of needling Þórr, not about the goddess.[3]

​### Ullr, Sif's Son (Gylfaginning 32)

In ‌Snorri's catalogue of the Æsir: 'Ullr is ​the name of a son of Sif and ‌a stepson of Þórr. He is such ​a good archer and so fast on ‌skis that no one can compete with him.' ​Stepson — the word is the whole ‌of it. Sif had a child before ‌the thunder-god, by a father no source names, ​and the speculation that has filled the ​gap is speculation, not attestation. Snorri's kennings ​keep the household straight: Þórr is 'husband of ​Sif, stepfather of Ullr, father of Móði ‌and Magni and Þrúðr' — Magni by ‌the giantess Járnsaxa, Þrúðr counted as Sif's daughter ​in the reference tradition.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Sif has ​attracted exactly ‌one great ‌comparative reading, ​and it begins ​with her ​hair. Jacob ​Grimm took ​the shearing and ‌the golden ​regrowth as ​a nature myth: ‌the hair ​is the ‌ripe corn, ​the shearing the ‌harvest, and ​the dwarfs' ​gold the ​field growing back ​golden — ​a reading ‌that made ​her the Germanic ‌harvest-goddess beside ​Demeter and ‌the corn-mothers ​of folklore, and ‌that matches ​her marriage ‌to Þórr, ​the farmer's weather-god.[1]

​Twentieth-century scholarship ‌cooled on ‌the certainty without ‌abandoning the ​observation. Simek ​and Turville-Petre ‌note that the ​sources never ​call her ‌an earth ​or harvest goddess, ​that her ‌name means ​only 'kinship ‌by marriage', and ​that the ​corn-gold reading ​is interpretation ​laid over a ‌myth that ‌explains itself ​well enough ‌as the aetiology ​of the ​gods' treasures.[2] ‌Within the corpus ‌her closest ‌kin are ‌the figures ​of her own ‌household and ​its story: ​Loki, whose ​mischief she both ‌suffers and, ​in Ægir's ​hall, alone ​tries to answer ‌with courtesy; ​and Ullr, ​the son ​whose father the ‌tradition declined ​to name. ‌The clean ​statement remains the ‌lexicon's: her ​attested sphere ‌is the household ‌and its ​bonds; the ​harvest is ​what modern readers, ​following Grimm, ‌have grown ‌in her ​golden hair.

09

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Sif's afterlife ​runs on ‌two tracks, ‌one scholarly ​and one ​popular. In ​the scholarly line ​she is ​the type-case ‌of the ​Edda's reticence ​about goddesses: ‌a figure ​with no cult ‌record and ​one great ‌myth, whose ​golden hair ​made her, ​from Grimm onward, ​the default ​Germanic corn-goddess ‌of the ​comparative handbooks, ‌and whose ​name — plain ‌sif, 'affinity' ​— became ‌the standing ​example of ‌a theonym ​that is simply ​an abstract ‌noun.[1]

In ‌the popular ‌line her ​golden hair ​carried her ‌into every Victorian ​retelling of ​the gods, ‌and modern ​popular culture ​has borrowed ‌her outright — ​most visibly ‌as the ​Marvel comics ​and film ​character — ​usually trading the ‌wife of ‌the sources ​for a ‌swordswoman. Modern ​Heathen and ​Ásatrú communities ‌keep the older ‌reading, hailing ‌her at ‌harvest rites ​as the ‌golden-haired one, ​patroness of grain, ​marriage, and ​household bonds; ‌in Iceland ​the name ​Sif remains ​a living given ‌name.[2] And ​her quietest ​legacy is ​lexical: the ‌root of ​her name lives ‌on in ​English, unbeknownst ‌to its ​speakers, in ‌sib, sibling, ‌and gossip ​— kinship worn ​down into ​ordinary speech, ​which is ‌exactly what ‌her name ​always meant.[3]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Sif ​is a ‌wholly textual ‌goddess: no ​runestone invokes ​her, ​no votive ​deposit names ​her, and ‌no ​image can ​be securely ‌identified with ​her — ‌a ​silence she ‌shares with ​most of ​the ásynjur, ​who ​appear in ​verse and ‌prose but ​rarely, ‌if ever, ​in dedications.[1] ‌Her attestation ​hangs on ‌the ​manuscript witnesses ‌of the ​two Eddas: ​the ‌Codex Regius ‌of the ‌Poetic Edda ​for Lokasenna ​and ‌Hárbarðsljóð, and ​the codices ​of Snorri's ‌Edda for ​the ​Skáldskaparmál hair-myth ‌and the ​Gylfaginning genealogy ‌that ​names Ullr ​her son.

​No certain ​place-name contains ‌her ‌name, and ​no Viking-Age ‌carving of ​the theonym ​has ‌ever been ‌read. The ‌material afterlife ‌of ​her myth ‌is real ​but indirect: ​the treasures ​her ‌shearing produced ​— Mjölnir ​above all ​— ‌are among ​the most ​depicted objects ​of the ‌Viking ​Age, worn ‌as amulets ​in thousands ‌of graves, ​so ‌that the ‌consequence of ​her one ​great ​scene is ​archaeologically everywhere ‌while she ‌herself is ​nowhere.[2]

Sources

  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Sif, Mjöllnir.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda gives ‌Sif two ‌scenes and ​no poem ​of her ​own. In ​Lokasenna 53 ​she rises ‌in Ægir's hall ​and pours ​Loki mead ‌in a ​crystal cup ‌— the ​flyting's one ‌courteous stanza, ​asking only ​to be counted ​blameless among ​the Æsir's ​kin — ‌and in ​stanza 54 ‌Loki answers ​with the ‌accusation that he ​himself lay ‌with her. ​The poem ‌moves on ​without verdict, ​and she ‌does not ‌speak again.[1]

‌In Hárbarðsljóð 48, ​Óðinn in ​ferryman's disguise ‌flings the ​same rumour ​across the ‌water at ​Þórr — ​'Sif has ‌a lover at ​home' — ‌one dart ​in a ​long volley ​of gibes, ​given no ‌support and ‌no sequel.[2] ​Grímnismál 4 supplies ‌the household's ​address: Þrúðheimr, ​the world ‌of strength, ‌where Þórr ‌dwells 'until ‌the gods ​are destroyed' ‌— the fields ​her family ​farms, held ​in a ‌poem that ​never says ​her name.[3] ​The pattern ‌across the poems ​is consistent: ​the eddic ​tradition knows ‌Sif as ​the centre ‌of Þórr's ​household and ‌as the ​subject of taunts ‌it never ‌substantiates — ​evidence, as ​the handbooks ​note, about ​the poets' ‌sport and ‌not about ​the goddess.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Sif's ‌great witness. ​Skáldskaparmál tells ​the ​hair-myth whole: ​Loki's shearing, ​Þórr's near-fatal ‌rage, ​the oath ​that sent ‌Loki to ​the svartálfar, ‌and ​the forges ‌that answered ​— the ​sons of ​Ívaldi ​making the ​golden hair, ‌Gungnir, and ​Skíðblaðnir, ‌then Brokkr ​and Sindri, ‌at Loki's ​wagered head, ‌making ​Draupnir, Gullinbursti, ‌and Mjölnir. ​Snorri's kenning ​lists ‌thereafter define ‌Þórr as ‌'husband of ​Sif' and ​'stepfather ‌of Ullr', ​keeping the ​goddess present ‌in the ​poets' ​shorthand even ‌where no ​narrative needs ‌her.[1]

​Gylfaginning 32 ​supplies the ​genealogy that ​opens the ‌one ‌unexplained door ​in her ‌story: Ullr, ​the peerless ​archer ‌on skis, ‌is 'a ‌son of ‌Sif ​and a ‌stepson of ​Þórr' — ​proof of ​a ‌union before ​the thunder-god, ​about which ​Snorri, ‌like every ​other source, ​says nothing.[2] ​The ásynja ‌þulur ​preserved with ‌Skáldskaparmál hold ​her in ‌the catalogue ​of ‌the goddesses ‌by name: ​a deity ​of ​few scenes, ​kept with ‌bureaucratic fidelity ‌among the ​ásynjur.[3]

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains no ‌invocation ​of the ​goddess Sif. ​No ​runestone asks ​her favour, ‌no ​amulet bears ​her name, ‌and ​no inscription ‌yet found ​links ‌a dedicant ​to her ​cult ​— a ​silence she ​shares ‌with most ​of ‌the ásynjur ​and one ‌that ​marks her ‌as a ​figure ‌of verse ​and prose ​rather ‌than of ‌independent dedication.[1]

‌What ​can be ​said runologically ‌is ​the rendering ​itself: in ‌the ​Younger Futhark ​the name ‌would ​have been ‌carved ᛋᛁᚠ ​(s-i-f) ​— ᛋ ​(sól) writes ​/s/, ‌ᛁ (ís) ‌writes both ​/i/ ‌and /e/, ​and ᚠ ​(fé) ‌writes both ‌/f/ and ‌/v/, ‌so the ​sixteen-rune row ‌underspecifies ​nothing that ​matters in ​this ‌name. The ​spelling ​is a ​normalized phonetic ‌reconstruction; ​Younger Futhark ​does not ​distinguish ‌voiced and ​voiceless fricatives ‌or ​vowel quantities. ‌No Viking-Age ​carving ‌of the ‌theonym is ​known; ​the manuscript ​spelling of ​the ‌Eddas remains ‌the primary ​witness.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sif.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. sif.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​two Eddas, ‌Sif's medieval ‌presence is ​thin but ​real, and ​it runs ​through the ​skalds. Snorri's ‌kenning apparatus ​in Skáldskaparmál ​preserves the ‌poets' standard ​address to her ‌husband — ​Þórr is ‌'husband of ​Sif', 'friend ​of Sif' ​— which ​proves the ​goddess was ‌a fixed ​point in ‌the skaldic ​repertoire from the ‌Viking Age ​forward: the ‌kenning works ​only if ‌every listener ​already knows ​whose wife ‌she is.[1]

‌Saxo Grammaticus, ‌who gives ​so many ​Norse figures ‌a second Danish ​life, gives ​Sif none: ‌his euhemerized ​Thor appears ​in the ‌Gesta Danorum ​without a ‌wife, and ​the hair-myth ​finds no ​place in ​his telling — ‌a reminder ‌that even ​the greatest ‌Eddic episodes ​did not ​travel in ‌every direction.[2] ‌The legendary ‌and chivalric ‌sagas add ​nothing further; ‌her medieval ​dossier closes where ​it began, ​with the ‌two Eddas. ​What the ​later tradition ​does preserve ‌is her ​household: Ullr's ​place in ​the þulur ‌and genealogies ​keeps her named ‌as his ​mother wherever ‌the archer-god ​is catalogued, ‌and the ‌reference tradition ​counts Þrúðr ​her daughter ​— the ​kinship her ‌name promises, ‌documented to ​the end.[3]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Sif is ​the Edda's ‌study in how ‌much weight ​a silent figure ​can bear. ​She speaks one ​stanza in ​the whole corpus ‌— a ​cup of mead, ​a courteous ‌wish to be ​counted blameless ‌— and is ​answered with ‌the flyting's crudest ​accusation. She ​is sheared, restored, ​taunted about, ​and never once ​given an ‌adventure of her ​own; and ‌yet the tradition ​built the ‌gods' entire armoury ​on her ‌injury. Gungnir, Skíðblaðnir, ​Draupnir, Gullinbursti, ‌Mjölnir: take away ​Loki's shears ​and the Æsir ‌face Ragnarǫk ‌unarmed. The myths ‌do not ​say this. They ​simply arrange ‌that the greatest ​weapons in ​the North descend ‌from a ​wrong done to ​the woman ‌whose name means ​kinship.[1]

Perhaps ‌that is the ​point the ​name was always ​making. Sif ​is not the ‌goddess of ‌a thing — ​not thunder, ‌not wisdom, not ​the sea ​— but of ‌a relation: ‌the bond that ‌makes a ‌household out of ​separate lives. ‌The corpus honours ​her in ​its own idiom ​by making ‌her household the ​most blended ​in Ásgarðr — ​a stepson ‌of unknown father, ​sons by ​a giantess, a ​daughter of ‌strength — and ​by routing ‌every treasure of ​the gods ‌through her golden ​head. Grimm ‌read the hair ‌as corn; ​the Edda read ​it as ​kinship. Both readings ​agree on ‌the essential: what ‌she is, ​grows back.[2]

  2. Grimm, Jacob, Teutonic Mythology, trans. Stallybrass (1882–88), on Sif; de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Brill, 1962), s.v. sif.
