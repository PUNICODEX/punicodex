The stress test for Dakṣa. Every seat in the atlas was attacked with the same question — is the match real, or is it poetry? — and the survivors are recorded with their evidence.

The Test

Seat 1: Ancestry & Heritage Services

Claim: Father of sixty daughters; the lineage-map of gods, beasts, and stars. Exhibit: Satī and the Ascetic — His youngest daughter Satī loved Śiva — the ash-smeared wanderer whom Dakṣa considered no fit match for a Prajāpati's house.

Verdict: holds under load. The correspondence is functional, not decorative.

Seat 2: Education, Research & Knowledge

Claim: The skilled one; competence as a sacred discipline. Exhibit: Satī's Self-Immolation — Satī came uninvited to her father's rite and endured his contempt until she could not: declaring she would no longer wear a body born of one who insulted her lord, she entered the sacrificial fire and burned.

Verdict: stands. Strip both to their function and the functions match.

Seat 3: Leadership, Governance & Public Administration

Claim: The Prajāpati; order, procedure, and the cautionary tale of both. Exhibit: Vīrabhadra Unleashed — Śiva tore a lock from his hair and cast down Vīrabhadra, who wrecked the sacrifice: Bhaga's eyes were knocked out, Pūṣan's teeth smashed, the gods routed — and Dakṣa beheaded.

Verdict: survives scrutiny. The myth and the trade do the same work.

The Control Group

Against the seats that survived, one control: the temple's own summary of its figure — Dakṣa's cycle is the Indian study of procedure versus devotion: the man who performs the rite perfectly and loses everything because of who was left off the list. The seats do not drift far from it. That is the audit's cleanest result.

The Method, Stated Plainly

The atlas is not a horoscope. Seats are earned from the sources — argued, weighted, published — and every one of them can be challenged at the methodology page. A match that cannot survive an argument does not survive review. That is the whole trick: the patterns are claims, and claims can be tested.
