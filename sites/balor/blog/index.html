The hidden history behind Balor

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Balor is the opposite case, and the more instructive one: the most feared antagonist in Irish mythology, attached to a name so plain that Old Irish itself wrote it in unbroken ASCII. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, sound, eye, tower, sling-stone, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the king of the Fomorians has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII balor.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

  • Restored name: Balor
  • ASCII form: balor
  • Meaning: "Fomorian king whose destructive eye blasts armies when its lid is raised, slain by his grandson Lúg with a sling-stone at Cath Maige Tuired."
  • Domain of influence: Destruction, Evil Eye, Fomorians

Overview

Balor (balor) — Destruction, Evil Eye, Fomorians — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Destruction, Evil Eye, Fomorians". The name's gloss in the lexicon is descriptive, not etymological, because the etymology itself is unresolved.

Balor mac Doit meic Néit — Balor son of Dot, grandson of Nét — is king of the Fomorians, the sea-borne oppressors of medieval Irish tradition, and owner of the corpus's most feared weapon: a single eye, poisoned in his boyhood by the fumes of a druids' charm-brew, whose gaze made any army it looked upon worthless, though it were many thousands strong. His daughter Ethniu bore Lugh to Cian; and at the second battle of Mag Tuired it was the grandson's sling-stone that drove the eye backward through Balor's head, so that it was his own host that looked into it.

PuniCodex restores the name as Balor — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Irish writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the figure. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII balor.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old and Middle Irish as Balor, and its etymology is one of the honest open questions of Irish onomastics. No derivation commands general assent; the modern reference works register the proposals rather than endorse one. One scholarly line has connected the name with a root for 'blow, strike' — a reading that would make the name say the same thing as his standing epithet — but the dispute is genuine, and this temple registers it without settling it.

What the tradition itself supplies is the epithet: Balor Béimnech, or Bailcbéimnech, 'Balor of the Mighty Blows', 'the Strong-Smiter'. Whether or not the epithet etymologizes the name, it perfectly glosses the man: he is the one who strikes, and the whole corpus knows him by the blow. The Donegal oral tradition keeps its own title — Balor na Súile Nimhe, 'Balor of the Evil Eye' — under which the tower story and the stolen cow were still being told when the nineteenth-century collectors wrote them down.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in medieval Irish manuscripts in the Insular Latin script as Balor — the hand of the great codices that preserve Cath Maige Tuired and the Lebor Gabála Érenn. That is the primary witness, and it is plain ASCII.

The earlier monumental alphabet, ogham, predates the manuscript tradition and survives on some four hundred stones bearing personal names — but no attestation of this name has ever been read on any of them, and none would be expected: ogham stones mark graves and boundaries, not the names of gods. The epigraphic silence is characteristic of Irish paganism, which left literature rather than dedications.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈba.lor/ — Old Irish Reconstruction.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'BAH-lor' — the first syllable stressed hard, the l dark and broad, the final r tapped; the modern Irish Balor keeps essentially this shape.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Irish — Balor, mac Doit meic Néit, 'son of Dot, grandson of Nét'
  • Epithets — Balor Béimnech / Bailcbéimnech, 'of the Mighty Blows', 'the Strong-Smiter'
  • Folk titles — Balor na Súile Nimhe, 'Balor of the Evil Eye', of the Donegal oral tradition
  • Modern Irish — Balor, the unbroken living form

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Irish word. All three consonants are broad — velarized, with the back of the tongue raised — which Irish spelling marks by the surrounding vowels, not by any letter the ASCII alphabet lacks.

Mythology

Balor's mythology is a single arc — lineage, weapon, prophecy, and the stroke that answers all three — told between one great medieval text and the oral tradition that completes it.

Balor Son of Dot, Grandson of Nét (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

The book of invasions fixes his place before his deeds: Balor mac Doit meic Néit, king of the Fomorians, the sea-going race that had pressed on Ireland since the age of the Nemedians. His wife was Cethlenn, who dealt the Dagda the wound at Mag Tuired of which the All-Father later died at the Brug. His daughter was Ethniu, given to Cian, son of Dian Cécht — and their son was Lugh, the Samildánach, equally skilled in all arts. Thus the Fomorian king was the grandfather of the Túatha Dé Danann's deliverer: the genealogy the whole tragedy of Mag Tuired turns on.

How the Eye Was Poisoned (Cath Maige Tuired)

The battle-text explains the weapon before it uses it. Balor had a destructive eye, never opened except on a battlefield, and four men were needed to raise its lid with a polished handle passed through it. Any army that eye looked upon was made worthless, though it were many thousands strong. The poison had a childhood: when Balor was a boy, his father's druids were brewing a charm, and the boy looked in on the work; the fumes of the brew rose and struck the eye, and from that day its gaze was venom. Power taken in unguarded, as a child takes in smoke, and carried incurably for a lifetime.

The Tower and the Prophecy (the Oral Tradition of Tory)

The medieval text knows that Ethniu bore Lugh; how that came to be is told by the oral tradition of the north-west, collected in the nineteenth century on and around Tory Island, Balor's sea-girt fortress off the Donegal coast. A druid foretold that Balor would die by his own grandson's hand, and the king answered the prophecy with masonry: he locked his daughter Eithne in a tower on the rock, with twelve matrons to keep every man from her sight. Then Balor coveted and stole the Glas Gaibhnenn, the grey-green cow of Cian — and Cian crossed to Tory for redress, won into the tower with the druidess Biróg's help, and to Eithne's side. When the child was born Balor had him cast into the sea; the sea gave him back, fostered and hidden, until a young stranger called Lugh came to Nuadu's court at Tara and was asked what art he practiced.

The Sling-Stone at Mag Tuired (Cath Maige Tuired)

At the height of the second battle Balor came against the Túatha Dé Danann with the Fomorian host, destroying and slaughtering, and commanded that the lid of his eye be raised to blast the enemy. As the four men set the handle to the lid, Lugh cast a sling-stone at him which carried the eye backward through his head, so that it was his own host that looked into it. Balor fell on top of the Fomorian fighting-men, and twenty-seven of them died beneath his side. The weapon the Fomorians had built their tyranny on spent its last gaze on its owners — and the battle turned with the eye. Afterward the tribute was taken back from the defeated host, and the power of the Fomorians over Ireland was broken for good.

Symbols & Iconography

Balor's iconography is a weapon, the machinery that serves it, and the two objects of the oral tradition that explain his doom:

  • The venomed eye — poisoned in boyhood at the druids' brew; the gaze that made armies worthless
  • The polished lid-lifter — four men with a handle passed through the eyelid: kingship reduced to machinery
  • The tower of Tory — the sea-girt prison built against the prophecy: the fortress that made the doom it feared
  • The Glas Gaibhnenn — the grey-green cow Balor stole from Cian: the theft that set the grandson's birth in motion
  • The sling-stone — the small, aimed answer to the vast, unaimed gaze: the grandson's hand the druids foretold

Archaeology & Evidence

Balor is a textual and oral figure: no ogham stone names him, no votive inscription invokes him, and Irish paganism generally left literature rather than dedications. His manuscript witnesses are exact — Cath Maige Tuired survives uniquely in the sixteenth-century British Library MS Harley 5280, the basis of Elizabeth A. Gray's edition, while the Lebor Gabála Érenn is preserved in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster and its sister codices. His geographical anchor is one of the most dramatic pieces of coastline in Europe: Tory Island (Toraigh), nine miles off the Bloody Foreland of Donegal, whose cliffs and sea-stacks — above all the great rock called Tór Mór — the oral tradition names as his fortress and the site of Eithne's tower. The nineteenth-century collectors who wrote the tower story down were recording a living Donegal tradition anchored to those rocks.

Realm & Domain

Balor's domain is Destruction, Evil Eye, Fomorians — three terms that are, in his case, one fact stated three ways.

Destruction

His eye is the corpus's purest weapon of ruin, and he is given nothing else — no craft, no counsel, no feast, nothing but the blow his epithet names.

The Evil Eye

The boyhood accident at the druids' brew grounds the mythic weapon in the living Irish and Mediterranean folk-belief of the withering gaze — the look that blights cattle, milk, and men.

The Fomorians

He is king of Ireland's mythic adversaries, the sea-going people who exacted the island's produce as tribute until Mag Tuired broke the yoke.

Sovereignty That Poisons Its Possessor

Taken together, the three terms describe a single figure: kingship reduced to a weapon its owner cannot operate alone and cannot survive.

Across Cultures

Balor's clearest cross-border witnesses are comparative, not cognate. The Welsh corpus preserves no figure derived from the same name — the Brythonic silence is total. The parallels the scholarship leans on are typological: the Greek Acrisius, who prisoned his daughter Danae in a bronze chamber because a prophecy named her son his slayer, and died by that grandson Perseus all the same — the Eithne tower is the same tale-shape; the one-eyed monster answered by aimed cunning, the Polyphemus pattern, where the single enormous eye is both the ogre's strength and the one place he can be struck; and, nearest home, the Irish and Mediterranean complex of the evil eye itself, the living folk-ground of his venomed gaze. O'Rahilly read Balor further, as a figure of death and the western sea; the handbooks register the reading without making it doctrine. Within the Irish tradition, his structural kin are the adversaries and the avengers his war sets in motion: Nuadu, Lugh, and the Mórrígan.

Cultural Legacy

Balor entered the modern world through two doors. The first was scholarship: Lady Gregory's Gods and Fighting Men (1904) carried the sling-stone and the tower into English reading, and O'Rahilly's Early Irish History and Mythology (1946) made him the type-case of the Irish death-king — a scholarly afterlife as large as his medieval dossier is short. The second was the folklore itself: the Donegal oral versions — the tower, the stolen cow, the child cast into the sea — were collected by O'Donovan, Curtin, and Larminie in the nineteenth century and never stopped being retold, and 'Balor of the Evil Eye' remains his standing name in Irish and in English. In the modern imagination he has become the Celtic dark lord par excellence: the remote tyrant in his island fortress, answered not by a greater weapon but by the prophesied grandchild the fortress was built to prevent. Tory Island keeps his address; the Irish language never lost his name.

A Meditation

Every part of Balor's story is about containment failing. The eye is contained — lidded, lifted only by machinery — and containment is the whole catastrophe: a gaze so dangerous it must be operated by four other men is no longer a weapon but an occupation. The daughter is contained in a tower against a prophecy, and the tower is the prophecy's instrument: no tower, no Cian, no Lugh. And the answer the tradition gives him is exact — not a bigger eye, not a stronger king, but a sling-stone, small and aimed, thrown by the one hand the whole machinery existed to forestall. The venom that could wither an army is turned in a single instant into the Fomorians' own epitaph: it was his own host that looked into it. That is the Irish judgment on every power built to wither rather than to make — it ends by looking inward.

The Unicode Restoration

Balor is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Irish Balor carries none of these. The manuscripts write five plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the Fomorian king; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Balor mean? The etymology is unresolved — no derivation commands general assent, though one line connects the name with a root for 'blow, strike', matching his epithet Béimnech, 'of the Mighty Blows'. The lexicon therefore glosses the name descriptively: the Fomorian king whose destructive eye blasts armies, slain by his grandson Lúg's sling-stone at Cath Maige Tuired.

Which tradition does Balor belong to? Balor is catalogued in the Celtic pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon — specifically the Irish Mythological Cycle.

Why is Balor classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Old Irish form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the figure's standing is beside the point.

Is balor.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

How does Balor die in the sources? At the second battle of Mag Tuired: as four men raise the lid of his destructive eye, Lugh casts a sling-stone that drives the eye backward through Balor's head, so that its gaze blasts the Fomorian host itself.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines inherit whatever spelling the web normalizes — and for a plain-ASCII name, normalization is not the risk; confusion is. Balor the Fomorian king, Balor of the fantasy novel and the game boss, balor.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps them sorted.
