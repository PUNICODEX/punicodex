01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Balor (balor) ​— Destruction, Evil ‌Eye, Fomorians · ‌the Fomorian king ​of the venomed eye ​— is the ​great antagonist of ​medieval Irish myth: ​Balor mac Doit ‌meic Néit, 'Balor ​son of Dot, ​grandson of Nét', ‌king of the Fomorians, ​the sea-borne oppressors ‌who taxed Ireland ​from the outer ‌islands until the ​second battle of ​Mag Tuired broke ​them.[1] His weapon ​is the most feared ​in the corpus: ‌a single eye, ​poisoned in his ‌boyhood by the ​fumes of a ‌druids' charm-brew, whose ​gaze made any ‌army it looked upon ​worthless though it ‌were many thousands ​strong — an ​eye never opened ‌save on a ‌battlefield, its lid ‌raised by four ​men with a polished ​handle.[1]

He is ‌also, by the ​tradition's own reckoning, ​the grandfather of ‌his destroyer. His ​daughter Ethniu bore ​Lugh to Cian, ‌son of Dian Cécht; ​and at Mag ‌Tuired it was ​Lugh's sling-stone that ​drove the eye ​backward through Balor's ​head, so that ‌it was his ‌own host that looked ​into it, and ‌the Fomorian power ​over Ireland ended ​under his fall.[1] ‌The Lebor Gabála ‌Érenn frames his ‌lineage and records ‌his wife Cethlenn's revenge ​— the wound ‌she dealt the ​Dagda at Mag ​Tuired, of which ​the All-Father later ‌died at the ​Brug.[2]

PuniCodex restores ​the name as Balor ​— identical to ‌the ASCII input, ​because normalized Old ​Irish writes no ​diacritic in this ‌name. The mechanical ​rule therefore places ‌it in Tier 2: ​nothing distinctive is ‌lost in transit, ​a statement about ‌orthography and never ‌about the figure. ​No domain is ​claimed: the plain-ASCII ​balor.com is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The theonym ​is attested in Old ‌and Middle Irish ‌as Balor, and its ​etymology is one ​of the honest open ​questions of Irish ​onomastics: no derivation ​commands general assent, and ‌the modern reference ​works register the proposals ​rather than endorse ‌one.[1] The tradition's own ​explanatory apparatus is ‌the epithet — Balor ​Béimnech or Bailcbéimnech, ‌'Balor of the Mighty ​Blows', 'the Strong-Smiter' ​— which glosses not ​the name's origin ​but its owner's function: ​he is the ‌one who strikes, ​and the whole corpus ‌knows him by ​the blow.[2] One scholarly ‌line has connected ​the name with a ‌root for 'blow, ​strike', which would make ‌epithet and name ​say the same thing; ​this edition records ‌the proposal without settling ‌it, and the ‌lexicon's meaning is given ​descriptively rather than ​etymologically.

His full ‌manuscript style is genealogical: ​Balor mac Doit ​meic Néit, 'Balor son ‌of Dot, grandson ​of Nét' — the ​ancestry the Lebor ‌Gabála and Cath Maige ​Tuired agree on.[3] ‌The Donegal oral tradition ​keeps its own ​standing title, Balor na ​Súile Nimhe, 'Balor ​of the Evil Eye', ‌the name under ‌which the tower ​story and the stolen ‌cow were still ​being told when the ​nineteenth-century collectors wrote ‌them down.[4]

The ASCII ‌form balor loses ‌nothing, because the normalized ‌Old Irish spelling ​is already plain Latin ‌letters; the restoration ​is therefore a matter ​of canonical reference, ​not rescued diacritics, and ‌the name is ​classified Tier 2 ​by the project's mechanical ​rule.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • b ​→ B — ​Same, capitalized
  • a ​→ a — ‌Same
  • l → ​l — Same

‌- o → o ​— Same

  • ‌r → r — ​Same

The project ‌holds no domain for ‌this name: balor.com ​is registered to ​a third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is watched for ‌release. The temple ‌lives at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈba.lor/ ​— Old ​Irish Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • Ba- ​— Broad ‌bilabial stop ​[b] and ​short open ‌[a], stressed ​on the ‌first syllable ​as in all ‌Old Irish

​- -lor ​— Broad ​(velarized) dental ​lateral [l̪ˠ], ​the dark ‌Irish l; short ​[o]; a ‌broad tapped ​or trilled ‌final [rˠ], ​the second ‌syllable unstressed

​For the modern ‌speaker, the ​closest approximation ​is: 'BAH-lor' ‌— stress ‌the first ‌syllable hard, ​keep the ​l dark and ‌broad, and ​let the ​final r ‌stay tapped; ​the modern ​Irish Balor ‌keeps essentially this ​shape.

Kindred ‌and historical ​forms of ​the name:

​- Old ​Irish — ‌Balor, mac Doit ‌meic Néit, ​'son of ‌Dot, grandson ​of Nét'
​- Epithets ‌— Balor ‌Béimnech / ‌Bailcbéimnech, 'of the ‌Mighty Blows', ​'the Strong-Smiter'[2]
‌- Folk ​titles — ​Balor na ​Súile Nimhe, ‌'Balor of the ​Evil Eye', ​of the ​Donegal oral ‌tradition[3]

  • ​Modern Irish ​— Balor, ​the unbroken living ‌form

Balor ​is Tier ‌2 because ​the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​ASCII loses: ‌the letters ‌of the manuscript ​form are ​the letters ​of the ​keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval Irish ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin alphabet ​as Balor ​— Old Irish ​/ Middle ​Irish in Insular ‌script, attested ​in the codices ​that preserve ‌Cath Maige ​Tuired and Lebor ‌Gabála Érenn.[1] ​The earlier monumental ‌alphabet, ogham, ​predates the manuscript ​tradition, but ​no attestation of ​this name ​has ever been ‌read on ​an ogham stone; ‌the manuscript ​form is the ‌primary witness.[2]

​The scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

  • ‌Old Irish ​was written in ​the Latin ‌alphabet using ‌Insular minuscule from ‌the early ​medieval period; the ​spelling Balor ‌requires no diacritic.

​- The ​manuscripts give the ‌name with ​its genealogy and ​epithets: Balor ‌mac Doit meic ​Néit, 'son ‌of Dot, grandson ​of Nét'; ​Balor Béimnech, 'of ​the Mighty ​Blows'.[3]

  • The ‌consonants are ‌broad — a ​velarized b, ‌l, and r ​that Irish ​orthography marks ‌by the surrounding ‌vowels, not ‌by any letter ‌ASCII lacks; ​nothing distinctive is ‌lost in ​transit.
  • The ​Unicode restoration ​Balor is therefore ‌identical to ​the ASCII form ​apart from ​the capital; the ‌temple form ​is a canonical ​reference, not ​a rescued spelling.

‌One caution ​attaches to the ‌record: the ​name's etymology ‌is unresolved, and ​this edition ‌registers the proposals ‌— including ​the connection with ​a root ​for 'blow, strike' ​that would ‌match the epithet ‌— without ​endorsing one.[4]

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Balor is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌'Destruction, Evil ​Eye, Fomorians' ​— three ​terms that are, ​in his ​case, one ‌fact stated ​three ways.[1]

​### Destruction

‌His eye is ​the corpus's ‌purest weapon ​of ruin: ‌whatever army ​it looked ​upon was made ​worthless, though ​it were ​many thousands ‌strong. He ​is given no ‌craft, no ​counsel, no ‌feast — ​nothing but ‌the blow, ​whence his epithet ‌Béimnech, 'of ​the Mighty ​Blows'.[1]

‌The Evil ‌Eye

The ‌boyhood accident at ​the druids' ​brew grounds ‌the mythic ​weapon in ​the living ‌Irish and Mediterranean ​folk-belief of ​the withering ‌gaze — ​the look ‌that blights ​cattle, milk, and ​men. Sjoestedt ​and Mac ​Cana both ‌treat that ‌folk complex ​as the ground ‌on which ​Balor's eye ​was imagined.[2]

‌### The ‌Fomorians

He ‌is king of ‌the Fomorians, ​the sea-going ‌adversaries of ​Irish myth ​who exacted ​Ireland's produce as ‌tribute from ​the outer ​islands — ​under Balor ‌and Indech the ​yoke that ​the second ​battle of ‌Mag Tuired ​finally broke.[1]

‌### Sovereignty That ​Poisons Its ‌Possessor

Taken ​together, the ‌three terms ‌describe a ​single figure: kingship ​reduced to ​a weapon ​its owner ‌cannot operate ‌alone and ​cannot survive.[3]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Balor's ​attributes are ‌a weapon, ‌the machinery ​that serves ​it, ​and the ​two objects ​of the ‌oral ​tradition that ​explain his ‌doom:[1]

  • ​The venomed ‌eye ​— Poisoned ‌in boyhood ​by the ​fumes of ​his ​father's druids' ​charm-brew; the ‌gaze that ​made ‌armies worthless ​— power ‌taken in ​unguarded and ‌carried ​incurably
  • ‌The polished ​lid-lifter — ​Four ‌men with ‌a handle ‌passed through ​the eyelid: ​kingship ‌reduced to ​machinery, a ​gaze other ‌men must ​operate

​- The ‌tower of ​Tory — ‌The ​sea-girt prison ​built against ​the druid's ​prophecy, where ‌Eithne ‌was kept ​from every ‌man's sight ​— the ​fortress ‌that made ‌the doom ‌it feared[2]
‌- ​The Glas ‌Gaibhnenn — ​The grey-green ​cow Balor ​stole ‌from Cian ​in the ​oral tradition: ​the ‌theft that ​set the ​grandson's birth ​in motion[2]
‌- ​The sling-stone ‌— The ​small, aimed ‌answer to ​the ‌vast, unaimed ‌gaze, cast ​by Lugh ​at ​Mag Tuired ​— the ‌grandson's hand ‌the prophecy ​foretold[1]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Balor's mythology is ​a single arc — lineage, weapon, ‌prophecy, and the stroke that answers ‌all three — told between one ​great medieval text and the oral ​tradition that completes it.[1]

​Balor Son of Dot, Grandson of ​Nét (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

The book ​of invasions fixes his place before ‌his deeds: Balor mac Doit meic ​Néit, king of the Fomorians, ​whose wife Cethlenn dealt the Dagda ‌the wound at Mag Tuired of ​which the All-Father later died at ‌the Brug — and whose daughter ​Ethniu bore Lugh to Cian, son ‌of Dian Cécht, making the ​Fomorian king the grandfather of the ​Túatha Dé Danann's deliverer.[2]

How ​the Eye Was Poisoned (Cath Maige ​Tuired)

The battle-text explains the weapon ​before it uses it. Balor ‌had a destructive eye, never opened ​except on a battlefield; four men ‌raised the lid with a polished ​handle passed through it, and any ‌army the eye looked upon ​was made worthless, though it were ‌many thousands strong. The poison had ​a childhood: as a boy he ‌looked in on his father's druids ​brewing a charm, and the ​fumes of the brew struck the ‌eye and venomed it for life.[1]

‌### The Tower and the Prophecy ‌(the Oral Tradition of Tory)

How ​Ethniu came to bear Lugh ​is told by the north-western oral ‌tradition, collected in the nineteenth century ​around Tory Island, Balor's sea-girt fortress. ​A druid foretold that Balor would ‌die by his own grandson's ​hand; the king prisoned his daughter ​in a tower on the rock ‌with twelve matrons for guard. But ​Balor coveted and stole the Glas ‌Gaibhnenn, the grey-green cow of Cian, ​and Cian crossed to Tory ​for redress; with the druidess Biróg's ​help he won into the tower ​and to Eithne's side. When the ‌child was born, Balor had him ‌cast into the sea — ​and the sea gave him back, ‌fostered and hidden, until a young ​stranger came to Nuadu's court and ​was asked what art he practiced.[3]

‌### The Sling-Stone at Mag ‌Tuired (Cath Maige Tuired)

At the ‌height of the second battle Balor ‌came against the Túatha Dé Danann ​with the Fomorian host, destroying and ‌slaughtering, and commanded that the ​lid of his eye be raised ​to blast the enemy. As the ​four men set the handle to ‌the lid, Lugh cast a sling-stone ​at him which carried the ​eye backward through his head, so ​that it was his own host ‌that looked into it; Balor fell ​on top of the Fomorian fighting-men, ​and twenty-seven of them died ​beneath his side. The weapon the ‌Fomorians had built their tyranny on ​spent its last gaze on its ‌owners, and the battle turned with ​the eye.[1]

The Price of ‌the War (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

​The tradition closes Balor's account slowly: ‌the tribute was taken back from ‌the defeated host, the Fomorian power ​over Ireland was broken for good ​— and years later, at ​the Brug on the Boyne, the ​Dagda died of the wound that ‌Cethlenn, Balor's wife, had dealt him ‌in the battle, the long-settled price ​of Balor's war.[2]

Sources

  1. Gray, Elizabeth A., ed. and trans., Cath Maige Tuired, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982).
  2. Macalister, R. A. S., ed. and trans., Lebor Gabála Érenn (Dublin: Irish Texts Society, 1938–56).
  3. Curtin, Jeremiah, Hero-Tales of Ireland (Macmillan, 1894); Larminie, William, West Irish Folk-Tales and Romances (Elliot Stock, 1893); and O'Donovan's note in the Annals of the Four Masters (1856).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Balor's clearest ​cross-border witnesses are ‌comparative, not ‌cognate. The Welsh ​corpus preserves no ​figure derived ​from the same ​name — the ​Brythonic silence ‌is total, as ​it is for ​so much ‌of the Fomorian ​cycle.[1] The parallels ‌the scholarship ​actually leans on ‌are typological.

First, ​the Greek ​Acrisius: the king ​who prisoned his ​daughter Danae ​in a bronze ‌chamber because a ​prophecy named ‌her son his ​slayer, and died ‌by that ​grandson Perseus all ‌the same. The ​Eithne tower ‌is the same ​tale-shape, and comparatists ​have set ‌the two side ‌by side since ‌the nineteenth ​century. Second, the ​one-eyed monster answered ‌by aimed ​cunning — the ​Polyphemus pattern — ‌where the ​single enormous eye ​is both the ‌ogre's strength ​and the one ‌place he can ​be struck. ​Third, and nearest ​home, the Irish ​and Mediterranean ‌complex of the ‌evil eye itself: ​the belief ‌that a look ​can wither cattle, ​milk, and ‌men, which Sjoestedt ‌and Mac Cana ‌both treat ‌as the living ​folk-ground of Balor's ‌venomed gaze.[2]

​Within the Irish ​tradition his structural ​kin are ‌the adversaries and ​the avengers his ​war sets ​in motion: Nuadu, ‌the king whose ​host he ​broke; Lugh, the ​grandson the fortress ‌was built ​to prevent; and ‌the Mórrígan, whose ​war-counsel stood ‌on the other ​side of Mag ‌Tuired. O'Rahilly ‌read him further, ​as a figure ​of death ​and the western ​sea; the handbooks ‌register the ‌reading without making ​it doctrine.[3]

  3. O'Rahilly, T. F., Early Irish History and Mythology (DIAS, 1946).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Balor ​entered the ‌modern world ‌through two ​doors. The ​first was ​scholarship: Lady ​Gregory's Gods ​and Fighting ‌Men (1904) ​carried the ​sling-stone and ‌the tower ​into ‌English reading, ​and O'Rahilly's ‌Early Irish ​History and ​Mythology (1946) ​made him ​the type-case ​of the ‌Irish death-king ​— a ‌scholarly afterlife ​as ‌large as ​his medieval ‌dossier is ​short.[1]

The ‌second door ​was the ​folklore itself. ‌The Donegal ‌oral versions ‌— the ​tower, the ​stolen cow, ‌the ​child cast ​into the ‌sea — ​were collected ​in the ‌nineteenth century ​by O'Donovan, ‌Curtin, and ​Larminie, and ​never stopped ​being retold; ​'Balor ‌of the ‌Evil Eye' ​remains the ‌standing name ​of the ​figure in ‌Irish and ‌in English.[2] ‌In the ‌modern imagination ​he has ‌become the ​Celtic ​dark lord ​par excellence: ‌the remote ​tyrant in ​his island ​fortress whose ‌one weapon ​poisons everything ​it touches, ​answered not ‌by a ​greater ‌weapon but ​by the ‌prophesied grandchild ​the fortress ‌was built ‌to prevent. ​Tory Island ​keeps his ​address, and ​the Irish ‌language never ‌lost his ​name.[3]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Balor ​is a ‌textual and ‌oral figure: ​no ​ogham stone ​names him, ​no votive ​inscription ‌invokes him, ​and Irish ​paganism ‌generally left ​literature rather ‌than dedications.[1] ​His ‌manuscript witnesses, ​however, are ​exact: Cath ​Maige ​Tuired survives ​uniquely in ‌the sixteenth-century ​British ‌Library MS ​Harley 5280, ‌the basis ​of ‌Gray's edition, ​while Lebor ‌Gabála Érenn ​is ​preserved in ‌the twelfth-century ‌Book ‌of Leinster ​and its ​sister codices.[2]

‌His ​geographical anchor ​is one ‌of the ​most ​dramatic pieces ‌of coastline ​in Europe: ‌Tory ​Island (Toraigh), ​nine miles ​off the ​Bloody ‌Foreland of ‌Donegal, whose ​cliffs and ‌sea-stacks ​— above ​all the ‌great ‌rock called ‌Tór Mór ‌— the ​oral ‌tradition names ​as Balor's ​fortress and ​the ‌site of ​Eithne's tower. ​The nineteenth-century ​collectors ‌who wrote ​the tower ​story down ​were ‌recording a ​living Donegal ‌tradition anchored ​to ‌those rocks, ​and the ‌island's ‌medieval monastic ​remains and ​round tower ​have ​kept the ‌name in ‌continuous local ​memory.[3]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Balor belongs ​to the ‌Mythological Cycle, the ‌stratum of ​Irish literature that ​stages the ​Túatha Dé Danann ​as gods ​before it remembers ‌them as ​kings — and ​within that ‌cycle he is ​the hinge ‌on which its ​central war ‌turns. His spine ​is Cath ​Maige Tuired: the ​boyhood poisoning ​of the eye, ​the muster ‌of the Fomorian ​tribute-takers, and the ‌sling-stone that ​ends his reign ‌and the ​Fomorian yoke in ‌one cast.[1] ​The Lebor Gabála ‌Érenn frames ​the tale in ​the book ‌of invasions: the ‌Fomorians as ‌Ireland's recurring sea-oppressors ​since the ​age of the ‌Nemedians, Balor's ​lineage from Nét, ​and the ‌long price of ​his war ​— Cethlenn's wound ‌and the ​Dagda's death at ‌the Brug.[2]

​Around that spine ​the later ​tradition builds the ​prophecy the ‌manuscripts only glance ‌at. The ​medieval text knows ‌that Ethniu, ​Balor's daughter, bore ​Lugh to ‌Cian; the how ‌is supplied ‌by the Donegal ‌oral tradition ​— the tower ‌on Tory, ​the twelve matrons, ​the stolen ​Glas Gaibhnenn, the ‌druidess Biróg, the ​child cast ​into the sea ​and given ‌back — collected ​in the ​nineteenth century and ​anchored to ‌the island's rocks.[3] ​Medieval scribes ‌euhemerized the Fomorians ​as a ‌piratical people of ​the northern ‌isles, but the ‌tales never ​quite reduce Balor ​to a ​mortal: the eye ​keeps him ‌on the mythic ‌side of ​the ledger.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Welsh corpus ‌is silent ‌on Balor, ​and ​the silence ​deserves to ​be recorded ​rather ‌than repaired. ​Neither the ​Mabinogi nor ‌the ​wider Brythonic ‌tradition preserves ​a figure ‌derived ​from the ​same name, ​and no ​convincing ​Welsh counterpart ‌has been ​proposed for ‌the ​Fomorian king ‌— the ​Fomorian cycle ‌as ​a whole ‌is an ​Irish construction ​without ‌Brythonic echo.[1]

‌What the ‌comparison does ​show ​is instructive ‌by contrast. ​The tale-shape ​Balor ‌embodies — ​the tyrant ​who prisons ‌his ​daughter against ‌a prophecy ​of a ​grandson's ​hand and ​is killed ‌by that ‌grandson ​— is ‌one of ​the great ​migratory ‌plots, and ‌its nearest ‌famous neighbor ‌is ​not Welsh ‌but Greek: ​Acrisius, Danae, ​and ​Perseus. The ‌honest Celtic ​judgment is ​that ​Balor is ‌Irish-made: his ​name, his ​war, ​his island ‌fortress, and ​his evil ‌eye ​all belong ‌to the ​Irish north-west, ‌and ‌the parallels ​that illuminate ​him come ​from ​the Mediterranean ‌tale-bag rather ‌than from ​Wales.[2]

Sources

  1. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Balor, Fomorians.
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (Hamlyn, 1970), on the migratory tale-types of the Irish tradition.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Balor. ‌Ogham, the ​early Irish ​monumental ​script of ​roughly ​the fourth ‌to ​seventh centuries, ​survives ‌on some ​four hundred ‌stones ​bearing mostly ‌personal ​names in ​formulaic ​genitives; no ​reading ​of Balor ‌has been ​proposed ‌for any ​of ‌them, and ​none ‌would be ​expected ‌— ogham ​stones mark ​graves ‌and boundaries, ‌not ‌votive dedications.[1]

​Nor ​does the ‌continental ​epigraphy help: ​the Fomorian ‌cycle ​is an ​Irish ‌literary construction, ​and ‌no altar ​or ​dedication from ​the Celtic ​provinces ‌can be ‌connected ​with its ‌king. ​The epigraphic ​silence ‌is characteristic ‌of Irish ‌paganism, ‌whose cult ​left ‌place-names and ​literature ​rather than ​stone ‌— though ​in Balor's ​case ​the place ‌itself ​survives: the ​rocks ​of Tory ‌Island, ​and above ‌all the ​great ‌sea-stack the ​tradition ‌made his ‌fortress, ​keep a ​memory ​no stone ​inscription was ‌ever ‌cut to ​hold.[2]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Every part ​of Balor's ‌story is ‌about containment failing. ​The eye ​is contained ​— lidded, lifted ​only by ​machinery — and ‌containment is ​the whole ​catastrophe: a gaze ‌so dangerous ​it must ‌be operated by ​four other ‌men is ​no longer a ​weapon but ​an occupation, a ​king reduced ​to the ‌caretaker of his ​own poison. ‌The daughter ​is contained in ‌a tower ​against a ‌prophecy, and the ​tower is ‌the prophecy's instrument: ​no tower, ​no Cian, ‌no Lugh. Balor ‌is the ‌tradition's purest ​study of power ​that can ‌only destroy ​— he is ​given no ‌craft, no counsel, ​no feast, ​nothing but ‌the eye and ​the tax ‌it enforces.[1]

​And the answer ​the tradition ​gives him ​is exact. Not ‌a bigger ‌eye, not a ​stronger king, ‌but a ​sling-stone: small, aimed, ​and thrown ‌by the ‌one hand the ‌whole machinery ‌existed to ​forestall. The venom ‌that could ​wither an army ​is turned ​in a ‌single instant into ​the Fomorians' ​own epitaph ​— it was ‌his own ​host that ​looked into it. ​That is ‌the Irish judgment ​on the ‌evil eye, ​and on every ‌power built ​to wither ‌rather than to ‌make: it ​ends by ​looking inward. The ​grandson does ​not defeat the ‌weapon. He ‌simply turns ​it around.

Sources

  1. Gray, Elizabeth A., ed. and trans., Cath Maige Tuired, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982).
