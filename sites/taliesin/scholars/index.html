01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Taliesin (taliesin) ​— Poet, Shapeshifter ‌· Welsh bard ‌— is the ​supreme bard of ​Welsh tradition and ​the recipient of ​the fullest legendary biography ​any Celtic poet ‌ever got: twice-born ​master of the ​awen, the flowing ‌spirit of poetic ​inspiration. As the ‌boy Gwion Bach ​he stirred Ceridwen's ‌cauldron for a ​year and a ​day, caught on ​his thumb the ​three drops of ​inspiration meant for ‌her son Afagddu, and ​fled her through ‌four shapes — ​hare, fish, bird, ‌grain — before ​she swallowed him ‌as a black ​hen and bore ‌him again; cast ​into the sea ​in a skin ‌coracle, he was ‌drawn from the ‌salmon weir of ​Gwyddno Garanhir by ​the luckless Elffin, who ‌named him for ​his radiant brow.[1]

​Before the court ‌of Maelgwn Gwynedd ​the child-bard silenced ​the assembled poets ‌with a puff ​of breath, exposed ‌the false proof ​laid against Elffin's ​wife, and sang ​his foster-father out ​of thirteen locks.[1] ‌Behind the legend stands ‌a historical poet ​of sixth-century Rheged: ‌the Historia Brittonum ​(§62) names Taliesin ​among the five ‌poets famed in ‌the time of ‌Ida of Bernicia, ‌and the Book ​of Taliesin preserves ‌a core of ​praise-poetry to Urien ​of Rheged that ​modern scholarship accepts ‌as plausibly his.[2]

PuniCodex ​restores the name ​as Taliesin — ​identical to the ‌ASCII input, because ​medieval Welsh writes ​the name in ​plain Latin letters. ‌The mechanical rule ​therefore places it ‌in Tier 2: ​nothing distinctive is ‌lost in transit, ​a statement about ‌orthography and never ‌about the bard. No ​domain is claimed: ​the plain-ASCII taliesin.com ​is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Middle Welsh as ‌Taliesin, unchanged into ​the modern language ​— the spelling ​of the Book ​of Taliesin and ​of the Historia ‌Brittonum's ninth-century notice ​alike.[1] The tradition's ​own etymology is ‌staged inside the ​legend: when Elffin ‌opens the coracle ​and sees the ‌child's forehead shining ​from the folds ​of the skin, ​he cries 'behold ​a radiant brow' ​— tal iesin ‌— and names ​him for it.[2]

‌The medieval gloss ​survives scholarly scrutiny. ‌Welsh tal means ​'forehead, brow', and ‌the second element ​iesin is related ‌to a root ​meaning 'radiant, shining'; ​Ifor Williams discussed ‌the compound as ‌the traditional analysis, ‌and the etymological ​dictionaries still print ​essentially that reading: ‌'radiant brow'.[3] His ​standing style in ​the Welsh Triads ‌is Taliesin Ben ​Beirdd, 'Taliesin Chief ​of Bards', head ‌of the Primary Chief ​Bards of the ‌Isle of Britain.[4]

​The ASCII form ​taliesin loses nothing, ​because the medieval ​Welsh spelling is ‌already plain Latin ‌letters; the restoration ​is therefore a ‌matter of canonical ​reference, not rescued ​diacritics, and the ‌name is classified ‌Tier 2 by ‌the project's mechanical ‌rule.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • ‌t → T ​— Same, capitalized

​- a → ​a — Same
‌- l → ​l — Same
​- i → ​i — Same
‌- e → ​e — Same
​- s → ​s — Same
‌- i → ​i — Same
‌- n → ​n — Same

‌The project holds ​no domain for ‌this name: taliesin.com ‌is registered to ​a third party ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The pronunciation ​of the ‌name is /talˈjɛsɪn/ ‌— Modern Welsh.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • Ta- ​— Voiceless ​alveolar stop [t], ​plain and unaspirated ‌as Welsh ​stops are, and ​short open [a]; ‌the first ​syllable is light

‌- -l- ​— Voiced alveolar ‌lateral [l]: the ​ordinary Welsh ​l, not the ​voiceless fricative ​ll [ɬ] — ​Taliesin keeps the ‌soft single ​lateral

  • -ies- ‌— The stressed ​penult [jɛs]: ‌a palatal glide ​into open-mid ‌[ɛ], sounding exactly ​like English 'yes'; ‌Welsh accent ​falls on the ​penultimate syllable

‌- -in — ‌Short close [ɪ] ‌and a ​final alveolar nasal ​[n], lightly sounded; ‌Welsh final ​syllables are never ​swallowed

For ‌the modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ​is: 'tal-YES-in' ‌— three syllables, ​the weight ‌squarely on the ​middle one, the ​first a ​soft tap.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of the ‌name:

  • Middle ​Welsh — ‌Taliesin, the spelling ​of the Book ​of Taliesin ‌(NLW Peniarth MS ‌2), unchanged ‌into modern Welsh

‌- Historia Brittonum ​— Taliesin, ‌among the five ​poets famed ​in the time ​of Ida of ‌Bernicia, the ​name's oldest surviving ​notice

  • Welsh ​Triads — ‌Taliesin Ben Beirdd, ​'Taliesin Chief ​of Bards'
  • ​Etymology — tal, ‌'forehead, brow' ​+ iesin, 'radiant, ‌shining': 'radiant ​brow'[2]

Taliesin is ‌Tier 2 because ​the restoration ‌preserves nothing ASCII ‌loses: the letters ​of the ​manuscript form are ​the letters ​of the keyboard. ‌The capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Sources

  1. Jackson, Kenneth, Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on the Welsh sound-system and stress.
  2. Williams, Ifor, The Poems of Taliesin, trans. J. E. Caerwyn Williams (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1968).

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌medieval Welsh ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet as ​Taliesin — Middle ​Welsh in ​Insular script, with ​the poetry attested ‌in the early ​fourteenth-century Book ​of Taliesin (NLW ‌Peniarth MS 2) ​and the ‌historical notice in ​the ninth-century recensions ‌of the Historia ​Brittonum.[1] The ​full legendary biography ​survives in the ​sixteenth-century chronicle of ​Elis Gruffydd; ‌the manuscript forms ​are the primary ‌witnesses, and they ​agree on ‌the spelling.[2]

The ​scholarly rendering proceeds ‌step by step:

​- Medieval ‌Welsh was written ​in the Latin ​alphabet using Insular ‌minuscule; the ‌spelling Taliesin requires ‌no diacritic.

  • ​The tradition's ​own etymology is ‌staged in the ​legend itself — ​Elffin's cry ‌at the weir, ​'behold a radiant ​brow', tal iesin ‌— and ​the modern dictionaries ‌accept essentially the ​same compound: tal, ​'forehead, brow', ​plus iesin, 'radiant, ​shining'.[3]
  • No ‌earlier alphabet enters ‌the record: ​ogham in Wales ‌belongs to the ​Irish settlers ​of Dyfed and ‌never carries this ‌name; the manuscript ‌form is ‌the sole witness.[4]

​- The Unicode ‌restoration Taliesin is ​therefore identical ​to the ASCII ​form apart from ‌the capital; the ​temple form ​is a canonical ​reference, not a ‌rescued spelling.

One ​caution attaches ​to the record: ​the legend's persona-poems ‌in the Book ​of Taliesin ‌are medieval compositions ​under the great ‌name, not ​the sixth-century poet's ‌own work — ‌Ifor Williams's separation ​of the ​Urien corpus from ​the legendary Book ​remains the standing ‌framework, and ‌the one this ​edition follows.[5]

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Taliesin ​is catalogued ‌under the ‌domain 'Poet, ​Shapeshifter' — ​two words ​that are ​the two ​halves of ‌the Hanes ​Taliesin itself.[1]

​### Poet

‌Chief of ​Bards: the ‌infant who ​answers his ‌naming with ​the Consolation ​of Elffin; ​the child ​who drones ​down Maelgwn's ‌court poets ​with 'blerwm ‌blerwm' and ​then sings; ‌the adult ​voice of ‌the Urien ​praise-poems, of ‌Cad Goddeu, ​and of ​Preiddeu Annwfn. ‌Poetry in ‌this dossier ‌is not ​an ornament ​but the ‌central power ​— it ​consoles, accuses, ‌vindicates, and ​opens locks.[1]

​### Shapeshifter

‌Hare, fish, ​bird, grain: ‌the four ​shapes of ​the flight ​before Ceridwen, ​and then ‌the fifth ‌and strangest ​shape of ‌all — ​the child ​in the ‌coracle, reborn. ‌The legendary ‌poems extend ‌the grammar ​indefinitely: 'I ‌have been ​in many ​shapes before ​I attained ‌a congenial ​form' — ​sword, rain, ​harp-string, eagle, ‌word.[2]

​Chief of ​Bards

The ​Welsh Triads ‌give him ​the title ‌outright — ​Taliesin Ben ‌Beirdd — ​and rank ‌him at ‌the head ​of the ​Primary Chief ​Bards of ​the Isle ‌of Britain: ‌the tradition's ​own domain-statement.[3]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Taliesin's ​attributes are ‌one vessel, ‌one accident, ​and ​a sequence ​of shapes ​— the ​most ‌kinetic regalia ​in the ​Welsh corpus:[1]

‌- The ​cauldron ‌of inspiration ​— The ‌vessel Ceridwen ​set ​to boil ​for a ​year and ​a ‌day beside ​Lake Tegid: ‌the awen ​made ‌material, the ​source from ‌which all ​poetry ‌in the ​legend flows
​- The ‌three drops ‌— ‌The sum ​of the ​cauldron's virtue, ‌caught ​on a ​boy's scalded ‌thumb: inspiration ​as ​a finite, ‌transferable grace
​- The ‌black ​hen — ​Ceridwen's final ​shape in ​the ‌chase: the ‌goddess as ​the mother ‌who ​must swallow ​to give ‌birth

  • ‌The coracle ‌in ‌the weir ​— The ‌skin boat ​Elffin ​found among ​the salmon ‌of Gwyddno's ​weir: ​the poet's ​second arrival, ‌from the ​water ​like every ​gift of ‌Annwn
  • ​The ‌silenced bards ​— Maelgwn's ‌court poets ​reduced to ‌droning ‌'blerwm blerwm' ​by a ​child's breath: ​the ​power of ‌true song ‌over mere ​office[2]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Taliesin's mythology divides into ​two figures the Welsh tradition never quite separated: ‌the historical poet of sixth-century Rheged, and ‌the wonder-child of the cauldron whose story is ​medieval in conception but survives whole only ​in sixteenth-century manuscripts.

The Poet of Urien ​(Historia Brittonum §62; Book of Taliesin)

The ​Historia Brittonum (c. 829) names five poets famed ​in the days of Ida of Bernicia, ‌in the mid-sixth century: Talhaearn Tad Awen, Aneirin, ​Cian, Blwchfardd — 'and Taliesin, who is ​renowned in British poetry'.[1] The Book of Taliesin ‌preserves the substance behind the notice: a ​core of praise-poems to Urien of Rheged and ‌his son Owain, court verse of the ​Old North singing the wars of Rheged against ‌the Bernician Angles. Ifor Williams's scholarship established ​the canon: a dozen poems of the Book ​stand as plausibly the work of a ​real Taliesin, court bard of Urien in the ​generation before Rheged fell.[2] The legend did ​not invent him; it annexed him.

The ‌Cauldron and the Chase (Hanes Taliesin)

Ceridwen, dwelling ​by Lake Tegid, had two children: Creirwy ‌the fairest of maidens and Afagddu, called Morfran, ​the most ill-favored of men. To compensate ‌her dark son she set a cauldron of ​inspiration and science to boil for a ‌year and a day, put blind Morda to ​the fire, and set the boy Gwion ‌Bach to stir. On the last day three ​drops flew onto Gwion's thumb; scalded, he ​put it in his mouth, and at once ‌knew all things past and to come; ‌the cauldron split, and all its virtue was ‌spent on the wrong child.[3]

What follows ​is the great transformation chase of Celtic literature: ​Gwion as a hare, Ceridwen as a ‌greyhound; he a fish in the river, she ​an otter; he a bird, she a ​hawk; at last he a single grain on ‌the winnowing floor, and she a black ​hen who swallowed him. Nine months she bore ​him, could not kill so fair a ‌child when he was born, and cast him ​into the sea in a skin coracle ‌on the eve of May Day.[3]

The ​Weir and the Court of Maelgwn (Hanes ​Taliesin)

The coracle drifted into the salmon weir ​of Gwyddno Garanhir at the mouth of ​the Dyfi, where Gwyddno's luckless son Elffin drew ‌it from the water, saw the child's ‌forehead shining from the folds, and cried 'behold ​a radiant brow!' — and so named ‌him. The infant answered with his first poem, ​the Consolation of Elffin, and from that ​day Elffin's luck turned.[3]

Years later Elffin boasted ‌at Maelgwn Gwynedd's court that he had ‌a wife more virtuous and a bard more ‌skilled than any there; Maelgwn cast him ‌into prison under thirteen locks and sent Rhun ​to test the wife, and Rhun returned ‌with a drugged servant-maid's finger and ring as ​false proof of her fall. Taliesin came ​to court, put a finger to his lips ​and blew 'blerwm blerwm' until all the ‌king's bards could do the same and no ​more, save Heinin their chief; then the child ​stood and sang, summoned a wind that ​shook the court, exposed the false finger, vindicated ‌Elffin's wife, and sang the thirteen locks ​open.[3]

I Have Been in Many Shapes ​(Cad Goddeu; Preiddeu Annwfn)

The legendary poems ​of the Book give Taliesin the widest voice ‌in Celtic literature. In Cad Goddeu, the ​Battle of the Trees, he boasts of a ‌hundred existences — sword in hand, rain ​in the sky, string of a harp, word ‌among letters, eagle on the tide — ​while Gwydion enchants the trees of the wood ‌to march for the sons of Dôn.[4] ‌In Preiddeu Annwfn, the Spoils of Annwn, he ​is the one survivor who can tell ​of Arthur's raid on the Otherworld for the ​cauldron of the Head of Annwn — ​kindled by the breath of nine maidens, rimmed ‌with pearls, that will not boil a ‌coward's food; three full shiploads went in, and ​save seven, none returned.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Taliesin's ​closest kin ‌is Irish. ‌The Song ​of Amairgen ​— 'I ​am the ​wind on ​the sea, ‌I am ​the wave ​of the ‌sea, I ​am the ‌stag of ​seven tines' ‌— speaks ​in the ​same grammar ​of self-predication ​as Cad ​Goddeu, and ‌Sjoestedt set ​the two ‌side by ​side as ‌the Celtic ​poet's native ‌form: the ​boast ‌that is ​a cosmology, ​the claim ‌that the ‌inspired singer ‌has been ​all things ​and so ‌may speak ​of all.[1] ​The awen ‌itself has ​its Irish ​counterpart in ‌imbas, the ​'great knowledge' ‌of the ​filid, and ​the transformation ​chase of ​the Hanes ‌belongs to ‌the international ​tale-type of ‌the flight ​in shifting ​shapes.[2]

Within ‌Wales, Taliesin ‌was paired ‌from ‌early on ​with Myrddin: ‌the Black ​Book of ​Carmarthen's Ymddiddan ​Myrddin a ‌Thaliesin stages ​the two ​prophets in ​dialogue over ‌the battle ​of Arfderydd ​— the ​two great ‌names of ​Welsh poetry ‌reading each ​other's grief.[3] ‌The Christian ​scribes needed ‌no euhemerism ‌for him: ​a poet, ​however marvelous, ​was safe ​to keep, ‌and they ‌kept him ​whole.

Sources

  2. MacKillop, James, A Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1998), s.v. Taliesin, awen, imbas.
  3. Jarman, A. O. H., ed. and trans., Ymddiddan Myrddin a Thaliesin (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1951).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Taliesin ​entered the ‌modern world ‌with Lady ​Charlotte Guest's ​Mabinogion (1838–49), ​which ​first printed ​the Hanes ‌Taliesin from ​Elis Gruffydd's ​sixteenth-century chronicle ‌and made ​the ‌cauldron story ​a classic ‌far beyond ​Wales.[1] Twentieth-century ​scholarship restored ​the ​historical poet: ​Ifor Williams's ‌Canu Taliesin ​(1960) separated ‌the Urien ​corpus ‌from the ​legendary Book ‌and set ​the standard ‌for all ​later ​work.[2]

Robert ‌Graves's The ‌White Goddess ‌(1948) built ​a famous ​and much-contested ‌mythology ​on Cad ​Goddeu — ‌scholars reject ​his tree-alphabet, ​but he ‌carried ​the poem ‌to a ​vast new ​readership. Modern ​Druidry took ​the ‌awen for ‌its own: ​the three ‌rays are ​its emblem ​and the ‌word ‌its name ‌for inspiration. ‌The name ​itself passed ‌into architecture ​— ​Frank Lloyd ​Wright named ‌his Wisconsin ​home and ​school Taliesin ​— ‌and in ​Wales it ​never needed ​reviving: Taliesin ‌remains a ​living ‌given name, ​and Swansea's ‌arts centre ​bears it.[3] ‌Few figures ‌in the ​lexicon ​bridge a ​sixth-century court ​and a ‌modern concert ‌hall so ​completely.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Taliesin ​is a ‌wholly textual ‌figure: ​no inscription ​names ​him, no ​ogham stone ​or ‌cross-slab invokes ​him, and ​the ‌Welsh epigraphic ​record ‌— Romano-British ​memorial stones ‌and ​the ogham ​stones of ​the ​Irish settlers ​in ‌Dyfed — ​belongs to ‌other ​names entirely.[1] ‌His witnesses ​are ‌manuscripts of ​unusually ‌late but ​exact pedigree.

​The ‌Book of ‌Taliesin (NLW ‌Peniarth ​MS 2, ​early ‌fourteenth century) ​preserves both ​the ‌sixth-century praise-poems ​to Urien ​of ‌Rheged and ​the ‌legendary cycle ​in a ​single ​hand.[2] The ​Black Book ‌of ‌Carmarthen (mid-thirteenth ​century) ‌holds the ​Ymddiddan Myrddin ​a ‌Thaliesin.[3] The ‌Historia Brittonum's ‌notice ‌travels in ​its ‌ninth-century recensions, ​Harley MS ​3859 ​chief among ‌them.[4] And ​the ​full legendary ​biography, ‌the Hanes ​Taliesin, survives ​entire ​only in ‌the sixteenth-century ​chronicle ‌of Elis ​Gruffydd ‌— a ​late witness ‌to ‌a story ​whose own ​materials, ​the Triads ​and ‌the Book's ‌persona-poems, prove ​medieval.[5]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Taliesin ​belongs to ‌no Irish ‌cycle, and ​the absence ​deserves to ​be recorded ​rather ​than repaired: ‌neither the ​Mythological nor ​the Ulster, ‌Fenian, or ​Historical cycles ‌preserve a ​figure ‌derived from ​his name, ​and the ​modern handbooks ​know no ​Irish attestation.[1]

‌What ​the Irish ‌comparison does ​supply is ‌the tradition's ​closest structural ‌kin. The ​Song ‌of Amairgen ​— 'I ​am the ‌wind on ‌the sea, ‌I am ​the wave ​of ‌the sea, ​I am ​the stag ‌of seven ​tines' — ​speaks in ‌the ​same grammar ‌of self-predication ​as Cad ​Goddeu, and ​Sjoestedt set ​the two ‌side ‌by side ​as the ‌Celtic poet's ​native form: ​the boast ‌that is ‌a cosmology. ‌The ‌awen likewise ​has its ‌Irish counterpart ​in imbas, ​the 'great ​knowledge' of ‌the ​filid, and ​the cauldron ​of inspiration ‌has its ​analogues in ​the Irish ​vessels ‌of plenty ​and prophecy.[2] ‌These are ​parallels of ‌structure, not ​borrowings: two ‌literatures giving ‌the ​inspired poet ​the same ​native grammar ​on opposite ‌sides of ‌the Irish ​Sea.

  1. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia, 5 vols. (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Taliesin.
  2. Sjoestedt, Marie-Louise, Gods and Heroes of the Celts, trans. Myles Dillon (London: Methuen, 1949); MacKillop, James, A Dictionary of Celtic Mythology (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1998), s.v. awen, imbas.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Taliesin is ​a Welsh ‌figure to ‌the core, ​and his ​dossier is the ​fullest of ​any Welsh ​poet. The ‌narrative spine ​is the Hanes ​Taliesin: the ‌cauldron beside ​Lake Tegid, ‌the three ​drops and ‌the transformation chase, ​the coracle ​in the ​weir of ​Gwyddno Garanhir, ​and the contest ‌at the ​court of ‌Maelgwn Gwynedd ​— preserved ‌entire in the ​sixteenth-century chronicle ‌of Elis ​Gruffydd and ‌first printed ​by Lady Charlotte ​Guest.[1]

Around ‌that spine ‌the older ‌witnesses build ​the poet. The ​Historia Brittonum ‌(§62) names ​him among ​the five ‌famed poets of ​Ida of ​Bernicia's day;[2] ‌the Book ​of Taliesin ‌preserves both ​the Urien praise-poems ​scholarship accepts ​as plausibly ​historical and ‌the legendary ‌cycle — Cad ​Goddeu, Preiddeu ‌Annwfn, and ​the rest ​of the ‌persona-poems;[3] the Welsh ‌Triads style ‌him Ben ‌Beirdd, Chief ​of Bards, ‌at the head ​of the ​Primary Chief ​Bards of ‌the Isle ​of Britain;[4] and ​the Black ​Book of ‌Carmarthen's Ymddiddan ​Myrddin a ​Thaliesin stages him ​in prophetic ‌dialogue with ​Myrddin over ‌the battle ​of Arfderydd.[5] ‌Between the ninth-century ​notice and ‌the sixteenth-century ‌chronicle stands ​the whole ​Welsh transmission: a ​poet who ​is historical, ‌legendary, and ‌manuscript all ​at once.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Taliesin. The ‌Welsh ​epigraphic record ​of the ​early ​centuries — ​the Romano-British ‌memorial ​stones catalogued ​by Nash-Williams, ‌bearing personal ​names ‌in Latin ​and formulaic ‌genitives ​— belongs ​to the ​dead ​of the ​kingdoms, not ‌to ​its poets, ‌and no ​reading ‌of Taliesin ​has been ‌proposed ​for any ‌of them.[1]

​Ogham, ​the early ‌Irish monumental ‌script, survives ‌on ​some thirty ​stones in ‌Wales, ​mostly in ​Dyfed, where ‌it ​records the ​names of ‌Irish ​settlers alongside ‌their Latin ​equivalents; ​none names ​Taliesin, and ​none ‌would be ‌expected — ​ogham ‌stones mark ​graves and ​boundaries, not ‌bardic ‌fame.[2] The ‌epigraphic silence ‌is ​characteristic of ‌the whole ​Welsh ​poetic tradition, ​whose transmission ‌ran ​through memory ​and manuscript ​rather ‌than stone: ​the earliest ​physical ​witness to ‌the name ​is ‌not an ​inscription but ‌the ninth-century ​recensions ‌of the ‌Historia Brittonum, ​and ​the fullest ​is the ​early ‌fourteenth-century Book ‌of Taliesin ​itself.[3]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Taliesin ​is the ‌one figure ‌in the ​Celtic dossier ​whose myth ​is a ​complete ​theory of ‌art. Inspiration ​exists: it ​is real, ‌finite, and ​brewable — ‌a cauldron's ​worth, ‌a year ​and a ​day in ​the making. ​It is ​unjust: meant ‌for ​the ill-favored ‌son of ​a goddess, ‌it lands ​on the ‌thumb of ​a ‌hired boy, ​and no ​pursuit, however ‌divine, can ‌win it ‌back. And ​it is ​total: ‌once swallowed, ​it remakes ​both the ‌swallower and ​the swallowed, ​and what ‌is ​reborn is ‌not a ​boy with ​a gift ​but a ​poet who ‌has ‌been everything ​— sword ‌and rain, ​eagle and ​grain — ‌because poetry ‌is exactly ‌the ‌faculty of ​having been ‌everything.[1]

The ​name keeps ​the whole ​doctrine in ‌two ​words: radiant ​brow, the ​visible mark ‌where the ​three drops ​passed through. ​Taliesin ‌asks the ​question every ‌bard answers ​nightly: not ‌whether the ​gift is ‌deserved, but ‌what ​it costs ​to carry ​a light ​on the ‌forehead that ‌anyone can ​see.

Sources

  1. Ford, Patrick K., ed. and trans., Ystoria Taliesin (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
