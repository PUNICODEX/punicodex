The provenance file on Ninlil

Catalogue entry — Ninlil. Every claim below carries its chain of custody.

Provenance

1. The attestation. 𒀭𒊩𒌆𒆤, from the tradition's own record. On record: ETCSL, Black & Green, Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia, Jacobsen, The Treasures of Darkness, Kramer, Sumerian Literary Texts from Nippur.

2. The narrative record. Enlil and Ninlil — The Sumerian poem Enlil and Ninlil (ETCSL 1. 2. Mother of the Moon — Ninlil is the mother of Nanna/Sîn, the moon god, and in some traditions also of Inanna/Ištar and Utu/Shamash. The moon's regular cycle becomes an extension of the air-queen's generative power: what moves unseen by day shines by night.

3. The physical record. Cuneiform tablets from Nippur, Ur, and Assur attest Ninlil's cult from the Early Dynastic period into the first millennium BCE. The Ekur sanctuary at Nippur, excavated by the University of Pennsylvania and Iraqi teams, preserves the architectural setting of her worship alongside Enlil. Assyrian royal inscriptions from Nineveh and Assur name Mullissu/Ninlil as the divine consort who legitimises the king, while cylinder seals and votive objects depict her wearing the horned crown of divine queenship.

4. The current holding. The temple at /ninlil/, classified Tier 2. Tier-2 here means one preserved truth — not less important, just singular. The temple shows it and names it.

5. The documented resonances. Wind & Atmospheric Energy, Agriculture, Food & Harvest, Environmental & Climate Services — seats argued and weighted in the pattern atlas, read through the myths in this temple's Resonance File.

6. The primary sources. Enūma Eliš, The Epic of Gilgamesh — the library this file answers to, open at the Sacred Texts collection.

7. The continuing record. Ninlil survives in Assyriological scholarship and in the modern recovery of Sumerian religion through ETCSL and the great lexical projects of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. She is less famous in popular culture than her daughter Ištar or her consort Enlil, yet her role as wind-queen and mother of lunar and netherworld powers makes her essential to any coherent picture of the Mesopotamian pantheon.
