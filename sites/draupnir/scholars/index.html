01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Draupnir (draupnir) ​— Odin's Ring, ‌Wealth · ‌The Dripper — ​is the golden ​arm-ring of Óðinn ​and the ​one treasure in ​the northern corpus ‌whose nature is ​increase: every ​ninth night, eight ‌rings of equal ​weight drip ‌from it.[1] Forged ​by the dwarf ‌Sindri while his ​brother Brokkr ​worked the bellows, ​it came out ​of the same ​wager that ‌produced the boar ​Gullinbursti and Þórr's ‌hammer Mjǫllnir, and ​was judged ‌beside them at ​Ásgarðr.[1]

Its career ‌in the corpus ​is short ‌and perfectly shaped. ​Skáldskaparmál tells its ​making; Skírnismál catches ‌it in ‌motion, offered as ‌bride-price to the ​giantess Gerðr ​and refused; and ‌Gylfaginning 49 gives ​it the two ​gestures that ‌define it — ​Óðinn laying it ​on Baldr's pyre, ‌and Baldr ​sending it back ‌from Hel with ​Hermóðr as a ​keepsake for ​his father.[2][3] Völuspá's ​catalogue of dwarfs ‌preserves the name's ‌older root: ​a dwarf Draupnir ‌stands in the ​dvergatal, the ​treasure wearing a ‌name the smith-lists ‌already owned.[4]

PuniCodex ‌restores the ‌name as Draupnir ​— identical to ‌the ASCII input, ​because the ​normalized Old Norse ​form is itself ‌plain ASCII: no ​diacritic, no ​distinctive letter, nothing ​for the ASCII ‌alphabet to lose. ​The name ​is therefore Tier ​2 by the ‌project's mechanical rule ​— a ‌property of its ​orthography, not of ‌the treasure's ​standing. The plain-ASCII ‌domain draupnir.com is ‌registered to a ​third party ​outside the collection ​and is watched ​for release; the ‌project claims ‌no domain for ​this temple.[5]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Old ‌Norse as Draupnir ‌(masculine; genitive Draupnis). It ​is a transparent ​deverbal formation: the verb ​drjúpa 'to drip' ​plus the agent and ​instrument suffix -nir ‌— the same ending ​that makes Mjǫllnir ​'the grinder' from mala/mylja ‌— yielding 'the ​dripper'.[1] The name states ‌the ring's single ​miraculous property as plainly ‌as a label: ​it is called after ​what it does, ​which is to drip ​gold.

The Poetic ​Edda knows the same ‌word as a ​dwarf-name: in Völuspá's ‌dvergatal, Draupnir stands in ​the catalogue among ‌the earth-smiths.[2] The double ​life of the ‌word — dwarf in ​the oldest poem, ‌treasure in the prose ​tradition — is ​typical of the corpus's ‌wonder-names, and scholars ‌have long read the ‌ring's name as ​a dwarf's name transferred ​to a dwarf's ‌work.

The ASCII form ​is not a ​compromise here: normalized Old ‌Norse Draupnir is ​already written in plain ​ASCII letters. There ‌is no diacritic, no ​thorn or eth, ‌no vowel-length mark for ​the early domain-name ​system to have stripped; ​the diphthong au ​is written with two ‌ordinary letters. The ‌name is classified Tier ​2 by the ‌project's mechanical rule — ​the restoration preserves ​nothing the ASCII form ‌loses — which ‌is a statement about ‌orthography, never about ‌the treasure. The final ​-r belongs to ‌the nominative ending fused ​into the -nir ​suffix.

The letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • d ‌→ D — ​Same, capitalized
  • r ​→ r — ​Same
  • a → ‌a — Same

​- u → u ​— Same

  • ​p → p — ‌Same
  • n ​→ n — Same

‌- i → ​i — Same

  • ‌r → r ​— Same

The project ‌holds no domain ‌for this name: draupnir.com ​is registered to ​a third party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is watched for ‌release. The temple ‌lives at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈdrɑup.nir/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Dr- ​— Fully ‌voiced dental ​stop [d] ​releasing directly ‌into a ​trilled [r]; the ‌cluster is ​voiced throughout, ‌with no ​aspiration after ​the d
​- -au- — ​The falling ​diphthong [ɑu], ‌open back ​vowel gliding ‌to [u]; ​the sound Old ‌Norse wrote ​au, cousin ‌to the ​'ow' of ‌English 'cow'
​- -pnir — ​Voiceless bilabial ‌[p], alveolar ‌nasal [n], ‌short close ​[i], and ​the final ‌trilled [r] of ​the agent ​suffix -nir

‌For the ​modern speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation is: 'DROYP-nir' ​— two ‌syllables, stress ​on the ​first; 'droyp' ​rhyming with ​'cowp', the r's ‌lightly trilled.

‌Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • ​Old Norse ‌— Draupnir, Óðinn's ‌ring; from ‌drjúpa 'to ‌drip' + ​-nir; also ‌a dwarf-name ​in Völuspá
  • ​Younger Futhark ​— ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᛦ, ‌the Viking-Age ​runic rendering ​(t-r-au-b-n-i-R)
  • ​Modern Icelandic — ‌Draupnir, the ​unbroken living ​form

Draupnir ​is Tier ‌2 because ​the Old Norse ‌form is ​already plain ‌ASCII: nothing ​distinctive is ‌lost in ‌transit. The ​diphthong au and ​the suffix ​-nir are ​written with ‌the plainest ‌letters the ​language owns.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Draupnir — ​Old Norse ​(North Germanic), attested ​13th c. ‌CE in the ​Codex Regius of ​the Prose ‌Edda (Skáldskaparmál, Gylfaginning) ​and the ‌Codex Regius of ​the Poetic ‌Edda (Skírnismál, Völuspá), ​recording material centuries ​older.[1] The ​manuscript form is ​itself plain ​ASCII: Old Norse ‌writes no diacritic ​in this ‌name, and the ​restoration changes ‌nothing but the ​capital. In ‌the Younger Futhark ​the name would ‌have been ​carved ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᛦ (t-r-au-b-n-i-R); ​the runic ‌row underspecifies what ‌the manuscripts ‌fix, with ᛏ ​writing both t ​and d, ‌ᛒ writing both ​b and ​p, and ᛦ ‌the final nominative ​-r.[2]

The ​scholarly rendering proceeds ‌step by ​step:

  • The ‌word is ​a deverbal masculine: ​drjúpa 'to drip' ​+ the ​agent suffix -nir, ‌hence 'the ‌dripper'.
  • The ​diphthong au is ‌written with ​two plain letters; ​Old Norse ‌orthography requires no ‌accent anywhere ‌in the word.

‌- The name ​also occurs ‌as a dwarf-name ​in Völuspá's ​dvergatal; the treasure ​and the ‌smith share one ​word.[3]

  • The ​Unicode restoration ​Draupnir is therefore ‌identical to ​the ASCII form ​— a Tier ​2 name ‌by the mechanical ​rule, whose ‌dignity comes from ​the treasure, ‌not from rescued ​diacritics.

PuniCodex holds ‌no domain ‌for this name: ​the plain-ASCII ​draupnir.com is registered ​to a ​third party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.

  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. draupnir, drjúpa.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Draupnir ​is catalogued ‌under Odin's ‌Ring, Wealth ​— and ​the sources ​earn both ​halves of ​the phrase.[1]

‌### Odin's ​Ring

The ​ring is ‌Óðinn's own, ​one of ‌his two ​great treasures ‌from the ​forging wager, ​beside the ​spear Gungnir. ​It moves ​only at ‌his giving: ​onto Baldr's ‌pyre, and ​— in ‌Skírnismál — ​into the ‌wooing-gifts carried ​for Freyr's ‌suit.

​Wealth

Its ​single property ‌is increase: ‌eight rings ‌of equal ​weight every ​ninth night. ‌Snorri states ​it in ​one sentence ‌and the ​whole tradition ​repeats it ‌— ​the ring ‌is wealth's ​self-portrait, gold ​that breeds ​gold without ​labour or ‌taking.

‌The Gift

​Every appearance ‌of the ​ring in ​the corpus ‌is a ‌gift: judged ‌and given ‌at Ásgarðr, ​offered as ‌bride-price, laid ​on the ​pyre as ​a father's ‌last giving, ​returned from ​Hel as ​a keepsake. ‌It is ​never bought, ​sold, won, ​or stolen.

‌### The ​Number Nine

‌The multiplication ​runs on ‌the corpus's ​sacred interval ‌— nine ‌nights, the ​same rhythm ​as Óðinn's ​hanging on ​the world-tree ‌and Hermóðr's ‌ride to ​Hel.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál and Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Draupnir ​is itself ‌the symbol ‌— a ​treasure ​whose whole ​meaning is ​its working ​— ‌but the ​tradition has ​fixed a ‌small constellation ​of ‌attributes around ​it:[1]

  • ‌The eight ​rings ​— The ​dripping yield ​of every ​ninth ‌night: increase ​as a ‌calendar, wealth ​arriving ‌on a ​schedule rather ‌than by ​conquest

‌- Sindri's ​forge and ​Brokkr's bellows ‌— The ‌making ‌under pressure: ​the unbroken ​blast that ‌produced ​the ring ​while Loki, ‌in a ​fly's ​shape, spent ‌his malice ​on the ‌bellows-hand
​- The ​fly — ​The sting ​that ‌cost Mjǫllnir ‌its handle ​but never ‌touched ​the ring: ​the proof ‌that not ‌all sabotage ‌succeeds
‌- The ​pyre — ‌Óðinn's last ​gift ​laid in ​the fire ‌with Baldr: ​the ​one inexhaustible ​treasure deliberately ‌given away
​- ​The return ​token — ‌The ring ​in ‌Hermóðr's hand ​on the ‌road back ​from Hel: ‌the ‌only object ​in the ​corpus to ​cross ​the boundary ‌of death ‌in both ​directions[2]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Draupnir's mythology divides ​into its making and its journey, ‌with the Poetic Edda catching ‌it twice between.

The Wager ​of Loki's Head (Skáldskaparmál)

It ​begins with Loki's crime against Sif, ​whose hair he cuts off ​for mischief, and his oath to ​replace it with gold. From ‌the sons of Ivaldi he gets ​the golden hair, the ship ​Skíðblaðnir, and the spear Gungnir — ‌and then, boasting, he wagers his ​own head with the dwarf ‌Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri cannot ​make three treasures as good. ‌The bet frames the whole episode: ​everything that follows is forge-work ​under a death-wager.[1]

Three Treasures ​from the Bellows (Skáldskaparmál)

Sindri ​lays pigskin, then gold, then iron ​in the forge, and Brokkr ‌blows without ceasing while a fly ​— Loki — stings his ‌hand, his neck, and at last ​his eyelid until blood blinds ‌him and the bellows falter for ​one stroke. Out come the ‌golden boar Gullinbursti, the ring Draupnir, ​and the short-handled hammer Mjǫllnir. ‌Judged at Ásgarðr against Loki's three, ​the treasures are dealt out: ​Gungnir and Draupnir to Óðinn — ‌and Snorri fixes the ring's nature ‌in the sentence the whole ‌tradition repeats: every ninth night, eight ​rings of equal weight drip ​from it. Mjǫllnir is judged the ‌best for the defence of ​the gods; Loki keeps his head ​on the point of law ‌that Brokkr may not take the ​neck, and has his lips ​sewn shut instead.[1]

The Ring ‌Refused (Skírnismál 21–22)

When Freyr's ​servant Skírnir woos the giantess Gerðr ‌for his master, his second ​gift is 'the ring that was ​burned with Óðinn's young son; ​eight of equal weight drip from ​it every ninth night' — ‌unnamed, unmistakably Draupnir. Gerðr refuses: she ‌lacks no gold in Gymir's ​halls. The self-breeding treasure of the ‌Allfather, the one offer that ​cannot be outbid, is weighed by ​a giantess and found worthless — ‌the poem's verdict that gold, ‌even multiplying gold, buys nothing that ‌matters. Only Skírnir's rune-carved curse ‌moves her.[2]

The Pyre and ​the Return (Gylfaginning 49)

At ‌Baldr's funeral on Hringhorni, Óðinn lays ​Draupnir on the pyre with ​his dead son — the fountain ​of his gold given away ‌in the one gesture that makes ​a treasure a gift. Then ​Hermóðr rides the nine nights to ​Hel, wins the famous condition, ‌and brings the tokens back: first ​among them Draupnir, sent by ​Baldr to Óðinn as a keepsake. ​The ransom fails at Þökk's ‌refusal and Baldr remains with the ​dead; but the ring completes ‌the journey no other object makes, ​out of the fire, into Hel, ‌and home again.[3]

The ​Dwarf's Name (Völuspá)

In Völuspá's dvergatal, ‌the great catalogue of the ‌earth-smiths, a dwarf named Draupnir stands ​in the list. The treasure-name ​and the smith-name are one word ​— 'the dripper' — and ​the corpus seems to keep, fossilized ‌in its oldest poem, the ‌truth that such wonders come from ​under the earth.[4]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the wager, the forging, the judgment), trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Skírnismál 21–22 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  4. Völuspá, dvergatal (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Draupnir's ​true company ‌is objects, ‌not gods, ​and the ​corpus itself ​supplies the ​sharpest contrast: ​Andvari's ring ‌Andvaranaut, extorted ​at the ​waterfall and ‌answered with ​a curse, ‌kills every ​hand that ‌holds it, ​while Draupnir ​— ​made freely, ​on a ​wager's terms, ‌for the ​gods — ‌breeds gold ​for its ‌keeper. The ​taken ring ‌destroys; the ​given ring ‌multiplies.[1] The ​wider Germanic ​frame is ‌the ring-giving ‌culture the ‌heroic ​poetry everywhere ​assumes: the ‌lord of ​Beowulf is ​the beaga ‌brytta, the ​'ring-giver', and ​arm-rings are ‌the very ​currency of ‌the loyalty ​between lord ​and man ​— a ​self-breeding ring ‌is that ‌economy's dream ​made ‌metal.[2]

Folklorists ​file Draupnir ​among the ‌self-multiplying magic ‌treasures of ‌the international ‌motif-index, beside ​inexhaustible purses, ‌tables that ​set themselves, ​and horns ​of plenty; ‌its nearest ​mythic kin ​are things, ​not deities. ‌Within ​the corpus, ​its kin ​are its ‌fellow-wonders of ​the wager ‌— Gullinbursti ​and Mjǫllnir ‌— and ​its makers ‌Brokkr and ‌Sindri, the ​dwarf-brothers whose ​names the ​corpus binds ​to the ‌finest smith-work ‌in the ​world.[3]

Sources

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Draupnir's afterlife ​is the ‌afterlife of ‌an idea: ​the treasure ​that makes ​treasure. The ​nineteenth-century Norse ​revival fixed ‌the forging-wager as ​a set-piece ​— the ‌Kearys' The ​Heroes of ‌Asgard (1857) ​carried Brokkr ‌and Sindri ​at the ​bellows to generations ​of English-speaking ​children, and ​every handbook ‌since has ​repeated the ‌ninth-night dripping.[1]

​In contemporary ‌popular culture the ​name has ‌become a ​byword for ‌self-replicating wonders. ​God of ​War Ragnarök ‌(2022) gives ‌Brok and ‌Sindri a Draupnir ​of its ​own — ‌a spear ​forged through ​the ring's ‌multiplying magic, ​reproducing itself ​in the ‌wielder's hand — ​the most ‌widely seen ​adaptation of ​the dwarf-brothers ​and their ​craft in ‌any medium.[2] ‌Fantasy games ​and fiction reach ‌for the ​name whenever ​gear must ‌breed gear. ‌In Iceland ‌the word ‌never needed ​reviving: Draupnir ‌remains a living ​name for ​ships, firms, ​and horses, ‌the dripper ​doing ordinary ​work in ​the language ‌that wrote the ​Eddas. And ​wherever a ​modern storyteller ‌needs wealth ​that grows ‌honestly — ​by time, ‌by patience, ​by nature rather ‌than by ‌taking — ​the shape ​they reach ​for is ​Óðinn's ring, ‌whether they ‌name it ​or not.

Sources

  1. Keary, Annie, and Eliza Keary, The Heroes of Asgard: Tales from Scandinavian Mythology (London: Macmillan, 1857).
  2. God of War Ragnarök (Santa Monica Studio, 2022): the Draupnir Spear questline, 'The Realms at War'.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Draupnir is ​a literary ‌object: no inscription ‌names it ​and no find ​can be identified ​with it. ​But the ring ​it describes ‌belongs to one ​of the ​best-attested object-classes in ‌the northern ​record. Gold and ‌silver arm-rings are ​the standard ‌currency of Viking-Age ​hoards — ​the great Gotlandic ​deposits, crowned ​by the Spillings ​hoard of ‌1999, the largest ​Viking silver hoard ‌yet found, ​are full of ‌ring-money cut ​and weighed as ‌bullion, the ​archaeological reality behind ‌the poetry's ​ring-giver.[1]

The ring's ​social weight is ‌documented from ‌both sides of ‌the conversion. ​Eyrbyggja saga (ch. ​4) describes ‌a ring of ​twenty ounces ​lying on the ‌altar of the ​hof at ​Þórsnes, on which ‌all oaths ​were sworn; and ‌the Forsa ​ring of Hälsingland ​— an iron ​ring bearing ​a runic law ‌inscription, the ‌oldest legal text ​in Scandinavia ‌— survives as ​that practice's ​one famous material ‌witness.[2] Earlier still, ‌the ring-swords ‌of the Vendel-period ‌elite, with ​a ring mounted ‌on the ​pommel as the ​sign of ​the oath between ‌lord and man ​— attested ​at Valsgärde and ​famously at ‌Sutton Hoo — ​carry the ​ring's meaning back ​into the ‌Migration Age.[3] The ​manuscript witnesses are ‌the usual ​two codices: Skáldskaparmál ‌and Gylfaginning ​in the Codex ‌Regius of ‌the Prose Edda ​(GKS 2367 ​4to), Skírnismál and ​Völuspá in the ​Codex Regius ‌of the Poetic ‌Edda (GKS ​2365 4to).

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda attests ‌Draupnir twice, ‌and never ​by name in ​the same ​breath as ​its owner. ​Skírnismál 21–22 catches ‌the ring ​in motion: ​among the wooing-gifts ‌Skírnir carries ​to Gerðr ‌is 'the ​ring that was ‌burned with ​Óðinn's young ​son; eight ​of equal weight ​drip from ​it every ‌ninth night' ​— a description ‌no other ​object in ‌the corpus ​fits, read from ‌the prose ​commentators onward ‌as Draupnir ​itself. Gerðr's refusal ​is the ‌poem's own ‌judgment on treasure: ‌she lacks ​no gold ​in Gymir's ‌halls, and the ​one offer ​that cannot ‌be outbid ​buys nothing.[1]

Völuspá ​gives the ‌name its ​older root. ‌In the dvergatal ​— the ​catalogue of ​dwarfs interpolated ​into the seeress's ‌account of ‌the world's ​making — ‌a smith named ​Draupnir stands ​in the ‌list. The treasure ‌of the ‌Prose Edda ‌wears a ​name the oldest ‌poem assigns ​to a ​dwarf: the ​dripper was a ‌person before ​it was ​a possession, ​or the corpus ‌kept the ​smith's name ​on the ​smith's work.[2] Between ‌the two ​attestations the ‌Edda sketches ​the ring's whole ‌character without ​ever telling ‌its story: a ‌thing made ​under the ​earth, offered ​as a gift, ​and counted ‌worthless by ‌the one ​being wooed.

Sources

  1. Skírnismál 21–22 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Völuspá, dvergatal (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Draupnir's great witness, ‌and it ​owns both ends ​of the ​ring's story. Skáldskaparmál ​tells the making ​as part ‌of the wager ​of Loki's ​head: the crime ‌against Sif's ​hair, the three ‌treasures of ​the sons of ‌Ivaldi, the ​bet with Brokkr, ​and the forging ​under the ​fly's assault — ​pigskin, gold, ‌iron; boar, ring, ​hammer. Snorri's ‌style is at ​its best ‌here: the whole ​catastrophe of ‌the short handle ​turns on one ‌interrupted stroke ​of the bellows, ​while the ‌ring, made in ‌the middle ‌assault, comes out ​flawless. His ​sentence on its ‌nature is ​the tradition's fixed ​formula: every ninth ‌night eight ​rings of equal ​weight drip ‌from it.[1]

Gylfaginning ​49 gives ‌the ring its ​two gestures. ​At Baldr's funeral ​Óðinn lays ​Draupnir on the ‌pyre — one ‌of the ​few named acts ‌of the ​burning, beside Þórr's ​hallowing and ‌Nanna's death of ‌grief. And ‌when Hermóðr rides ‌back out ​of Hel with ‌the ransom's condition, ​Baldr's tokens ​come with him, ​Draupnir first: ‌the dead god's ​keepsake to ​his father. Snorri ​lets the ‌object carry what ​the narrative ​cannot — the ​ransom fails, the ‌god stays ​dead, and the ‌ring comes ​home anyway.[2] No ‌other object ​in the corpus ‌makes that ‌crossing in both ​directions, and ​Snorri makes nothing ​of it, which ​is the ‌surest sign the ‌tradition found ​it self-evident.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​runic inscription ‌names Draupnir: ‌the ​ring belongs ​to ​the literary ​record, and ​the ‌rune-stones keep ​their ​silence about ‌it as ​they ‌do for ​most mythological ‌objects.[1] ​What the ​runic ​corpus does ​preserve is ​the ‌ring's social ​world. ‌The Forsa ​ring of ‌Hälsingland ​— an ‌iron ​oath-ring bearing ‌the oldest ​legal ​inscription in ‌Scandinavia — ‌is ‌the material ​proof ​of the ‌ring's juridical ​weight: ​law itself ‌was, ​in one ​famous instance, ‌carried ​on a ‌ring. The ​altar ​ring of ​twenty ​ounces that ‌Eyrbyggja saga ‌describes ​at Þórsnes, ‌on ​which all ​oaths were ‌sworn, ‌belongs to ‌the same ‌complex.[2]

​In the ‌Younger ​Futhark the ​name would ​have ‌been carved ​ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᛦ ​(t-r-au-b-n-i-R): the ​ᛏ-rune writing ‌the ​voiced d, ​ᛒ ​writing p, ‌and ᛦ ​the ‌final nominative ​-r — ‌a ​rendering that ‌underspecifies ‌exactly what ​the manuscripts ​fix, ​in the ​usual ‌way of ‌the sixteen-rune ​row.[3]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. rings, oath.
  2. Eyrbyggja saga, ch. 4; the Forsa ring (Hälsingland), runic law inscription.
  3. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), on the Younger Futhark.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Outside ​the Eddas, ‌Draupnir's medieval ‌afterlife is ​quiet but ​real, and ​it runs ​along the ​ring's social ‌meaning rather ​than ​its mythology. ‌The Íslendingasögur ​keep the ‌oath-ring complex ​alive: Eyrbyggja ‌saga's account ​of the ​Þórsnes hof ​— the ​ring ​of twenty ‌ounces on ​the altar, ‌the oath ​sworn upon ‌it — ​is the ‌corpus's fullest ​picture ‌of the ​ring as ​the instrument ‌of law ‌and cult, ‌the everyday ​institution behind ​the mythic ‌wonder.[1]

The ​skaldic ​and learned ‌tradition keeps ​the name ​in its ‌proper place: ​Snorri's Skáldskaparmál, ‌which preserves ​the forging ​tale, frames ​it ​as background ‌for the ‌poets' periphrases ​— the ‌treasures of ​the gods ​are catalogued ‌so that ‌verse can ‌be ‌understood — ​and the ‌þulur, the ​mnemonic name-lists, ​keep Draupnir ​among the ‌registered wonders.[2] ​Saxo Grammaticus, ​whose ​Baldr narrative ‌diverges so ​sharply from ​Snorri's, knows ​no Draupnir ‌on his ​Balderus's pyre: ‌the ring's ​role in ‌the ​funeral belongs ‌to the ‌Icelandic telling, ​a reminder ​that the ​corpus's most ​famous treasure-journey ‌rests on ‌one tradition's ​account.[3]

  1. Eyrbyggja saga, ch. 4, trans. Hermann Pálsson and Paul Edwards (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1989).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál and the þulur, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 3 (Balderus and Høtherus), trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

The North ​was not ‌sentimental about ‌gold. Its ​greatest hoard ​is cursed ​at birth, ​its dragons ​die on ‌beds of ​metal, and ​its poets ‌ken gold ​as the ‌fire of ​the flood where ‌the Niflungar ​sank it. ​Against all ​of that ​stands one ​exception, and ‌it is ​the highest ‌treasure of ​the highest ‌god: a ​ring whose ‌only property ​is that it ‌increases.[1]

Draupnir ​does not ​shine, speak, ‌strike, or ‌protect. It ‌drips — ​on a ​schedule, in ‌the dark, ​eight for ​one every ‌ninth night, ​wealth arriving ​the way ‌wealth actually arrives ​for those ‌who keep ​it lawfully: ​slowly, cyclically, ​by nature ​rather than ‌by conquest. ‌The myth ​then does ‌the two ​most instructive ​things a ‌myth can ‌do with ‌such an object. ‌It gives ​it away: ‌Óðinn lays ​the one ​inexhaustible treasure ​in the ‌world on ​his son's ​pyre, because ​no fountain ‌of gold ​outweighs one ​act of ​love. And it ‌sends it ​back: the ‌dead return ​the gift ‌to the ​living, because ‌some bonds ‌outlast the ​boundary. Gold ​that is ​kept breeds ​gold. Gold ‌that is ‌given breeds ​everything else.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál and Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
