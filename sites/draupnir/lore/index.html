The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᚱ

The name in its original Norse form. Draupnir (ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᚱ) is attested in the source tradition — “The dripper”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

draupnir

The plain draupnir form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Draupnir

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Draupnir. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Draupnir.com → draupnir.com

The non-ASCII characters in Draupnir are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Draupnir.

Pronunciation

How Draupnir was spoken

/ˈdrɑup.nir/ Old Norse Reconstruction
Dr- Fully voiced dental stop [d] releasing directly into a trilled [r]; Old Norse initial d is unaspirated, and the cluster is voiced throughout.
-au- The falling diphthong [ɑu] — open back [ɑ] gliding to [u], the sound Old Norse wrote au, cousin to the 'ow' of English 'cow'.
-pnir Voiceless bilabial [p], alveolar nasal [n], short close [i], and the final trilled [r] of the agent suffix -nir, the same ending as in Mjǫllnir.

Modern Approximation

'DROYP-nir' — two syllables, stress on the first; 'droyp' rhyming with 'cowp', the au a single gliding vowel, the r's trilled.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Draupnir, Óðinn's ring, from the verb drjúpa 'to drip' with the agent suffix -nir: 'the dripper'. Völuspá's catalogue of dwarfs also knows a dwarf Draupnir.
  • Younger Futhark ᛏᚱᛅᚢᛒᚾᛁᛦ (t-r-au-b-n-i-R) — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age rendering, with ᛏ writing the voiced d, ᛒ writing p, and ᛦ the final nominative -r.
  • Modern Icelandic Draupnir — still current as a name for ships, companies, and horses, the dripper unbroken in the language that kept the myths.

The Accent / Script Rule

Draupnir is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying it. The richest treasure in Ásgarðr wears one of its plainest names; only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input.

05

Odin's Ring, Wealth

The Dripper of the Ninth Night

Draupnir is the golden arm-ring of Óðinn, forged by the dwarf Sindri while his brother Brokkr worked the bellows — the one treasure in the northern corpus that increases by its own nature. Every ninth night eight new rings of equal weight drip from it, so that the Allfather's wealth is not a hoard but a fountain. Óðinn laid it on Baldr's pyre as his last gift to his son, and Baldr sent it back from Hel with Hermóðr: the ring that crosses the boundary of death in both directions.

Sindri's Forge

The dwarf-smith's hearth where gold went in and the ring came out — Brokkr at the bellows, forbidden to stop, and a fly on his hand.

The Eight Rings

Every ninth night, eight rings of equal weight drip from the one — wealth that multiplies in the dark, without labour and without theft.

Baldr's Pyre

The ring Óðinn laid on Hringhorni's fire beside his dead son — the father's parting gift, sent into the smoke with the fairest of the Æsir.

Hermóðr's Return

The token Baldr sent back from Hel's hall with the nine-night rider — proof, in gold, that the errand reached the dead god's hand.

Sacred Symbols

The gold ring itself Wealth that breeds wealth — the one treasure in the corpus whose nature is increase, not keeping
The ninth night The interval of multiplication — eight rings of equal weight for every nine nights the ring is kept
Brokkr's bellows The unbroken breath of the forge — the wager turned on a blast no fly could interrupt twice
The fly (Loki) The sting that cost the hammer its handle but could not touch the ring — malice spent against patience
The pyre-token The gift that goes down with the dead and returns with the messenger — love's keepsake across Hel's gate
06

Mythology

Stories of Draupnir

Draupnir's dossier divides cleanly in two: Skáldskaparmál tells its making, in the wager that also produced Gullinbursti and Mjǫllnir, and the Baldr cycle tells its journey — into the fire with the dead god and back out of Hel with the living one's token. Between those poles the Poetic Edda catches it twice: offered and refused in Skírnismál, and catalogued as a dwarf's own name in Völuspá.

Skáldskaparmál

The Wager of Loki's Head

It begins with a crime of Loki's: he cut off all the hair of Sif, Þórr's wife, for mischief. Þórr caught him and would have broken every bone in him, but Loki swore to get the black-elves to make Sif hair of gold that would grow like other hair. He went to the dwarfs called the sons of Ivaldi, and they made the golden hair, and Skíðblaðnir, the best of ships, for Freyr, and the spear Gungnir for Óðinn. Then Loki boasted, and his boasting made the bet: he wagered his own head with the dwarf Brokkr that Brokkr's brother Sindri could not make three treasures as good as these.

Snorri frames the whole episode as an explanation of how the gods got their treasures — the myth exists in the Edda to account for the properties of the great objects, and Draupnir's property is the strangest of them: it does not merely exist; it multiplies.

Skáldskaparmál

Three Treasures from the Bellows

Sindri laid a pig's skin in the forge and told Brokkr to blow, and never to stop until he himself drew the work out. When the smith had gone from the forge, a fly settled on Brokkr's hand and stung him — but he blew on. Out came the boar with bristles of gold, Gullinbursti. Next Sindri laid gold in the forge and gave the same command; the fly settled on Brokkr's neck and stung twice as hard — but he blew on. Out came the ring Draupnir. Last came iron, and the fly settled between Brokkr's eyes and stung the eyelid until blood ran down and blinded him; he swept the fly away with his hand, and for that one moment the bellows failed — and so the hammer Mjǫllnir came out with its handle too short. The fly was Loki, spending himself against the bellows.

The brothers carried the three treasures to Ásgarðr to be judged against Loki's three. Óðinn received the spear Gungnir and the ring Draupnir, and Snorri gives the ring's nature in a single sentence the whole tradition repeats: every ninth night, eight rings of equal weight drip from it. Freyr took the ship and the boar; Þórr took the hammer. The Æsir judged Mjǫllnir the best of all, because it was the surest defence against the rime-giants — but Loki kept his head on a point of law: Brokkr might have the head, but not one hair of the neck. So the dwarf sewed Loki's lips shut instead, and the wager closed with the gods six treasures richer.

Skírnismál 21–22

The Ring Refused

The Poetic Edda catches the ring in motion, being offered as a bride-price. When Freyr's servant Skírnir rides to Jötunheimr to woo the giant's daughter Gerðr for his lovesick master, he carries gifts, and the second of them is the ring: 'the ring that was burned with Óðinn's young son; eight of equal weight drip from it every ninth night.' The poem does not name it — it does not need to; no other ring in the corpus matches the description, and the tradition from the prose commentators onward has read it as Draupnir.

Gerðr refuses it: she wants no gold, she says, for she lacks none in her father Gymir's halls, and will not share wealth with Freyr's messenger. The episode is a quiet marvel of the corpus: the self-breeding ring of the Allfather, the one gift that cannot be outbid, laid before a giantess who counts it worthless. Only Skírnir's curse, carved in runes and threats, moves her where Óðinn's treasure could not — the poem's own verdict that gold, even multiplying gold, buys nothing that matters.

Gylfaginning 49

The Ring on the Pyre

When Baldr lay dead of the mistletoe and the Æsir built his pyre on Hringhorni, the greatest of ships, the funeral gathered the whole wealth of the gods' grief. Nanna died of sorrow at the sight of him and was laid beside her husband; Þórr hallowed the pyre with Mjǫllnir; and Óðinn did the thing this temple exists to record: he laid the ring Draupnir on the fire with his son. No word of his is preserved with the gift. The dripper, the fountain of the Allfather's gold, went into the smoke as a father's last giving to the child he could not keep.

The corpus keeps the gesture where it belongs — among the funeral's few named acts, beside the hallowing hammer and the burst heart. A ring that makes eight more of itself every ninth night is the least losable treasure in the world, and Óðinn loses it deliberately, which is exactly what makes it a gift.

Gylfaginning 49 · Völuspá

Back from Hel, and the Dwarf's Name

The story does not let the ring rest in the fire. Hermóðr the Bold rode nine nights down the dark road, leapt Helgrindr on Sleipnir, and found Baldr seated in the high seat of Hel's hall. When Hel set her famous condition — that all things in the world must weep Baldr out — Baldr sent tokens home with the rider, and first among them was Draupnir, returned to Óðinn as a keepsake. The ransom failed at Þökk's one refusal, and Baldr stayed with the dead; but the ring came back across the boundary that nothing else crosses, carrying the dead god's love to the living father. It is the only object in the corpus to make that journey in both directions.

Völuspá preserves the name's older, stranger root: in the dvergatal, the great catalogue of dwarfs, a dwarf named Draupnir stands in the list beside Dólgþrasir and his kin. The treasure-name and the dwarf-name are the same word — 'the dripper' — and scholars have long noted how naturally the corpus names its wonders after its smiths. The ring that drips gold wears a name the dwarf-lists already owned, as if the Edda kept, fossilized in its oldest poem, the memory that such things come from under the earth.

Extended Lore

The North was not sentimental about gold. Its greatest hoard is cursed at birth, its dragons die on their beds of metal, and its poets ken gold as the fire of the flood where the Niflungar sank it. Against all of that stands one exception, and it is the highest treasure of the highest god: a ring whose only property is that it increases. Draupnir does not shine, speak, strike, or protect. It drips — on a schedule, in the dark, eight for one every ninth night, wealth arriving the way wealth actually arrives for those who keep it lawfully: slowly, cyclically, and by nature rather than by conquest. The myth then does the two most instructive things a myth can do with such an object. It gives it away: Óðinn lays the one inexhaustible treasure in the world on his son's pyre, because no fountain of gold outweighs one act of love. And it sends it back: the dead return the gift to the living, because some bonds outlast the boundary. Between those two gestures the ring becomes something stranger than a treasure — a measure of what treasure is for. The cursed ring of the dwarf [[andvari|Andvari]] passes from hand to hand and each owner dies; the dripper passes from father to son and back to father, and the only thing that multiplies is the meaning. Gold that is kept breeds gold. Gold that is given breeds everything else.
