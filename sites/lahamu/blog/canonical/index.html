Lahamu: canonical, contested, and the false forms on record

A letter to a colleague, 31 July 2026 — on the name Lahamu, and on the forms that keep failing it.

Your note said, roughly: surely the marks are a nicety. Permit me to use Lahamu to show they are not.

First, the attestation. The attested form is 𒀭𒆷𒄩𒈬, from the tradition's own record. The canonical transliteration is Lahamu.

Say it aloud as the sources heard it: 'la-HAH-moo' — stress the middle syllable and let the final vowel settle like water in a deep basin. The marks are what carry that sound to a reader who has never heard it spoken.

On the plain form — and I want to be precise, because you pushed on this. The plain form lahamu differs from the canonical Lahamu only by convention of display — case, not content. It is acceptable everywhere: in legacy systems, in plain-text citation, in the DNS itself. What is not acceptable is what follows in the register: respellings that change the letters, not just their case.

One more thing, because it always surprises: the name did not stay home. Lahamu has no independent cult outside the genealogical poetry of the Enuma Elish. She is inseparable from Lahmu; the two function as a single cosmogonic unit.

And it never stopped working. Lahamu survives chiefly in the study of Mesopotamian cosmogony. Unlike Tiāmat, she has not become a modern icon of primordial feminine chaos; unlike Marduk, she has no festivals or city cults.

The Convention's Own History

Cuneiform writes a consonant inventory English does not have: š, ṣ, ṭ, ḫ mark sibilants and stops that Akkadian and Sumerian distinguished and meaning depends on — the difference between 'sun' and 'son' is exactly one ṣ. Assyriological transliteration (the sign lists of Borger and the Chicago Assyrian Dictionary) settled those marks over the last century and a half, and the register keeps them because dropping them merges gods, cities, and concepts that the tablets keep apart.

Where Protocol Meets Practice

The technical answer is simple: the canonical marks are legal IDNA 2008 characters, encoded as punycode under `xn--`. The practical answer is messier: legal does not mean stocked (registrar inventory is partial), and stocked does not mean typeable (stacked diacritics defeat mobile keyboards).

So the register's rule is a hierarchy of attested forms — full restoration, circumflex, macron-only, attested variant, and finally plain ASCII for the systems that cannot carry marks. Every rung is defensible because every rung is attested. The forms this register rules against are not rungs; they are errors, and errors do not get rungs.

Write back when you have checked the sources; they are better company than I am.

— The Register

The Verdict

Lahamu is the canonical form: attested, checkable, and enforced across the temple, the lexicon, the cards, the APIs, and this register. The plain form lahamu shares that standing — the two are the same name in every system. The false forms above are documented so that no reader has to take the temple's word on faith — the violations are stated, the sources are named, and the register stands open to challenge on every line.

The temple of Lahamu stands at /lahamu/.

The docket for this ruling: no false forms on the docket; no contested forms attested; the attestation rests on 8 cited sources — Enuma Elish, CAD (Chicago Assyrian Dictionary), Black & Green, Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia.
