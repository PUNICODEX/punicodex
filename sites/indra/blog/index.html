Mythology

The Indra of the Rigveda is a storm in divine form: mighty, boisterous, sometimes embarrassed, always central. ​His one great myth — the combat with Vritra — is the Vedic creation story: the waters of life imprisoned by the dragon, the thunderbolt that splits the mountains, the rivers released. ​Everything else in his mythology is commentary on that victory.

The Slaying of Vritra (Rigveda 1.32)

Vritra, the “Enveloper”, coiled about the world’s waters and held them fast in his mountain caves. ​Indra, armed with the vajra forged by Tvashtri from Dadhichi’s bones, struck the dragon on its flank; the waters burst free and flooded to the sea. ‘Like lowing cows the waters rushed’ ‌— the oldest dragon-slaying in recorded literature, and the template for every one after it.

The Drink of Soma (Rigveda 8)

Before the combat, Indra drinks three lakes of soma pressed by ​the priests; filled with the draught, his strength is irresistible and his belly vast. The hymns taunt and flatter him by turns — the Vedic poets knew their storm-god was vain, and ‌loved him for it. Soma is both his fuel and his fee.

The Intoxication Hymn (Rigveda 10.119)

The most human hymn in the Rigveda: Indra speaks in the first person, drunk on ​soma, boasting — ‘I will break the earth’s two halves apart, like a gourd I will break the two worlds’. The gods’ king, for one hymn, sounds like a warrior at the ‌feast, and the poets let him.

From King of Gods to Dethroned Elder (Epic & Purāṇic Cycle)

In the later epics the balance shifts: Vishnu’s avatars take the great victories, and ​Indra becomes the anxious king who sins — the seducer of Ahalyā, the raider of Triparu, the god who must yield his throne’s preeminence. The Purāṇas preserve a dignity-muted Indra, still lord ‌of the eastern quarter and of the storm, but no longer the cosmos’ protagonist.

​Across Cultures

In Buddhism Indra becomes Śakra, lord of the Trāyastriṃśa heaven, subservient to the Buddha and guardian of the dharma — the storm-god converted into a devotee. In Jainism he is ​a class of heaven-rulers. Greek observers heard Zeus in him; modern comparative philology confirmed the kinship: Indra and Zeus descend from the same Proto-Indo-European sky-father’s thunderbolt.

Kindred figures in the PuniCodex cross-tradition ‌index include Þórr (thunder sovereignty), Zeús (thunder sovereignty), Adad (thunder / storm sovereignty), Baꜥal (thunder / storm sovereignty), Enlīl (thunder / storm sovereignty), and Illapa (thunder / storm sovereignty).

Cultural Legacy

He ​is embedded in the oldest Indo-European poetry extant — every study of dragon-slaying, thunder-gods, or soma/haoma ritual begins with him. The word ‘Indra’ entered Southeast Asian royal names (Indravarman, Indrapura) carried by ​Hindu expansion; and the Vajra passed east into Tibetan Buddhism as the thunderbolt-sceptre of the vajrayāna itself.
