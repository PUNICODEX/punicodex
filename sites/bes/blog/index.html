Mythology

Bes has no myth because he was always already there — at the door, ​at the bed, at the birth. His ‘theology’ is apotropaic: power through ugliness, protection through fright. Later tradition gave him a birth and a family (the lion-dwarf Pantheos of ​the late period), but his real domain never moved from the threshold of ordinary Egyptian life.

The Mask of the Midwife (Birth Protection)

In the birth-chamber, the midwife donned ​the Bes-mask and danced his dance; his grimace drew the malign spirits’ attention onto himself and away from mother and child. The Beset-amulets and the ‘Bes-chamber’ scenes of Deir ‌el-Medina show the rite in domestic detail.

The Dwarf Who Amuses the Gods (Papyrus Westcar Analogy)

The Westcar Papyrus’ wonder-tale of the dwarf Dedi, who dances and performs before ​the court, is read as Bes’ narrative shadow — the small deformed being whose skill and humour carry more power than the great. Bes never speaks in the texts; ‌he dances.

Bes the Pantheos (Late Period Theology)

By the Late Period, Bes absorbed attributes of every major god — the wings of Horus, the crown of Amun, the ​snakes of Mehen — becoming the ‘Pantheos-Bes’, one dwarf wearing the whole pantheon’s power. The type, with its many arms and animal faces, spread through the Greco-Roman world as ‌an amulet-image.

The Son of the Great Goddess (Tutu & Bes)

At Saqqara and in the demotic tales, Bes appears as the son of Neith, sent against the enemies ​of the gods — a dwarf-hero with a sword. The strand gives him a genealogy the earlier household cult never needed: the protector domesticated into a champion.
