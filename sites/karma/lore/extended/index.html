The name Karma reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the sanskrit tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

Its liturgy is life itself: the gift, the vow, the fast, the pilgrimage — each a karma audited at once by the doer. The śrāddha for the dead transfers merit across the ledger; the daily recitation of the deed-formulae keeps the accounts current.

In the Earth

Karma leaves no temple — it is the reason temples leave merit. The stūpas’ circumambulation-paths, the ghāṭs’ staircases into the rivers, and the endless charity-inscriptions of Indian epigraphy are its monuments: every recorded gift is a seed on record.

Iconography

How Karma was imaged across the ages

The Image

The wheel is karma’s emblem — the wheel of becoming, bhavacakra, held by Yama in Tibetan paintings, its hub marked by the three poisons. The ox and the cart, the seed and the sprout, the hunter’s net: the doctrine’s imagery is agricultural, mechanical, forensic.

06

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

The doctrine entered Western thought through Schopenhauer — who called it the finest idea humanity had produced — and through theosophy into the modern vernacular, where it now functions as secularized justice. Religious studies treat it as the most successful moral-causal theory ever devised: private, precise, and impersonal as gravity.

Identifications Across Traditions

Karma is the one doctrine shared by Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain without alteration of name — the subcontinent’s common physics. The West received it through theosophy and thence into the global vernacular, where ‘karma’ now means cosmic payback in any language; classical comparisons run to the Egyptian heart-weighing and the Greek Adrasteia.

Modern Legacy

The word entered every major language as a loan — among the few Sanskrit terms to do so. It underwrites the theory of rebirth, the ethics of non-harm (the deed’s victim returns as one’s debtor), and the quietist strand of Indian political thought: the revolution you cannot make today, your karma will make eventually.

The Meditation

What Karma means — beyond the dictionary

Karma: the plainest noun in the batch — ‘deed’ — carrying the heaviest doctrine. Restored without marks, it reads as it acts: no ornament, no exemption, no appeal. The lexicon’s other names guard temples; this one guards the law the temples were built to honour.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Karma, Action, Consequence, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Karma?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Karma is /ˈkɑːrmə/ — approximately “KAHR-muh”.

02What does Karma mean?

Karma means Deed, action in the sanskrit tradition.

03What are the symbols of Karma?

Karma is associated with The Seed and the Fruit (Every deed is a seed; the fruit may ripen in this life or a later one — but it ripens.), Action by Mind, Word, Deed (Karma accrues by thought, speech, and body — the three doors of action the law watches.), The Burning Off (Karma can be exhausted by experience — burned like a field of stubble — or bound by attachment like a new seed.), Liberation Beyond It (Mokṣa, nirvāṇa: the states beyond karma’s reach — the doctrines’ common horizon.).

04Why restore Karma in Unicode?

Karma is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.
