Born of the Jewel in ​Akaniṣṭha (The Vow)

In the Mahāyāna cosmology codified in the yoga-tantra canon — above all the Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha, the 'Compendium of the Truth of All Tathāgatas' — each of the five Dhyani Buddhas ‌began as a bodhisattva who made universal vows before the Buddhas of former eons and attained perfect enlightenment in Akaniṣṭha, the highest heaven of form. Ratnasambhava's vow was the vow of giving: ​to ripen in all beings the capacity for generosity, the first of the perfections (pāramitā), until they too stood as self-arisen jewels. When the vow was complete, he awoke as the Buddha ‌of the south, and the other Buddhas assigned him the ratna family — the line of beings whose affliction is avarice and whose fruit is equality.

The 'myth' is doctrine wearing narrative dress: ​enlightenment here is not escape from the world but its enrichment — a Buddha whose very title declares that what is most precious arises from within.

The South Gate of the Five ‌Families (The Maṇḍala)

The five-family maṇḍala arranges the awakened mind as a cosmic cross: Vairocana at the center, Akṣobhya in the east, Ratnasambhava in the south, Amitābha in the west, Amoghasiddhi in ​the north. Each presides over a family (kula) of beings bound by one of the five poisons and liberated by its counter-wisdom. Ratnasambhava's family is the ratna-kula: those in whom pride and ‌greed dominate. His counter-agent is the wisdom of equality (samatā-jñāna) — the direct seeing that self and other, gold and dust, are equal in emptiness — and his emblematic act is the ​varada mudrā, the open hand. The Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha and its commentarial tradition assign him his mantra, his color, and his throne; Tibetan manuals such as those of the Sa skya school describe his visualization ​in detail for initiates.

The maṇḍala's teaching is exact: the poison of the south is not destroyed but converted — avarice, rightly seen, is the very energy of giving.

Māmakī and the ‌Wisdom of Equality (The Consort)

In the later tantric development of the five-Buddha system, each Dhyani Buddha is paired with a consort who embodies the wisdom (prajñā) complementing his method (upāya): Ākāśadhātvīśvarī ‌with Vairocana, Locanā with Akṣobhya, Māmakī with Ratnasambhava, Pāṇḍaravāsinī with Amitābha, Tārā with Amoghasiddhi. Māmakī — 'the Stainless One', golden in color, her name a cult-title already known from the inscriptions and ‌art of the Gupta age — stands at the south gate of the maṇḍala as the wisdom of equality in female form. In Tibetan iconography the pair appears in yab-yum, the divine embrace: ​two figures, one awakening. Snellgrove and other historians of Indo-Tibetan Buddhism trace this pairing through the yoga-tantra commentaries into the initiatory maṇḍalas still conferred in Tibetan lineages today.

The pairing is the ​theology of relationship: equality is not a solitary insight but one that arrives, as wisdom does, between two.
