Before the Office, the Story

A Son of Heaven and Earth. In Theogony (132–136), Hesiod catalogues the children Gaia bore to Ouranos: the deep-swirling Ocean, then Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, and Iapetus, and with them the Titanesses — Theía, Rheía, Thémis, Mnēmosýnē, Phoíbē, and Tēthýs — and finally Krónos, 'crooked-counselled, the most terrible of her children'. Hyperíōn enters the poem with no deed attached, only a station: his name already declares it, from ὑπέρ, 'above'. He is born of the union of Sky and Earth and belongs to the sky's own house.

Father of Sun, Moon, and Dawn. In Theogony (371–374), Hyperíōn takes his sister Theía — 'the divine one' — to wife, and she bears him Hēlios the Sun, Selḗnē the Moon, and Ēṓs the Dawn, 'who shines on all that dwell on the earth and on the deathless gods who hold broad heaven'. The marriage is a small cosmology in itself: the High One wed to the Divine produces every light that crosses the sky. Pindar opens Isthmian 5 by addressing Theía as 'mother of the Sun, of many names', the hymn-like acknowledgement of this same union.

Not the First Rebrand

Rome produced no native Hyperion; the Titan travelled into Latin letters chiefly as a learned name for the sun itself, the Hesiodic father and the Homeric epithet collapsing into one solar word in the poets. Hyginus still lists him soberly among the Titans born of Earth and Sky (Fabulae, praefatio), but in later epic usage 'Hyperion' rides the sky in place of Sol. The office is only the latest translation.
