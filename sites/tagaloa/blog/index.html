The Vine Let Down into the Sea (The Making of the Isles)

In the ​beginning Tagaloa dwelt alone in Lani-uli, the highest heaven, and beneath him lay only the boundless sea. He let down into the waters a vine from the sky; where its parts touched ‌the surface, the islands of Samoa rose. On the largest he set the first settlement, and from the sky he watched over it. The fullest form of this creation account was recorded ​by the missionary J. B. Stair in Old Samoa (1897) and compared with related versions by Augustin Krämer in The Samoa Islands (1902); George Turner had published a shorter form in Nineteen ‌Years in Polynesia (1861). Manu'a, the island group of the myth's first landing, is still styled the 'beginning of Samoa' in formal oratory, and the creation chant remained the deep charter for its ​paramount titles. Sina and the Eel (The Daughter of Heaven)

Tagaloa's daughter Sina came down to the pool of Mata o le Alelo to bathe, where a great eel, Tuna, confessed its ​love. When the eel was killed at Sina's command, she buried its head, and from it grew the first coconut palm — its two eyes, mouth, and body still showing on the ‌nut. The tale was recorded across Polynesia (Gill, Myths and Songs from the South Pacific, 1876; Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, §§ on the coconut cycle) and became the most retold of all ​Samoan stories. In Samoa the cycle carries a chiefly resonance as well: Sina's name became a byword for beauty in proverb, and her bathing pool, Mata o le Alelo, was pointed out to ‌visitors into the twentieth century. The Storeys of the Sky (The Heavens Above)

Samoan cosmology stacks the sky into storeys — Lani-uli, Lani-ʻula, and the heavens beneath them ​— and places Tagaloa at the summit, above the winds that carry rain. Krämer (The Samoa Islands, 1902) recorded prayers addressing the ascending heavens by name, each storey ruled or traversed on ‌the way to the Lord's seat. No text gives a complete, consistent map; the ethnographers themselves note that districts preserved different orders of the heavens. Below the storeys of the sky the ‌same cosmology placed the underworld and the remote ends of the earth, and prayers were imagined to rise through the layers like visitors announced at the thresholds of a great chief's house.
