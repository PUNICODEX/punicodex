The Vine Let Down into the Sea (The Making of the Isles)

In the beginning Tagaloa dwelt alone in Lani-uli, the highest heaven, and beneath ​him lay only the boundless sea. He let down into the waters a vine from the sky; where its parts touched the surface, the islands of Samoa rose. On the largest he ‌set the first settlement, and from the sky he watched over it. The fullest form of this creation account was recorded by the missionary J. B. Stair in Old Samoa (1897) and compared ​with related versions by Augustin Krämer in The Samoa Islands (1902); George Turner had published a shorter form in Nineteen Years in Polynesia (1861). Manu'a, the island group of the myth's first landing, ‌is still styled the 'beginning of Samoa' in formal oratory, and the creation chant remained the deep charter for its paramount titles. Sina and the Eel (The Daughter of Heaven)

Tagaloa's daughter Sina ‌came down to the pool of Mata o le Alelo to bathe, where a great eel, Tuna, confessed its love. When the eel was killed at Sina's command, she buried its head, ​and from it grew the first coconut palm — its two eyes, mouth, and body still showing on the nut. The tale was recorded across Polynesia (Gill, Myths and Songs from the South ‌Pacific, 1876; Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, §§ on the coconut cycle) and became the most retold of all Samoan stories. In Samoa the cycle carries a chiefly resonance as well: Sina's name ​became a byword for beauty in proverb, and her bathing pool, Mata o le Alelo, was pointed out to visitors into the twentieth century. The Storeys of the Sky ​(The Heavens Above)

Samoan cosmology stacks the sky into storeys — Lani-uli, Lani-ʻula, and the heavens beneath them — and places Tagaloa at the summit, above the winds that carry rain. Krämer (The ​Samoa Islands, 1902) recorded prayers addressing the ascending heavens by name, each storey ruled or traversed on the way to the Lord's seat. No text gives a complete, consistent map; the ethnographers themselves ‌note that districts preserved different orders of the heavens. Below the storeys of the sky the same cosmology placed the underworld and the remote ends of the earth, and prayers were imagined ‌to rise through the layers like visitors announced at the thresholds of a great chief's house. A ‌Meditation

What Tagaloa teaches is that creation can be an act of looking down. His world begins not with a battle or a body but with a lord in the highest heaven and ​an empty sea — and then a decision: to let something down. Polynesian cosmology is famously oceanic, yet its supreme god is a sky-dweller; the islands are the rope and bridge between ​the two. This is a theology suited to navigators: the world is made of layers — sky, sea, land, underworld — and authority flows along the vertical axis, from above downward, from the ‌god to the chief to the people. Tagaloa is therefore less a 'god of' any one thing than the source of rank itself: where he acts, land appears; where his daughter descends, chiefs ‌are born. To study him is to see how a non-literate society stored its constitution in cosmology, safe from conquest, until ethnographers wrote it down.
