Mythology

Stories of Tagaloa

Tagaloa's mythology is cosmogony in the strict sense — stories of how land, people, and rank came to be. It survives not in epic but in creation chants recorded by nineteenth-century missionaries and ethnographers, whose accounts disagree in detail but agree that the world began when the Lord of the Heavens acted upon the sea.

The Making of the Isles

The Vine Let Down into the Sea

In the beginning Tagaloa dwelt alone in Lani-uli, the highest heaven, and beneath him lay only the boundless sea. He let down into the waters a vine from the sky; where its parts touched the surface, the islands of Samoa rose. On the largest he set the first settlement, and from the sky he watched over it. The fullest form of this creation account was recorded by the missionary J. B. Stair in Old Samoa (1897) and compared with related versions by Augustin Krämer in The Samoa Islands (1902); George Turner had published a shorter form in Nineteen Years in Polynesia (1861). Manu'a, the island group of the myth's first landing, is still styled the 'beginning of Samoa' in formal oratory, and the creation chant remained the deep charter for its paramount titles. The Daughter of Heaven

Sina and the Eel

Tagaloa's daughter Sina came down to the pool of Mata o le Alelo to bathe, where a great eel, Tuna, confessed its love. When the eel was killed at Sina's command, she buried its head, and from it grew the first coconut palm — its two eyes, mouth, and body still showing on the nut. The tale was recorded across Polynesia (Gill, Myths and Songs from the South Pacific, 1876; Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, §§ on the coconut cycle) and became the most retold of all Samoan stories. In Samoa the cycle carries a chiefly resonance as well: Sina's name became a byword for beauty in proverb, and her bathing pool, Mata o le Alelo, was pointed out to visitors into the twentieth century. The Heavens Above

The Storeys of the Sky

Samoan cosmology stacks the sky into storeys — Lani-uli, Lani-ʻula, and the heavens beneath them — and places Tagaloa at the summit, above the winds that carry rain. Krämer (The Samoa Islands, 1902) recorded prayers addressing the ascending heavens by name, each storey ruled or traversed on the way to the Lord's seat. No text gives a complete, consistent map; the ethnographers themselves note that districts preserved different orders of the heavens. Below the storeys of the sky the same cosmology placed the underworld and the remote ends of the earth, and prayers were imagined to rise through the layers like visitors announced at the thresholds of a great chief's house. Yet the structure is constant:Yet the structure is constant: the sacred is vertical, and the higher the heaven, the nearer the Lord. Cosmology here is not speculation but orientation — the same layering a navigator reads in cloud and star.

The Polynesian Dispersal

Tangaroa Driven to the Sea

In the Māori version of the same divine name, Tangaroa is a child of Ranginui and Papatūānuku, the sky-father and earth-mother crushed in their parents' tight embrace. When Tāne thrust his parents apart to let in the light, Tangaroa fled to the ocean, and his enmity with Tāne — whose forests silt the sea — endures in myth (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, 1855; Orbell, A Concise Encyclopedia of Māori Myth and Legend, 1998). The Hawaiian Kanaloa and Tahitian Ta'aroa preserve further branches of the same god. Linguists reconstruct the shared ancestral form and read the god's names as a map of settlement itself — each island's dialect keeping its own office for the one Lord. 02

Tagaloa

Creator

Tagaloa is the supreme creator god of the Samoan islands, lord of the layered heavens who looked down on the empty sea and made the land. He is the maker of islands, the father of gods and chiefs, and the dweller in the highest sky — the figure around whom Samoan cosmology turns.

Creator of the Isles

He threw down the vine and stone from which the Samoan islands were formed, founding the world itself.

Lord of the Heavens

He dwells in Lani-uli, the highest of the layered heavens, above wind, rain, and the soaring birds.

Father of Sina

His daughter Sina, mother of chiefs, anchors the island's greatest cycle of stories.

Ocean's Lord

Across Polynesia his name is the sea's: Tangaroa in Aotearoa, Ta'aroa in Tahiti, Kanaloa in Hawai'i.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Tagaloa

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Tagaloa is the standard Polynesian romanisation, documented in academic sources — “The lord”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual polynesian names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

tagaloa

The plain tagaloa form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Tagaloa. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Tagaloa was spoken

/ta.ŋa.ˈlo.a/ Approximate Samoan phonemic rendering
ta- Open a as in 'father'; in Samoan orthography t is lightly tapped.
-ga- The letter g in Samoan is the velar nasal [ŋ], the 'ng' of 'singer'.
-lo- The stressed syllable, with a clear, round o.
-a Final open a, short and bright.

Modern Approximation

'tah-ngah-LO-ah' — stress falls on the third syllable, and the g is a soft 'ng,' never a hard English g.

Extended Lore

What Tagaloa teaches is that creation can be an act of looking down. His world begins not with a battle or a body but with a lord in the highest heaven and an empty sea — and then a decision: to let something down. Polynesian cosmology is famously oceanic, yet its supreme god is a sky-dweller; the islands are the rope and bridge between the two. This is a theology suited to navigators: the world is made of layers — sky, sea, land, underworld — and authority flows along the vertical axis, from above downward, from the god to the chief to the people. Tagaloa is therefore less a 'god of' any one thing than the source of rank itself: where he acts, land appears; where his daughter descends, chiefs are born. To study him is to see how a non-literate society stored its constitution in cosmology, safe from conquest, until ethnographers wrote it down.
