Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

Cōātlīcue's temple was the Great Temple of Tenochtitlan itself. Her shrine stood at the foot of the pyramid, her daughter's vast dismembered disk — Coyolxāuhqui — lay exactly where the myth threw her, and Huitzilopōchtli's sanctuary reared above. The cult's great performance was Panquetzaliztli, the month of raising the banners, when the birth myth on Coatepec was re-enacted on the pyramid stair with a living image of the war god and columns of captives; the temple's 1487 dedication reportedly consumed thousands. How far the precinct's 'Mother of the Gods' was Cōātlīcue as against Tōnantzin or Tōci is honestly debated — the sweeping-festival Ochpaniztli, with its flayed woman-impersonator of the old earth goddess, belongs to that same complex — and some scholars, Karl Taube most prominently, read even the great statue itself as Tlāltēcuhtli. Her most eloquent votaries are architectural: the pyramid is her cult image, enlarged to the scale of a capital.

In the Earth

The most famous image of Cōātlīcue is the 2.5-meter basalt statue discovered near the Cathedral of Mexico City in 1790, now in the National Museum of Anthropology. Excavations at the Templo Mayor have revealed her shrine at the foot of the temple pyramid, the massive Coyolxāuhqui stone disk, and numerous offerings of seashells, coral, and sacrificial remains. The spatial layout of the sacred precinct dramatizes the myth: Coatepec as the temple, Cōātlīcue as its base, and Huitzilopōchtli's shrine above.

05

Iconography

How Cōātlīcue was imaged across the ages

The Image

One statue fixed her image forever. The colossal basalt Cōātlīcue — over two and a half metres of it — surfaced near Mexico City's cathedral in August 1790: skirt of interlaced serpents, necklace of alternating human hearts and hands, bare breasts, clawed feet, and no head at all, her face replaced by two facing serpents whose meeting forms it. The reading is argument, not illustration: serpents for the fertile and dangerous earth, trophies for her appetite for the dead. The pre-conquest pedigree of the family is anchored by her daughter: the Coyolxāuhqui disk, carved in the fifteenth century and excavated at the Great Temple in 1978, shows the same monumental grammar — limbs splayed in a compass pattern, ornament dense as rhetoric. In manuscripts the birth myth kept circulating through the colonial centuries, the Florentine Codex illustrations re-staging Coatepec for European paper. The statue rests today in the Museo Nacional de Antropología, still mid-argument over whether the earth-mother wears her own name.

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

Cōātlīcue's afterlife begins with a discovery. Unearthed in 1790 beside the Sun Stone, her statue staggered the viceregal capital and was buried again almost at once — too naked, too heathen — before being exhumed for good; Humboldt carried her fame to Europe in Vues des Cordillères (1810–13). The nineteenth century made her the first lady of what Europe called the Aztec sublime: Kingsborough's Antiquities of Mexico placed her sculptural world in learned libraries, Désiré Charnay's expedition photographs, published in 1863, let the public finally see her, and young Mexican nationalism adopted her as an emblem older than the conquest. The twentieth century gave her back her context: the 1964 opening of the Museo Nacional de Antropología, the Great Temple excavations from 1978, and a generation of scholarship — Pasztory's Aztec Art, Moctezuma's temple studies, Taube's earth-lord counter-argument — turned a curiosity into a theological document. Modern artists and writers took her from there, which is another field's story; the reception proper ends with scholarship still re-reading her snakes.

Identifications Across Traditions

Cōātlīcue overlaps functionally with Tlāltēcuhtli, the 'Lord of the Earth,' whose toad-like crouching form receives the same dead, and with Tōnantzin, 'Our Mother,' the more diffuse and venerable earth-mother honoured at shrines across the highlands. The Spanish friars read these overlapping female earth powers through the lens of the Virgin Mary, and the basilica of Guadalupe rose at Tepeyac, a site of Tōnantzin's cult — a fusion that the friar Sahagún himself noticed with dismay. Whether Guadalupe is a successful Christian substitution or a pre-contact devotion wearing a new name is still argued; what is certain is that the earth-mother did not leave her mountain. Modern scholars add a further identification: some read the decapitated Cōātlīcue — head replaced by serpents, womb as the earth's mouth — as the night sky or the devouring earth of myth-time, making her the same power the Mexica worshipped under several names and the Spanish could not name at all.

Modern Legacy

The monumental basalt statue of Cōātlīcue unearthed in Mexico City in 1790 — nearly eight feet of serpents, skull, and clawed feet — became one of the defining images of Aztec art and was displayed beside the Calendar Stone and the Stone of Tīzoc; she now stands in the National Museum of Anthropology as an emblem of Mesoamerican sacred power. The Templo Mayor excavations and the Coyolxāuhqui disk made her myth a public story again, taught in Mexican schools and staged in the Zócalo's Aztec dance revivals. In contemporary Mexican and Chicana thought — from the poetry of Cherríe Moraga to Gloria Anzaldúa's writings on the borderlands — Cōātlīcue has been reclaimed as a symbol of indestructible female creativity, the earth that survives conquest and continues to give birth. Artists and activists invoke her when the land itself is the subject: migration, extraction, the wounded ground that will not stop bearing.

The Meditation

What Cōātlīcue means — beyond the dictionary

Cōātlīcue asks us to look at the earth without sentimentality. She is not the passive 'mother nature' of romantic painting; she is the soil that receives corpses, the mountain that gives birth to warriors, the skirt of snakes that moves even when she stands still. To approach her is to accept that fertility and death are not opposites but phases of a single metabolism.

In an age of ecological crisis, her image is uncomfortably apt. The earth does not owe us gentleness; it owes us only reciprocity. Cōātlīcue's necklace of hearts is a stark symbol of what must be given back for life to continue. The name Cōātlīcue, with its long vowels restored, sounds out a truth older than empire: everything that rises from the ground eventually returns to it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Cōātlīcue, Earth, Mother of Gods, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Cōātlīcue?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Cōātlīcue is /koː.aːˈtɬiː.kwe/ — approximately 'koh-AH-tlee-kweh' — the 'tl' is one crisp sound, and the macrons stretch the o, a, and i into long vowels.

02What does Cōātlīcue mean?

Cōātlīcue means She of the serpent skirt in the nahuatl tradition.

03What are the symbols of Cōātlīcue?

Cōātlīcue is associated with Serpent skirt (Her identifying garment of intertwined snakes, symbolizing the living, dangerous, regenerative earth), Necklace of hands and hearts (A garland of sacrificial trophies marking her role as receiver of war-dead and tribute), Two serpent heads (Her own head is replaced by facing serpent faces, signifying duality and the gaping maw of the earth), Clawed feet (Talons that can dig and grasp, identifying her with predatory earth powers and burial).

04Why restore Cōātlīcue in Unicode?

Plain ASCII coatlicue strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Cōātlīcue?
