Mythology

Nun’s mythology is Egypt’s creation mythology seen from the water’s side. He never acts — that ​is his nature, and the meaning of his name, ‘the inert one’ — but everything that acts, acts upon or out of him. The creator gods of every temple city are alike ​in one thing: they stood on Nun’s surface and spoke.

The Ogdoad and the First Mound (Hermopolitan Cosmogony)

At Hermopolis the theology ran: four frog-headed males and four snake-headed females — ​Nun and Naunet, Huh and Hauhet, Kuk and Kauket, Amun and Amaunet — swam in the primordial water until their inertness ripened into an egg, or a lotus, or the first ‌mound. From that the sun rose. Nun heads the eight because water is first among the inert things.

The Word Upon the Waters (Memphite Theology)

The Shabaka Stone’s Memphite account has ​Ptah conceiving the world in the heart and speaking it on the primeval waters: creation as a command uttered over Nun. The theology made the water the page and the god’s tongue ‌the pen — the deepest Egyptian metaphor for creation as articulation.

The Sea That Holds the Barque (Solar Books)

In the underworld books the sun’s night barque sails the Nun beneath ​the world — the primeval water is also the water below, the source of renewal. At dawn the barque re-emerges from Nun’s eastern flood: every sunrise is a re-creation, every night ‌a return to the uncreated.

The Inundation as Epiphany (Flood Theology)

The Egyptians called the annual flood simply ‘the arrival’; its waters were Nun revisiting the world, refertilising the mound. Temple ​holy lakes and the ‘Ocean of the Temple’ pools staged the same theology in architecture: a miniature primeval water at the god’s door, in which purity and creation are the same act.

Across Cultures

Nun is the ‌substrate into which every creator-god’s theology sinks its roots: Atum self-created upon him, Amun hidden within him, Ptah speaking over him. Greek cosmologists drew the parallel with their own water-first doxographies — Thales’ ‘all is water’ found its Egyptian precedent in Nun.
