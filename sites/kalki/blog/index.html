Mythology

The Kalki myth is the Purāṇic eschaton: the prophecies of the Kali Yuga’s decay — ​merchants as kings, wealth as virtue, fathers feared by sons — are the diagnosis, and Kalki is the cure. He appears in the latest strata of the Mahābhārata and the Purāṇas ‌as the tenth and final avatāra, the one Vishnu keeps in reserve for the world’s last night.

The Prophecy of the Horse (Mahābhārata 3.191)

The earliest Kalki-passage names the signs: ​when time decays, a being of immense power will be born to a family of Brahmins, swift as thought, his hand a sword, his steed white as milk, blazing like ‌a comet. The passage is embedded in the epic’s cosmological books as Vishnu’s promise to the tormented earth.

The Full Biography (Kalki Purāṇa)

The eponymous Purāṇa tells it whole: the ​child Kalki, mocked by the corrupt, learns the Vedas from Paraśurāma himself, marries the princess Padmāvatī by the sword-contest, and rides out against the mleccha kings. His campaigns purify the ‌world; the wicked fall to his blade and the earth’s burden lifts at last.

The Diagnosis of the Age (Kali Yuga Doctrines)

The Purāṇic Kali-Yuga lists read like satire: rulers ​will be thieves, vows will be sold, the low will govern the high. Kalki is the answer to this audit — not a reformer but an ending. The theology is cyclical: ‌he destroys the age as a farmer burns the stubble, so the next golden age can be sown.

The Expected One (Living Traditions)

Kalki remains expected: in Śambhala-legend — Tibetan ​Buddhism adopted the hidden kingdom wholesale — and in popular Hinduism, where every generation finds the Kali Yuga’s signs fulfilled. He is the only god in this pantheon whose temple ​is the future, and whose devotees wait rather than celebrate.
