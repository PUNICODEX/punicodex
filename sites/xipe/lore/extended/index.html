The name Xipe reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the nahuatl tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Nahuatl — xipehua, 'to flay, to peel (a fruit or a man)' — the verb at the root of the god's name (Karttunen, An Analytical Dictionary of Nahuatl, s.v.)
  • Nahuatl — Tōtec, 'our lord' — the full cult name is Xipe Tōtec, 'Our Lord the Flayed One'
  • Solar title — Tlatlauhqui, 'the red one' — Xipe as 'Red Tezcatlipoca', lord of the west and patron of goldsmiths (Sahagún, Florentine Codex; Miller & Taube, s.v. Xipe Totec)

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Xipe in Later Traditions

The Dominican Diego de Durán, watching Tlacaxipehualiztli in the decades after the Conquest, was struck that the feast fell near Holy Week — the god who wore a flayed skin commemorated at the season when Christians mourned a god who wore a body and lost it. The friars suppressed the rite, yet its imagery proved durable: in the Puebla and Guerrero regions, masked and costumed dance confraternities — ancestors of today's concheros and Danza Azteca — carried the flayed lord's memory in transformed dress. Twentieth-century revival movements such as the Mexicayotl circle reclaimed Xipe Tōtec as a figure of indigenous identity and renewal rather than barbarism. Comparative religion places him among the world's 'dying vegetation' gods, but the Mexica theology was stranger and more precise: not a god who dies like a plant, but a god who peels like a seed.

Modern Legacy

Xipe Tōtec stands at the center of the modern debate over Aztec sacrifice — read by some as the theological heart of a sophisticated agricultural civilization, by others as evidence of state terror (Clendinnen, Townsend). His images anchor museum collections: the monolithic Xipe of the Templo Mayor Museum, wearing his knotted second skin, is among the most reproduced Aztec sculptures. Nahuatl lexicography (Karttunen, Lockhart) keeps his name etymologically alive, and Danza Azteca troupes across Mexico and the United States still perform in the spring season his festival once owned. For better and worse, the Flayed One is now the world's most recognizable emblem of Mexica religion.

In the Earth

The earliest material witness to the flayed-skin complex comes from the shaft tombs of West Mexico (Colima, Jalisco, Nayarit, c. 200 BCE–500 CE), whose ceramic figures wear the flayed skins of other men — proof the imagery predates the Aztec state by centuries. At Tenochtitlan, the INAH excavations of the Templo Mayor precinct (from 1978) located Xipe's shrine, Yopico, and its caches; the Templo Mayor Museum preserves a monolithic Xipe Tōtec whose back shows the wearer's own skin beneath the knotted cords of the second skin. The cult's heartland lay among the Yope of the Guerrero coast and in the Puebla region, and the screenfold Codex Borgia (c. 1500) gives the god's fullest painted dossier.

The Meditation

What Xipe means — beyond the dictionary

Xipe Tōtec asks the question every farmer knows and every city forgets: what must die for something else to live? His answer is a skin. The maize seed does not negotiate with its husk; it splits it. The god does not sympathize with the covering; he wears it. In a culture that now separates death from dinner by an almost perfectly opaque supply chain, the Flayed One insists on the connection: your bread was once a seed's corpse, your gold was once a stone's wound.

To meditate on Xipe is to meditate on the cost of renewal. He is not a god of resurrection — nothing in the rite promises the flayed man his life back. He is a god of transfer: life moves, and something is always left behind. The friars saw only cruelty; the Mexica saw the farming year. Both were reading the same skin.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Xipe, Spring, Agriculture, Flayed, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Xipe?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Xipe is /ˈʃi.pe/ — approximately 'SHE-peh' — both vowels are short, the stress falls on the first syllable, and the x is never the English 'ks' or 'z' sound.

02What does Xipe mean?

Xipe means The flayed one in the nahuatl tradition.

03What are the symbols of Xipe?

Xipe is associated with Flayed human skin (His defining attribute — a second skin knotted at the wrists and ankles, the visible claim that the old covering must die for new life to emerge.), Maize husk (The theological key to his cult: the seed cannot germinate until it sheds its sheath, as the god shed his skin to feed humanity.), Temalacatl (The round gladiator stone on which captive warriors fought bound during Tlacaxipehualiztli before being sacrificed in his honor.), Pointed cap (copilli) (The conical headgear of his image, marking lordship and linking him to the solar-warrior complex of the western sky.), Chicahuaztli (The rattle staff he carries in codex imagery, an instrument of fertility whose sound accompanies the ancestral dances.).

04Why restore Xipe in Unicode?

Xipe is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Xipe?

“The Lord Who Fed the World” — Xipe's mythology survives only in fragments: one late creation story, the great festival book of the Florentine Codex, and the uneasy testimony of Spanish friars. Pieced together, they reveal a god whose apparent cruelty is an agricultural theology — the flayed skin is the seed coat, and the death of the husk is the birth of the maize. The full telling lives on the lore page.
