Overview

Xipe (xipe) — Spring, Agriculture, Flayed · The flayed one — belongs to the Nahuatl tradition, where it is catalogued ​under the domain "Spring, Agriculture, Flayed". The name means "The flayed one".

Xipe — Xipe Tōtec, 'Our Lord the Flayed One' — is the Mexica god of spring, agriculture, and renewal whose priests ​wore the flayed skins of sacrificial captives for twenty days. The Lord Who Fed the World (The Flaying)

One late account, preserved in the colonial Histoyre ​du Mechique, tells that after the great flood the earth gave humanity nothing to eat, and the god Xipe Tōtec flayed himself so that people might be fed; for this reason his images ‌are always shown wearing a human skin. Diego de Durán, who watched the rite's last performances in sixteenth-century Mexico, recorded the same conviction: the god had given his own covering for the ​world's sustenance. The friars were witnessing an agricultural allegory they only half understood. A maize seed cannot sprout until it splits and sheds its husk; the Mexica made that everyday miracle into a ‌deity who wears the shed skin of a man. The myth teaches that feeding others is never costless: the renewal of the world is paid for with a skin.

The Festival of the ​Flaying (Tlacaxipehualiztli)

The Florentine Codex (Book 2) and Durán describe the great feast of the second veintena, roughly February–March: captives taken in war were dedicated at Yopico, the flayed lord's shrine in the ‌Templo Mayor precinct; bound to the round temalacatl stone, they fought gladiatorial combats against seasoned warriors, then were stretched across the sacrificial stone. The priests then flayed the victims and wore their skins ​for twenty days, knotted at the wrists and ankles, visiting houses and receiving offerings as living images of the god, until the dried skins were laid away. Sahagún's informants named them tototectin, 'the ‌flayed ones.' The rite horrified the friars and obsesses modern readers, but its logic was exact: at the start of the farming year the city reenacted the first flaying, so that what was ​done to the god might be done to the seed. Shed skin, shed husk, new season.

The Red Lord of the West (Goldsmiths)

In the theological geography recorded from Sahagún's informants, Xipe Tōtec ‌was also Tlatlauhqui, 'the red one' — the Red Tezcatlipoca, lord of the west, the quarter of the setting sun and of the earth's buried treasures. During Tlacaxipehualiztli the goldsmiths held their own ​feast, for Xipe was patron of their craft: gold, the Nahuatl-speakers called teocuitlatl, 'divine excrement', the sun's deposit in the earth's gut. The same festival that flayed a man to renew the fields ​also honored the men who pulled shining metal from the ground. Modern scholarship (Miller & Taube) has long noted the coherence: metal and maize are both riches that must be stripped out of ‌a dead covering — ore from rock, grain from husk, flesh from skin. The red lord of the west governs them all.

A Meditation

Xipe Tōtec asks the question every farmer knows and every city forgets: what must die for something else to live? His answer is a skin. The maize ‌seed does not negotiate with its husk; it splits it. The god does not sympathize with the covering; he wears it. In a culture that now separates death from dinner by an almost ​perfectly opaque supply chain, the Flayed One insists on the connection: your bread was once a seed's corpse, your gold was once a stone's wound.

To meditate on Xipe is to meditate on ‌the cost of renewal. He is not a god of resurrection — nothing in the rite promises the flayed man his life back. He is a god of transfer: life moves, and something ​is always left behind. The friars saw only cruelty; the Mexica saw the farming year. Both were reading the same skin.
