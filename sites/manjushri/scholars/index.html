01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Mañjuśrī (manjushri) — Wisdom, Intellect · The Gentle Glory, Sword of Wisdom — is the bodhisattva of transcendent wisdom (prajñā): the ever-youthful prince of the Mahāyāna who wields the flaming sword that cuts through ignorance and carries the book of the Perfection of Wisdom on his lotus.[1]

His name joins mañju, ‘gentle, beautiful, charming’, with śrī, ‘glory, splendour, majesty’ — ‘Gentle Glory’. The tradition hears the softness as the point: true wisdom is not harsh, and the sword that ends confusion is wielded by a boy. He is the bodhisattva who dares what others will not — in the Vimalakīrtinirdeśa he alone accepts the call to visit the sick layman — and the patron of every student, scholar, and debater in the Buddhist world, from the academies of Nālandā to the examination halls of Tibet.[2]

PuniCodex restores the name as Mañjuśrī and serves its temple at mañjuśrī.com. The restoration keeps the palatal ñ, the palatal ś, and the long final ī of the Sanskrit; the plain ASCII manjushri is a modern convenience of the early domain-name system, not an attested ancient spelling.[3]

Sources

  2. Watson, Burton, trans., The Vimalakirti Sutra (New York: Columbia University Press, 1997).
  3. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. mañju, śrī.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name is attested in Sanskrit as मञ्जुश्री (IAST mañjuśrī): a compound of mañju, ‘gentle, sweet, lovely’, and śrī, ‘glory, splendour’ — ‘Gentle Glory’ or ‘Sweet Majesty’.[1] His epithets extend the music: Mañjughoṣa, ‘Gentle Voice’ (or ‘Beautiful Sound’); Kumārabhūta, ‘the Ever-Youthful’, for he is always figured as a prince of sixteen; and Vāgīśvara, ‘Lord of Speech’, the orators' bodhisattva.

The ASCII form manjushri survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling, and it flattens three distinctive letters — the palatal nasal ñ, the palatal sibilant ś, and the long ī — into approximations. The Unicode restoration Mañjuśrī recovers all three directly in the address bar. Because the restoration preserves distinctive letters and vowel length the ASCII form loses, the name is classified Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • m → M — Same, capitalized
  • a → a — Same
  • n → ñ — Palatal nasal ñ (as in Spanish mañana)
  • j → j — Same
  • u → u — Same
  • s → ś — Palatal sibilant ś
  • h → — — Dropped (English digraph, not Sanskrit)
  • r → r — Same
  • i → ī — Long vowel ī

The project holds the domain mañjuśrī.com (xn--majur-pta32bwm.com) as the canonical home of this name.

Sources

  1. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. mañju, śrī.
  2. Edgerton, Franklin, Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit Grammar and Dictionary, vol. 2 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1953), s.v. Mañjuśrī, Mañjughoṣa.
03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /məɲ.ˈdʑu.ɕriː/ — Sanskrit Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ma- — Bilabial [m] with short [ə]
  • -ñju- — Palatal nasal [ɲ] (the sound of Spanish ñ) with voiced palatal stop [dʑ] and short [u]; the ‘gentle’ of the name
  • -śrī — Palatal sibilant [ɕ] with trilled [r] and long close [iː]; the ‘glory’, its vowel held long

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: ‘mun-JOO-shree’ — the ‘nj’ soft as in onion, the final ‘ee’ sustained.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Sanskrit — मञ्जुश्री (mañjuśrī), ‘Gentle Glory’; also Mañjughoṣa, ‘Gentle Voice’
  • Chinese — 文殊師利 (Wénshūshīlì), abbreviated 文殊 (Wénshū)
  • Japanese — Monju (Monju Bosatsu)
  • Tibetan — 'Jam dpal (Jampel), ‘Gentle Splendour’; 'Jam dbyangs (Jamyang), ‘Gentle Voice’

Mañjuśrī is Tier 1: ñ, ś, and ī are all distinctive marks the ASCII alphabet cannot write.

Sources

  1. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. mañjuśrī.
04

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name is preserved in Devanagari as मञ्जुश्री — Brahmic abugida, attested Buddhist Sanskrit, in the sūtra manuscripts of India and Nepal; the script is written left-to-right.[1]

The scholarly transliteration is Mañjuśrī (IAST), giving the normalized reading /məɲ.ˈdʑu.ɕriː/.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • Sanskrit Mañjuśrī is written मञ्जुश्री in Devanagari: मञ्जु (mañju) + श्री (śrī); the conjunct ञ्ज writes the palatal cluster ñj, and श्री is the ligature śrī.
  • The compound is mañju (‘gentle, beautiful’) + śrī (‘glory, splendour’): ‘Gentle Glory’.
  • IAST distinguishes the palatal nasal ñ from dental n, the palatal sibilant ś from retroflex ṣ and dental s, and marks the long final ī with the macron.
  • The Unicode restoration Mañjuśrī is registrable in .com; the Devanagari form is not supported in the .com IDN table.[2]

Sources

  1. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. mañjuśrī.
  2. Edgerton, Franklin, Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit Dictionary (Yale University Press, 1953), s.v. Mañjuśrī.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Mañjuśrī is wisdom personified: not accumulated knowledge but prajñā, the seeing that cuts things loose from illusion.[1]

Transcendent Wisdom

He embodies the prajñā of the Perfection of Wisdom literature — the insight into emptiness that the sūtras call the mother of all Buddhas.

The Sword of Discrimination

His blade severs ignorance at the root: analysis, debate, and the courage to cut through one's own positions.

The Ever-Youthful

Kumārabhūta: wisdom as perpetual beginner's mind, a prince who never hardens into a sage.

Patron of Scholars

Students, monks, and debaters across Asia invoke him before study and examination; the great universities of Buddhist India counted him their patron.[2]

Sources

  2. Lopez, Donald S., ed., Buddhism in Practice (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1995).
06

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography of Mañjuśrī is the complete vocabulary of intellect:[1]

  • The flaming sword (khadga) — Raised in the right hand, wreathed in fire: the blade of prajñā that cuts ignorance and the flames that burn its roots
  • The book on the lotus — The Perfection of Wisdom sūtra (Prajñāpāramitā) resting on a blue utpala lotus in his left hand: the text as flower, wisdom as something that blooms
  • The youthful prince — Sixteen forever: princely ornaments, no monastic robes — insight is not a retirement
  • The lion mount — In many forms he rides a lion: the roar of the true teaching, fearlessness in debate
  • The syllable a — His seed-syllable and the first letter of the Arapacana syllabary, the alphabet taught as a meditation on emptiness; his mantra is oṃ a ra pa ca na dhīḥ[2]

Sources

  1. Getty, Alice, The Gods of Northern Buddhism (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1914).
  2. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Mañjuśrī’ and ‘Arapacana’.
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Mañjuśrī's mythology is a cycle of encounters in which wisdom proves itself by daring.[1]

The Visit to Vimalakīrti (Vimalakīrtinirdeśa)

When the layman Vimalakīrti falls ill and the Buddha asks his great disciples and bodhisattvas to call on him, one after another declines — each has been bested by the layman before. Only Mañjuśrī accepts. Their dialogue is the sūtra's summit: on nonduality, on emptiness, on the bodhisattva's work in the world of passion — and it ends with Vimalakīrti's thunderous silence, which Mañjuśrī alone is quick enough to praise.[2]

The Nāga Princess (Lotus Sūtra 12)

In the Lotus Sūtra's Devadatta chapter, Mañjuśrī emerges from the nāga palace beneath the sea and stuns the assembly by testifying that an eight-year-old nāga girl has attained full awakening — wisdom's declaration that enlightenment is no one's monopoly, not even humanity's.[3]

The Director of the Pilgrimage (Gaṇḍavyūha)

In the Avataṃsaka's closing book it is Mañjuśrī who sends the young Sudhana on his pilgrimage to fifty-three teachers — the bodhisattva of wisdom as the dispatcher of every genuine search.[4]

The Lake of Kathmandu (Svayambhū Purāṇa)

Nepal's own chronicle makes him the valley's founder: Mañjuśrī came from his mountain Wutai to venerate the self-existent flame at the lake of Nāgavāsa, saw that the waters imprisoned the shrine, and with one stroke of his sword cut the gorge at Chobar, draining the lake and opening the Kathmandu valley to settlement. Nepal's geography is, in its own telling, his handiwork.[5]

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Mañjuśrī's receptions are the history of Buddhist scholarship itself. In China he became Wénshū, and Mount Wutai in Shanxi — five flat peaks, cold and clear — was identified as his earthly abode, becoming for over a millennium the greatest pilgrimage mountain of East Asia, visited by monks of every nation, including the Japanese pilgrim Ennin, whose ninth-century diary is a classic of the road.[1] In Japan he is Monju, the wisdom of the Zen hall; in Tibet, Jampel and Jamyang, the personal deity of scholars — Tsongkhapa, founder of the Gelug school, was revered as his emanation.[2]

Within the PuniCodex cross-tradition index, kindred figures include Vajrapāṇi and Guānyīn (Avalokiteśvara) — with whom he forms the classic triad of the ‘Lords of the Three Families’: wisdom, power, and compassion as the bodhisattva's complete equipment.

Sources

  1. Reischauer, Edwin O., trans., Ennin's Diary: The Record of a Pilgrimage to China in Search of the Law (New York: Ronald Press, 1955).
  2. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Mañjuśrī’, ‘Wutai Shan’, ‘Tsongkhapa’.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Mañjuśrī is the patron saint of every Buddhist student. His mantra oṃ a ra pa ca na dhīḥ is recited before examinations from Kathmandu to Kyoto; the Arapacana syllabary he anchors — an alphabet whose letters are meditations — made him the Buddha of literacy itself.[1] Mount Wutai, his earthly seat, remains a living pilgrimage city and a UNESCO World Heritage landscape (2009), its temples — among them the Foguang Temple's east hall of 857, the oldest surviving timber hall in China — monuments to a millennium and a half of devotion.[2]

His image set the iconography of intellect for half the world: the sword and the book are so completely his that a bodhisattva bearing them is identifiable across a thousand years of art without an inscription.

Sources

  1. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Arapacana’, ‘Mañjuśrī’.
  2. UNESCO, Mount Wutai, World Heritage List no. 1279 (inscribed 2009). ↗

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Mañjuśrī's material record begins with the sūtra transmission itself and blossoms in the art of the mountain that claimed him. Early images — a princely figure with sword and book — appear in the art of late Kuṣāṇa and Gupta India and multiply in the Pāla-period bronzes of Nālandā, the very university whose scholars made him their patron.[1]

Mount Wutai is the great monument: the Avataṃsaka tradition's identification of its five terraces with the bodhisattva's ‘Clear-and-Cool Mountain’ made the range a sacred geography, and its surviving temples — Foguang si's east hall (857 CE), the oldest dated timber structure in China, and Nanchan si (782 CE), older still — preserve Tang architecture in situ. Ennin's diary (838–847) describes the mountain at the height of its international fame.[2] Nepalese art keeps his other legend: the sword that cut the Chobar gorge is the valley's founding artifact, and Svayambhū's hilltop stūpa marks the flame he came to worship.

Sources

  1. Huntington, Susan L., The ‘Pāla-Sena’ Schools of Sculpture (Leiden: Brill, 1984).
  2. Reischauer, Edwin O., trans., Ennin's Diary (Ronald Press, 1955); UNESCO, Mount Wutai (2009). ↗

Pāli Canon

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Mañjuśrī is absent from the Pāli Canon. The Tipiṭaka knows no bodhisattva of this name; its ideal figures are the arahants and the Buddha-to-be of the Jātakas, not the celestial bodhisattvas of the Mahāyāna. As with Amitābha and Akṣobhya, the absence is a fact of canon and lineage: the Pāli tradition closed around the early discourses, while Mañjuśrī rose with the Perfection of Wisdom literature in the centuries around the turn of the era.[1]

What the canon does hold is his substance: the analysis of prajñā/paññā as the path's crowning factor and the lion's-roar ideal of fearless teaching are Pāli materials that the Mahāyāna personified in the gentle prince.[2]

Sources

  1. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Mañjuśrī’.
  2. Edgerton, Franklin, Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit Dictionary (Yale University Press, 1953), s.v. Mañjuśrī.

Mahāyāna Sūtras

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Mañjuśrī is the most ubiquitous bodhisattva in the Mahāyāna sūtra literature. The Perfection of Wisdom texts make him prajñā's personal form — the Aṣṭasāhasrikā and its descendants stage him as wisdom's interlocutor; the Vimalakīrtinirdeśa gives him the greatest dialogue of the canon, the sickbed visit (ch. 5); the Saddharmapuṇḍarīka opens with his exchange with Maitreya on the Buddha's ray of light and closes its twelfth chapter with his testimony of the awakened nāga princess.[1]

The Avataṃsaka cycle casts him as the dispatcher of Sudhana's pilgrimage in the Gaṇḍavyūha; the Śūraṅgamasamādhi Sūtra takes his name for its hero's own; and the tantric Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti — ‘Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī’ — hymns him in a hundred and sixty verses as the treasury of every epithet of wisdom, becoming in Tibet the most recited liturgy of all.[2]

Sources

  1. Watson, Burton, trans., The Vimalakirti Sutra (Columbia University Press, 1997); Kern, H., trans., Saddharma-Puṇḍarīka (1884), ch. 1, 12.
  2. Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti; Cleary, Thomas, trans., The Flower Ornament Scripture (Shambhala, 1993), Gaṇḍavyūha.

Commentarial Tradition

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The commentarial crown of his tradition is the Nāmasaṃgīti exegesis: Indian masters — Vilāsavajra foremost among them — commented ‘Chanting the Names of Mañjuśrī’ as the root text of the wisdom tantras, and the work's transmission to Tibet made it the daily liturgy of the monastic colleges, where its verses are memorized before debate begins.[1] The Arapacana syllabary's commentaries read the alphabet itself as a curriculum of emptiness — the letter a as the door of the unborn, the first teaching and the last.

East Asian exegesis made Wutai's five terraces a commentary in geography: the Avataṃsaka tradition's gloss of ‘Clear-and-Cool Mountain’ fixed the pilgrimage theology that Ennin recorded and the Tang temples still attest. In Nepal, the Svayambhū Purāṇa's narrative of the sword and the lake functions as the valley's own exegetical chronicle — a land reading its founding as an act of prajñā.[2]

Sources

  1. Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti with the commentary tradition (Vilāsavajra).
  2. Reischauer, Edwin O., trans., Ennin's Diary (Ronald Press, 1955); Svayambhū Purāṇa.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Mañjuśrī is Buddhism's answer to a fear every thoughtful person knows: that thinking itself is the problem. The tradition could have made wisdom a grey elder; it made her a sixteen-year-old with a sword. The youth is the doctrine — insight does not arrive with age and does not exhaust itself in caution; it stays, in the best minds, permanently quick.

The sword and the book are the whole curriculum. The book says: learn everything. The sword says: hold none of it. Students across Asia still begin their year with his mantra, and the petition hidden in a ra pa ca na is the scholar's honest prayer: let me know a great deal, and let none of it make me stupid.

Sources

  1. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Mañjuśrī’.
