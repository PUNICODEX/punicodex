The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

राधा

The name in its original Sanskrit form. Rādhā (राधा) is attested in the source tradition — “N. of the 21st Nakṣatra Viśākhā (containing 4 stars in the form of a curve supposed to be α, ι, ν Librae, and γ Scorpionis, cf. nakzatra), L.”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

radha

Reduced to plain radha, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Rādhā

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Rādhā restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Rādhā.com → xn--rdh-1oac.com

The non-ASCII characters in Rādhā are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Rādhā.

Pronunciation

How Rādhā was spoken

/ˈrɑː.d̪ʱɑː/ Sanskrit Reconstruction

Modern Approximation

RAH-dhah — both a's are long; the dh is a single aspirated sound, not two letters.

Etymological Kin

  • Sanskrit राधा (rādhā), "prosperity, success" — from √rādh
  • Braj vocative Rādhe — the cry with which all Braj greets her
  • Astronomical namesake Viśākhā, the nakshatra MW lists under her name
  • Owned form Rādhā

The Accent / Script Rule

Sanskrit Rādhā derives from √rādh, "to prosper, accomplish, propitiate" — Monier-Williams glosses the word as prosperity and success, and records her celestial namesake: the nakshatra Viśākhā is also called Rādhā. The restoration Rādhā keeps both long vowels exactly where the Sanskrit puts them; the ASCII "Radha" flattens the two syllables the tradition holds long. Tier-1 by rule: two macrons preserved.

The Beloved of Vraja

The Rāsa Dance, the Gītagovinda, and the Theology of Longing

Rādhā is the chief of the gopīs and the supreme beloved of Krishna: the village girl of Vraja whom the poets and the theologians raised, century by century, until she stood as goddess beside — and, for her devotees, above — the god she loves.

The Rāsa Circle

The autumn full-moon dance of the Bhāgavata's tenth book — Krishna multiplied among the gopīs.

The Gītagovinda

Jayadeva's twelfth-century song-cycle — the poem that taught India to sing her name beside Mādhava's.

Rādhā-kuṇḍa

Her bathing-pool below Govardhan — for the Gauḍīyas the most sacred water on earth.

Varṣāṇā (Barsana)

Her village on the ridge — the hilltop temples where Holi is played in her honor every spring.

Sacred Symbols

The golden lotus Her complexion and her purity: gaurī, the golden one, the lotus of the Yamunā's bank
The kadamba tryst-tree The meeting-tree of the riverbank, witness of the appointed nights
The cry "Rādhe" The vocative that outranks every title: in Braj her name is itself a blessing
05

Mythology

Stories of Rādhā

Rādhā's mythology is the rarest kind: a figure the early texts leave unnamed, whom poetry and devotion then build, line by line, into the measure of divine love itself.

Bhāgavata Purāṇa

The Unnamed Beloved

The Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells the rāsa-līlā across five chapters of its tenth book — the autumn full-moon night on the Yamunā's bank, Krishna's flute emptying the village, the dance in which he multiplies himself among the gopīs — and never once speaks her name. Yet one verse (10.30.28) says of one gopī, anayārādhito nūnaṃ bhagavān hariḥ: "surely the Lord was perfectly worshipped by her." The great commentators hear the name hidden in the verb: ārādhitaḥ, worshipped — Rādhā. Her devotees have ever since read the whole book as her portrait, unsigned.

Gītagovinda

The Poem That Crowned Her

In the twelfth century Jayadeva wrote the Gītagovinda, and the unsigned portrait got its signature. His aṣṭapadīs — songs in eights — stage the whole drama of separation and union: the go-between urging Rādhā through the spring night to the tryst, Krishna begging her foot on his head, the refrain rādhā-mādhavayor jayanti, "victory to Rādhā and Mādhava." The poem was taken into temple liturgy — it is sung before Jagannātha at Puri to this day — and Rādhā's name became inseparable from the god's.

Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa

From Beloved to Goddess

The later purāṇas complete the ascent. The Brahma-vaivarta Purāṇa makes Rādhā the prakṛti itself — the primal nature from whom all goddesses proceed, Krishna's own other half, without whom he is not merely lonely but incomplete. What the Bhāgavata hinted and Jayadeva sang, the sectarian texts systematized: the cowherd girl of Vraja is the very power by which the divine loves, and being loved is shown to be a divine attribute, not a divine accident.

Caitanya-caritāmṛta

The Mood the Lord Borrowed

The Gauḍīya theologians gave the ascent its final form: Rādhā is Krishna's hlādinī-śakti, his bliss-potency, and her love is the one thing in existence he does not already possess. Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja's Caitanya-caritāmṛta explains the Golden Avatar by her: Krishna, curious what she feels in loving him, takes her mood and her golden complexion and is born as Caitanya. Devotion does not merely reach God here; God is defined by the reach of devotion. Her festival, Rādhāṣṭamī, falls on the eighth of Bhādrapada's bright fortnight, fifteen days after his birthday — her own day, in her own right.

Extended Lore

Rādhā is the proof that devotion is not a means but an end: she does not worship in order to obtain, she loves — and the tradition watched that love until it concluded that God must be made of the same substance. Every act of care done without calculation walks in her rāsa circle. Her question to the powerful is her question to Krishna: what is all your power for, if not this?
