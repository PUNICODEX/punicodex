Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script 月読 Tsukuyomi — "Moon reader"
Unicode Restoration Tsukuyomi Canonical scholarly spelling — ASCII-identical
Modern ASCII tsukuyomi Plain-ASCII fallback

The name Tsukuyomi reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the japanese tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Tsukuyomi in Later Traditions

Tsukuyomi was drawn early into the Buddhist astronomy that mapped the pantheon onto the heavens: in esoteric mandalas the lunar deity appears among the devas as a moon-disc (gachirin), and medieval Shinto-Buddhist thought quietly read the moon kami as Dainichi's reflected light — the moon as borrowed radiance, a natural metaphor for compassion that mirrors its source. In honji-suijaku terms he attracted no major buddha of his own, and his cult shrank as Amaterasu's and Susanoo's grew. His afterlife is more literary than liturgical: the moon of the Man'yōshū and the Kokinshū poems is Tsukuyomi's territory whether named or not.

Modern Legacy

The court's tsukimi moon-viewing banquets — still kept at Ise, at shrines, and in every garden with a patch of susuki grass — descend from rites addressed to the moon's power over the harvest calendar. In modern Japan he has been reinvented as the brooding nocturnal counterweight to solar Amaterasu, appearing in anime, manga, and games as the name of illusions and dream-techniques that trap their victims in a private night. Every autumn the full moon of the harvest festival hangs over rooftops exactly where the myth put him: alone, luminous, and apart.

In the Earth

Material evidence for Tsukuyomi is correspondingly scarce. His chief sanctuary, the Tsukiyomi-no-miya, stands among the subsidiary shrines of Ise and is renewed with the Grand Shrine's twenty-year rebuilding cycle; a mirror is enshrined there as his body. Heian court records document monthly tsukimi rites — offerings of dumplings, susuki grass, and sake to the harvest moon — and the Engishiki lists him among the kami receiving state offerings. Beyond Ise, independent shrines to Tsukuyomi are rare, which historians of religion read as the material trace of his thin mythology: a deity with no narrative cycle attracted no pilgrimage geography.

The Meditation

What Tsukuyomi means — beyond the dictionary

Tsukuyomi is the study of how little a god needs. No temple empire, no epic, no miracles — one birth, one terrible act, one sentence of exile, and the sky is arranged. Perhaps that is why the tradition did not embroider him: the moon asks for no stories, because everyone looks up and supplies their own.

His single myth is a parable about discernment gone rigid — a purity that cannot bear the body's generosity, and so murders the goddess who feeds the world. The moonlit nights that follow are beautiful and infertile. To live wholly by his light is to starve in radiance; to live wholly by the sun is to know nothing of the night. The myth of the estranged siblings keeps both halves of the sky in one household — divided, but never divorced.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Tsukuyomi, Moon God, Shintō, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Tsukuyomi?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Tsukuyomi is /tsɯ.kɯ.jo.mi/ — approximately 'TSOO-koo-yoh-mee' — the initial 'ts' is crisp, and the final vowel is clear.

02What does Tsukuyomi mean?

Tsukuyomi means Moon reader in the japanese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Tsukuyomi?

Tsukuyomi is associated with Moon (The luminous body he rules; in court poetry the moon outshines every other image of the sky), Night (The dark half of the cosmic day; his estrangement from the sun fixed its boundary), Blade (The weapon with which he answered Uke-mochi's feast — purity's fatal fastidiousness), Crescent (Waxing and waning; the counting of months that his name may encode), The five grains (Rice, millet, beans, wheat, and soy born from Uke-mochi's slain body — food that his night can never ripen).

04Why restore Tsukuyomi in Unicode?

Tsukuyomi is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Tsukuyomi?

“From the Right Eye of Izanagi” — Tsukuyomi's mythology is the shortest dossier of the three celestial siblings, and the chronicles themselves treat him as an afterthought. What survives is one birth, one killing, and one eternal estrangement — enough to divide the sky. The full telling lives on the lore page.
