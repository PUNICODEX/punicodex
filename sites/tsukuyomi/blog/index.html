Overview

Mythology

Tsukuyomi's mythology is the shortest dossier of the three celestial siblings, and ​the chronicles themselves treat him as an afterthought. What survives is one birth, one killing, and one eternal estrangement — enough to divide the sky.

From the Right Eye of Izanagi (The Birth)

​After his escape from Yomi, Izanagi washed away the pollution of the underworld, and the three great sovereign deities came from his face: Amaterasu from the left eye, Tsukuyomi from the right eye, ​and Susanoo from the nose (Kojiki 1). Izanagi appointed Amaterasu to rule the day and Tsukuyomi the night, dividing the luminous and dark halves of the world between the siblings. Where Amaterasu received ​her father's necklace and the highest heaven, Tsukuyomi received no emblem — a first hint of how thin his dossier would remain. His name joins tsuku, the moon, with yomi, read as 'night' ‌or as 'counting,' the deity who numbers the months. The myth establishes the moon as the sun's twin: born in the same instant of purification, ruling the complementary half of the sky.

​The Feast of Uke-mochi (The Killing)

Amaterasu heard of a wondrous goddess of food, Uke-mochi, and sent Tsukuyomi to look upon her. Uke-mochi turned toward the land and from her mouth and body ‌produced a banquet — boiled rice, millet, and every grain (Kojiki 1). Tsukuyomi was revolted: a meal that issued from a body was pollution itself, and he drew his blade and killed her. ​Amaterasu's anger was immediate and eternal — she declared him an evil god she would never again face, and from that moment the sun and moon kept separate courses. From Uke-mochi's slain body ‌the useful world sprang: silkworms from her head, rice from her eyes, millet from her ears, beans from her nose, wheat and soy from her lower body (Nihon Shoki 1). The myth weighs ​purity against fecundity: the moon's fastidious blade cuts the night off from the very source of food.

Why Sun and Moon Never Meet (The Separation)

The Kojiki gives no further adventures of Tsukuyomi ‌— his entire narrative after the killing is one sentence of divine estrangement. Amaterasu, as sovereign of the day, pronounced the sentence; the moon god departed, and the chronicle lets him go without ​a shrine, a child, or another deed. Later readers noticed the astronomy already inside the story: the moon that strikes down the goddess of food is the moon that rules the night in ‌which nothing grows; the sun that refuses to see him is the sun under whose gaze the five grains ripened from Uke-mochi's body. The separation is thus both an eternal feud and a ​daily rotation — estrangement written across the sky as the alternation of day and night, the celestial siblings meeting only at the thresholds of dawn and dusk, and never face to face.

​A God at the Edge of the Record (The Variants)

The compilers of the Nihon Shoki reproduce several variant accounts of the celestial birth, and their disagreements are themselves informative: the recensions differ ‌in the occasion of the washing and in small details of order, and one tradition lets Tsukuyomi's name be read as 'moon-counter,' the deity who numbers the months; the Kojiki grants him the ‌grand style Tsukuyomi-no-mikoto, 'His Augustness Moon-Night,' and then never uses it again. No variant gives him a further myth. The scholarly conclusion is sober: unlike his siblings, Tsukuyomi attracted no cycle of tales ‌— perhaps, as some have argued, because the moon's waxing and waning were read through other divinities in the popular cults. His faintness in the record is the most distinctive thing about him: ​the god of the night keeps to the edges of the text, exactly as the moon keeps to the edges of the day.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Tsukuyomi concentrates in ​a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Moon — The luminous body he rules; in court poetry the moon outshines every other image of the sky
  • Night ‌— The dark half of the cosmic day; his estrangement from the sun fixed its boundary
  • Blade — The weapon with which he answered Uke-mochi's feast — purity's fatal fastidiousness
  • Crescent — Waxing ​and waning; the counting of months that his name may encode
  • The five grains — Rice, millet, beans, wheat, and soy born from Uke-mochi's slain body — food that his night can never ​ripen

Archaeology & Evidence

Material evidence for Tsukuyomi is correspondingly scarce. His chief sanctuary, the Tsukiyomi-no-miya, stands among the subsidiary shrines of Ise and is renewed with the Grand Shrine's twenty-year rebuilding ‌cycle; a mirror is enshrined there as his body. Heian court records document monthly tsukimi rites — offerings of dumplings, susuki grass, and sake to the harvest moon — and the Engishiki lists ​him among the kami receiving state offerings. Beyond Ise, independent shrines to Tsukuyomi are rare, which historians of religion read as the material trace of his thin mythology: a deity with no narrative ​cycle attracted no pilgrimage geography.

Across Cultures

Tsukuyomi was drawn early into the Buddhist astronomy that mapped the pantheon onto the heavens: in ​esoteric mandalas the lunar deity appears among the devas as a moon-disc (gachirin), and medieval Shinto-Buddhist thought quietly read the moon kami as Dainichi's reflected light — the moon as borrowed radiance, a ​natural metaphor for compassion that mirrors its source. In honji-suijaku terms he attracted no major buddha of his own, and his cult shrank as Amaterasu's and Susanoo's grew. His afterlife is more ​literary than liturgical: the moon of the Man'yōshū and the Kokinshū poems is Tsukuyomi's territory whether named or not.

Cultural Legacy

The court's tsukimi moon-viewing banquets — still kept at Ise, at ‌shrines, and in every garden with a patch of susuki grass — descend from rites addressed to the moon's power over the harvest calendar. In modern Japan he has been reinvented as the ‌brooding nocturnal counterweight to solar Amaterasu, appearing in anime, manga, and games as the name of illusions and dream-techniques that trap their victims in a private night. Every autumn the full moon of ​the harvest festival hangs over rooftops exactly where the myth put him: alone, luminous, and apart.

A Meditation

Tsukuyomi is the study of how little a god needs. No temple empire, no epic, no miracles — one birth, one terrible ​act, one sentence of exile, and the sky is arranged. Perhaps that is why the tradition did not embroider him: the moon asks for no stories, because everyone looks up and supplies their ‌own.

His single myth is a parable about discernment gone rigid — a purity that cannot bear the body's generosity, and so murders the goddess who feeds the world. The moonlit nights ‌that follow are beautiful and infertile. To live wholly by his light is to starve in radiance; to live wholly by the sun is to know nothing of the night. The myth of ‌the estranged siblings keeps both halves of the sky in one household — divided, but never divorced.
