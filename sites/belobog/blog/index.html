Zeloboch of the Czech ​Chronicle (The Chronicle Line)

The only medieval author to name Belobog is Cosmas of Prague, whose Chronica Boemorum (c. 1125, Book I.4) describes the pre-Christian Czechs sacrificing to demons — and lists among ​them Zeloboch, the chronicler's Latinized rendering of Belobog, alongside gods of the sky, the harvest, and fortune. The sentence is a sneer from a Christian cleric, and its spelling is garbled; yet it ‌proves that around 1100 a 'White God' received offerings in Bohemia. Cosmas is careful to mock the whole catalogue — sky, harvest, and fortune gods jumbled together — which is exactly why scholars mine ​his scorn for the only list of Czech deities that survives. But the attestation does real work: it converts Belobog from a scholarly inference ‌into a historical god, however thinly witnessed — one line carrying the whole bright half of Slavic dualism.

Chernobog at Rethra (The Black God)

Belobog's structure is completed by his opposite. Helmold of ​Bosau (Chronica Slavorum I.52, c. 1172) reports that the Slavs acknowledged a god of all evil 'whom they call the Black One' — Chernobog — and modern scholarship assembles the pair: White God ‌and Black God, light and evil, the Slavic dualism (Gieysztor, Mitologia Słowian; Rosik, Białobóg). Later folklore strengthens the pairing: across the Slavic world the year's good and ill fortune are personified as opposed ​powers, and the White God's wreath is the benign half of that old bookkeeping. Historians rightly cautionHistorians rightly caution that the two attestations come from different peoples, centuries, and authors — no text ‌shows the pair worshiped together. The dualism is thus partly reconstruction. Still, the reconstruction rests on two independent medieval witnesses: wherever the sources reach the Slavic gods, they find darkness named — and ​naming darkness implies, at least to us, its bright counterpart.

Belobog in the Last Sheaf (The Kept Wreath)

Slavic harvest folklore preserved the god's name as a thing. Across Poland and Russia, the ‌final sheaf of the harvest was shaped into a wreath called belobog ('white god') and kept in the house — above the door, by the icons, or in the granary — until the ​spring sowing, when its grain went back into the field to carry the year's fortune forward (records collected in nineteenth-century ethnography; discussed by Gieysztor and Rosik). Ethnographers found the wreath hung beside the ​household icons — a quiet index of how long a pagan guest can lodge in a Christian home without ever being named aloud. Whatever his theology, Belobog lived in the rafters of Slavic farmhouses within living memory of the ‌ethnographers.
