The Authentic Names

Why hádes.com and Hádēs.com are both correct forms

Original Script

Ἅιδης

The name in its original Greek form, with rough breathing and the long vowel a (alpha) marked by the macron in our Tier-2 system. But here, in Tier-1, the acute on the á declares him: not invisible, but unwilling to be seen. The accent is defiance.

ASCII Constraint

HADES

Reduced to five capital letters. A Disney villain. A video game protagonist. A word children shout on playgrounds. The Lord of the Unseen — the god whose very name Greeks feared to speak — became a cartoon. The acute accent was not decoration. It was protection.

Unicode Restoration

hádes · hades

Tier-1 preserves the acute on á — the pitch accent that strikes like a command. Tier-2 strips the accent but keeps the macron on e. Both are authentic scholarly orthographies. Oxford prefers one. Cambridge prefers the other. The PUNYCODEX owns both. Neither is a compromise. Each is a different face of the same unseen king.

Punycode Encoding
hádēs.com → xn--hds-ela5w.com

Both forms encode their non-ASCII characters — Tier-1 with á (U+00E1) and e (U+0113), Tier-2 with e (U+0113) alone. To the DNS, they are different Punycode strings. To humanity, they are both Hádes.

Tier Classification

Where Hádes stands in the PUNYCODEX elegant tier system

Tier 1
hádes.com

Hádes carries the acute on the á and the macron on the e. It satisfies the dual-mark criterion exactly: acute plus macron, stress plus length. Under the elegant tier system, this is Tier 1.

The Greek original Ἅιδης contains both features — the rising pitch on the first syllable and the long eta that extends the name into the underworld itself. Hádes does not flatten. It carries the full scholarly orthography. Tier 1 is not an opinion. It is a measurement.

02

Pronunciation

How the Unseen was truly spoken

/hái.dɛːs/ Attic Greek Reconstruction
há- Rough breathing with the acute stress on the alpha. The hai is a diphthong — start with [h], open into [a], glide toward [i]. The acute raises the pitch sharply on this syllable, like a challenge.
-dɛː- Long epsilon, held. The de is not a diphthong but a single long vowel with a following consonant. It is the sound of something being pulled — a name that drags the listener downward.
-s Final voiceless sibilant. In Greek, this ending was common for masculine names. Here, it cuts off like a door closing. The name does not trail. It ends.

Modern Approximation

"HAH-days" — with the first syllable sharply stressed and the second held long. The a is broad, like the "a" in "father" but pitched higher. The e is the "e" in "air," stretched.

Etymological Truth

  • ἀ- (a-) — "not, without"
  • ἰδεῖν (ideîn) — "to see"
  • Meaning — "The Unseen One"
  • Also known as Πλούτων (Plouton) — "Wealthy"

The Taboo Name

The Greeks rarely spoke his true name. They called him Plouton (Wealthy), Eubouleus (Good Counselor), or simply The Host of Many. To say Hádes aloud was to invite his attention. The accent on our domain is not merely scholarly — it is restoration of a name too dangerous to forget.

03

The Unseen

Domains, symbols, and sovereign darkness

Hádes is not death. He is the administrator of death — the bureaucrat of the afterlife, the judge who never sleeps, the husband who stole his bride and kept her anyway. He does not kill. He receives. Every soul that descends passes through his gates. Every gem mined from the earth was once his. He is not cruel. He is fair — and fairness is the most terrifying thing a god can be.

The Underworld

Not hell. Not heaven. The place beneath the earth where all souls go — Tartarus for the wicked, Elysium for the heroic, and the Asphodel Meadows for everyone else.

Wealth

As Plouton, he is the god of mineral riches — gold, silver, gems. All wealth comes from below. The earth's treasures are his dowry.

Justice

He judges the dead with Minos and Rhadamanthus. There is no appeal. There is no bribe. The verdict is eternal — not because he is cruel, but because he is accurate.

The Dead

Not merely corpses. The psychai — the shades, the breath-souls. He is their shepherd, their warden, their final host. He remembers every name.

04

The Myths

Stories from beneath the earth

The Abduction

Persephone and the Narcissus

Hádes did not storm Olympus to claim a bride. He simply made a flower so beautiful that Persephone could not resist bending to pick it. The earth opened. His chariot rose. And she was gone before her mother could scream. Some call it kidnapping. Hádes calls it the only way to get a queen when you are not allowed above ground. He gave her the pomegranate — six seeds — and bound her to return. This was not cruelty. This was marriage in the underworld, where every contract is eternal.

The Musician

Orpheus and Eurydice

Orpheus descended with his lyre and played so beautifully that Hádes himself wept. He agreed to let Eurydice return — on one condition: Orpheus must not look back. The condition was not cruelty. It was law. When Orpheus turned, Hádes did not laugh. He simply enforced the contract. The underworld does not make exceptions for love. If it did, it would not be the underworld.

The Trickster

Sisyphus and the Boulder

Sisyphus cheated death twice. He chained Thanatos. He escaped the underworld. He mocked the god who cannot be mocked. Hádes did not kill him. He simply gave him a task: roll a boulder to the top of a hill. Forever. The boulder always rolls back down. This is not torture. This is administrative justice — the most perfectly fair punishment ever devised: effort without result, forever.

The Hero

Heracles and Cerberus

Heracles' final labor was to capture Cerberus — without weapons. He descended, wrestled the hound of hell with bare hands, and dragged him to the surface. Hádes did not intervene. He simply watched. Some say he was impressed. Others say he knew Heracles would return eventually — everyone does. The underworld does not lose. It simply waits.
