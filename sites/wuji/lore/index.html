01

Mythology

Stories of Wújí

Wújí belongs to cosmogony rather than narrative. Its myths are stories about the origin of differentiation, told by philosophers, alchemists, and meditators across two millennia.

Zhou Dunyi, Taijitu shuo

Wújí ér Tàijí

In the brief text appended to his famous diagram — the Taijitu shuo, 'Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate' — the Song philosopher Zhou Dunyi (1017–1073) wrote the five characters that launched a thousand commentaries: 無極而太極, wújí ér tàijí, 'the Limitless, and yet the Supreme Ultimate.' Read one way, the sentence is a genealogy: before the supreme ultimate — the yin-yang-bearing pivot from which the world unfolds — there lies the limitless, the blank page beneath the diagram. Read Zhu Xi's way, it is a definition: the supreme ultimate has no form, and 'limitless' names exactly that formlessness, not a prior stage. The Neo-Confucian tradition split over the reading for centuries — is the limitless before, or identical? — but Zhou's own sentence stands deliberately small: five characters, no verb of becoming.

Wújí is the thought that the first thing is not a thing, and that the diagram of everything must be drawn, if at all, on nothing.

Daodejing

The Use of Emptiness

The Daodejing (ch. 11) gives Wújí its favorite parables of the void: 'Thirty spokes converge on one hub, but it is the empty space at the center that makes the wheel useful; clay is shaped into a vessel, but it is the emptiness inside that makes the vessel useful; doors and windows are cut out to make a room, but it is the empty space within that makes the room useful.' Chapter 25 supplies the cosmological scale — 'there was something formless yet complete, born before heaven and earth... I do not know its name; I call it Dào' — and chapter 42 the sequence: 'the Dào gives birth to one; one gives birth to two; two give birth to three; three give birth to the ten thousand things.'

Read together, the chapters trace Wújí's career: the uncarved whole is not destroyed when things are named, but is what allows the naming to happen at all. Emptiness, Laozi insists, is not what remains after usefulness — it is what usefulness is made from.

Zhuangzi 12

The Great Beginning

The Zhuangzi (ch. 12, 'Heaven and Earth') stages Wújí as a dialogue's paradox: asked about the Great Beginning, the text answers that 'in the Great Beginning there was non-being (wú); having no being, there was nothing from which it could come.' Then — the Zhuangzi's characteristic somersault — from non-being came the one, from the one came form, from form came things; and from things came the flux in which worth and value can no longer be told apart. The whole book is an education in this ground: the butcher whose blade never dulls because he follows the empty spaces within the ox, the wheelwright who cannot put his knack into words and so distrusts every book, the useless tree that outlives the orchard because no one can name its use.

Each parable is Wújí applied: the emptiness is where the skill lives, and the person who has found it can no longer be instructed by what has been written down.

Neidan

Refining the Elixir in Stillness

Daoist internal alchemists (neidan) from the Tang onward structured their whole practice on the Wújí–Tàijí sequence. Before the furnace is lit, the adept stands or sits in wújí — the posture of no-pole, weight declared nowhere, intention undeclared — letting jīng, qì, and shén settle and re-gather of themselves; only then does the work of inversion begin, gathering the dispersed light back toward the One. The Song and Ming internal-alchemy manuals describe this opening stillness as the necessary return to the nameless: you cannot reverse the sprouting of things without first re-entering the state before sprouting. 'Practice begins from Wújí' is the standing instruction of a thousand lineages.

The cosmological word thus became a piece of practical technology — the limitless as the correct stance of a body about to act.

02

The Limitless

Emptiness Before Form

Wújí is the boundless before the bounded, the empty circle before the diagram is drawn. In Zhou Dunyi's cosmology it precedes Tàijí; in Daoist meditation it names the state of no-limit, no-position, no-preference from which the ten thousand things arise. It is not nihilistic emptiness but a plenum of undifferentiated potential — the silence that contains every possible note.

To think about Wújí is to practice standing at the edge of language, where names have not yet been attached to things.

Boundless Void

No center, no edge, no direction: Wújí is the canvas on which all distinctions are later painted.

Pre-Cosmic Stillness

Before movement and stillness differentiate, there is a quiet so complete that even 'silence' is too noisy a word.

Source of Tàijí

Wújí is not a rival to Tàijí but its ground; the limitless opens naturally into the supreme ultimate, and the supreme ultimate never leaves the limitless.

Daoist Meditation

Meditative and martial traditions use Wújí as a posture of total neutrality — empty, alert, and ready to become any response.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

無極

The name in its original Taoist form. Wújí (無極) is attested in the source tradition — “The primordial state of emptiness”. Its acute stress marks carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

wuji

Reduced to plain wuji, the name loses everything that made it specific: acute stress marks. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Wújí

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Wújí restores acute stress marks, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Wújí.com → xn--wj-oja4c.com

The non-ASCII characters in Wújí are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Wújí.

Pronunciation

How Wújí was spoken

/u˧˥ tɕi˧˥/ Modern Standard Mandarin (Pinyin)
wú Syllable beginning with labio-velar glide [w] (written 'w' in Pinyin), high back rounded vowel [u], and Tone 2 (rising, ˧˥).
jí Syllable with unaspirated alveolo-palatal affricate [tɕ], high front vowel [i], and Tone 2 (rising, ˧˥). Pinyin 'j' is always [tɕ] before front vowels.

Modern Approximation

WOO-JEE — both syllables carry a rising tone (mid to high), like a gentle question on each half.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

無極 (Wújí) means 'Limitless' or 'Ultimate Nothing', the undifferentiated state before the emergence of Tàijí in Zhou Dunyi's Taijitu shuo. The Modern Standard Mandarin reading is wú (Tone 2) + jí (Tone 2), per the Hanyu Pinyin scheme and the Unihan Database (kMandarin). Baxter & Sagart (2014) reconstruct 無 as *ma (GSR 0103a, 'not have') and 極 as *[g](r)ək (GSR 0910e, 'extreme'). The Unicode restoration Wújí preserves the parallel rising tones of both syllables.

Extended Lore

Wújí is the permission slip to not yet be anything. In a world that rewards definition — what do you do, where do you stand, what do you believe — Wújí is the value of the unmarked state. It is the silence before the opinion, the blank page before the sketch, the open hand before it grasps.
