Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is ​/əˈdiːti/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Aditi).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • A- — The a is the shwa of “about” — the prefix of negation.
  • -di-ti — From a-diti: “un-bound”, “un-fettered” — the name is a ​definition.

Mythology

Aditi’s ​mythology is concentrated in the Veda’s earliest hymns: the mother of the gods whose womb is the sky, whose sons are the days of the year, and to whom the gods themselves ​owe their radiance. Later tradition reduced her to a genealogy — mother of Vishnu in one Purāṇic strand — but the hymns keep her cosmic: the boundlessness in which everything, including the gods, ‌is contained.

The Prayer to Aditi (Rigveda 1.89)

The most famous Aditi-hymn is a prayer for release: ‘May Aditi by whom the gods’ realm is secured, who gives freedom, protect us… Aditi is ​the sky, Aditi the air, Aditi mother, father, son; Aditi all gods, all men, all that has been and shall be.’ The verse makes her the total container — the theonym for totality ‌itself.

The Birth of the Gods (Rigveda 10.72)

The extraordinary theogony-hymn has the gods born of Aditi, and Aditi herself born of Dakṣa, while Dakṣa is born of Aditi — ‘the gods ​later than the creation’ the poet shrugs, admitting the mystery exceeds the scheme. Aditi here is older than genealogy: the matrix in which the generations turn.

Mother of the Days (Dawn & Freedom ‌Cycle)

The Ādityas, her sons, are the months and the year — the solar measure. To be born under Aditi is to be born into time’s wideness; the Vedic householder’s morning prayer placed ​his day under her sons’ jurisdiction and his debts under her power to unbind.

Mother of the Dwarf (Purāṇic Echo)

In the Purāṇic revision of the Vāmana myth, Vishnu’s dwarf-incarnation is born to ‌Aditi and Kaśyapa — the mother of boundlessness bearing the god who measures the three worlds in three steps. The pairing compresses her whole theology: from the unmeasured comes the measurer.

Symbols & ​Iconography

The iconography associated with Aditi concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

Across Cultures

Aditi is the ​Vedic prototype of every Hindu great-mother: the later Devī and Lakṣmī inherit her inexhaustibility, and her sons’ names seeded a dozen theophorics. The Avestan opposition of the daeva is absent — in Iran ​her son Mitra kept his worship as Mithra, while in India the Ādityas were absorbed into Vishnu’s solar theology. Comparative studies pair her with the Greek Eurynome and the Norse Skaði’s freedom-of-the-mountains.

Within ‌the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, Bhakti, and Bhūdevī.

Cultural Legacy

The Āditya-names (Mitra, Aryaman, Bhaga, Dakṣa) seeded the vocabulary of contract, fortune, and skill ​across the Indo-Iranian world; and the Rigvedic prayer to Aditi is still recited at Indian rites of passage as the formula of release from every bond. ‘Aditi’ survives as a girl’s name ​across the subcontinent — the boundless given to a daughter.
