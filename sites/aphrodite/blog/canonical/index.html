The register rules: Aphrodítē

A letter to a colleague, 31 July 2026 — on the name Aphrodítē, and on the forms that keep failing it.

Your note said, roughly: surely the marks are a nicety. Permit me to use Aphrodítē to show they are not.

First, the attestation. The name is attested as Ἀφροδίτη, as LSJ and Beekes record. On the evidence, it means "Born of sea foam (from ἀφρός)". The canonical transliteration is Aphrodítē.

And the marks themselves: i → í (Acute on short iota); e → ē (Eta: long epsilon). Remove any one of them and you have not simplified the name — you have told a small lie about it, and the whole discipline is the refusal of such lies.

Say it aloud as the sources heard it: 'ah-fro-DEE-tay' — the third syllable carries the pitch, and the final vowel is long and clear. The marks are what carry that sound to a reader who has never heard it spoken.

On the plain form — and I want to be precise, because you pushed on this. The plain form aphrodite is not false — it is a fallback, and the register is exact about what that means. In systems that cannot carry diacritics (legacy databases, older tooling, parts of the address-bar pipeline) the flattened form is the accepted vehicle, and the rulebook names it explicitly as the final rung of the fallback hierarchy: full restoration first, circumflex where attested, macron-only by the academic standard, an attested variant, and only then plain ASCII.

What the fallback status forbids is promotion: aphrodite may carry the name where marks are impossible, but it may not represent the name where marks are possible. Every mark it drops — i → í, e → ē — is a fact the sources recorded. A fallback is a vehicle, never the primary.

Now to the forms that provoked your letter. As for Á́phrodítē: it comes from the English ear — English speakers slam the first syllable of every borrowed name (DEL-fi, MY-cene), so the accent drifts forward online, and it fails because moves the stress from i (where the Greek puts it) to A — a different word with a different rhythm; the sources stress i and nothing else As for Aphrodíte: it comes from the half-restoration — well-meaning posts that keep the accent because it looks Greek and drop the macron because it looks optional, and it fails because drops the vowel length on e → ē: the quantity is as attested as the accent (Eta: long epsilon), and without it the meter of the name collapses As for Aphrodîtē: it comes from the circumflex habit — writers who learned that "the little hat means Greek" and apply it wherever an acute belongs, and it fails because the source carries an acute (oxeia, a rising tone), not a circumflex (perispomene, a rise-fall): the two marks are different facts about pitch, and only the acute is attested on i As for afrodite: it comes from the anglicized respelling — the name written the way English guesses it sounds, and it fails because rewrites the consonants of the attested form to fit English spelling habits; every cluster simplified this way erases a letter the sources write

One more thing, because it always surprises: the name did not stay home. The Romans identified Aphrodítē with Venus, originally a goddess of gardens and spring whose cult became one of the most powerful in Rome. Julius Caesar claimed descent from Venus through Aeneas, making her the divine ancestress of the Roman people.

And it never stopped working. Aphrodítē shaped Greek culture more deeply than any martial god. Marriage, prostitution, homosexual love, and civic festivals all fell under her patronage.

The Convention's Own History

The marks this register defends were not invented for typesetting. Ancient Greek carried a melodic pitch accent, and the acute and circumflex were added by Alexandrian scholars — Aristophanes of Byzantium, tradition holds — precisely to preserve it when the spoken language began to level. Vowel quantity (the macron's concern) is older still: it changes meter, meaning, and grammar, and the poets of the Theogony depend on it. To drop either class of mark is to drop two millennia of deliberate preservation. The restored-stress convention simply continues the oldest documented project in European scholarship: keeping the sound of Greek alive in its letters.

Where Protocol Meets Practice

The technical answer is simple: the canonical marks are legal IDNA 2008 characters, encoded as punycode under `xn--`. The practical answer is messier: legal does not mean stocked (registrar inventory is partial), and stocked does not mean typeable (stacked diacritics defeat mobile keyboards).

So the register's rule is a hierarchy of attested forms — full restoration, circumflex, macron-only, attested variant, and finally plain ASCII for the systems that cannot carry marks. Every rung is defensible because every rung is attested. The forms this register rules against are not rungs; they are errors, and errors do not get rungs.

Write back when you have checked the sources; they are better company than I am.

The Verdict

Aphrodítē is the canonical form: attested, checkable, and enforced across the temple, the lexicon, the cards, the APIs, and this register. The plain form aphrodite is sanctioned as the fallback vehicle and nothing more. The false forms above are documented so that no reader has to take the temple's word on faith — the violations are stated, the sources are named, and the register stands open to challenge on every line.

The temple of Aphrodítē stands at /aphrodite/.
