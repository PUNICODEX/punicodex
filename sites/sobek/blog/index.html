Mythology

Sobek has no single continuous myth-cycle; his theology was assembled from the river itself — the annual flood that could drown or feed — and ​from the pharaonic appetite for a god who united raw natural violence with legitimate kingship. The Middle Kingdom elevated him to cosmic rank: a creator who rises from ‌the Nun, the primordial water.

The Crocodile Who Emerges from Nun (Fayum Theology)

The Sobek theology of the Fayum — best preserved in the hymn cycles of the ​crocodile temples — casts him as the one who first climbed from the primeval waters, creator of the world’s order and of the Nile itself. Later tradition even made ‌him a form of the sun god’s power, the fierce Eye of Ra returned from the Nubian desert.

The Double Temple (Kom Ombo)

At Kom Ombo south of ​Luxor, Sobek shares a perfectly symmetrical double temple with Horus the Elder: one half for the falcon of the sky, one for the crocodile of the river. The ‌division stages the Egyptian ideal of the Two Lands’ balance — sky and water, kingship and fertility — under one roof.

Sobek and the Wonders at Court (Papyrus Westcar)

​In the Westcar Papyrus’ cycle of Khufu-era wonders, the crocodile motif appears in the tale of the adulterer punished by a wax crocodile that comes alive at a ‌word — a demonstration that the river-god’s animal is an instrument of moral order, released and recalled by priestly power.

Petsuchos, the Sacred Crocodile (Greek Reportage)

Strabo and Herodotus ​describe the living god of Crocodilopolis: a great crocodile called Petsuchos, kept in a pool, fed by priests, adorned with gold and crystal rings. When it died the ‌city mourned; a new Petsuchos was sought among the river animals by oracular selection — the god re-incarnating indefinitely.

Archaeology & Evidence

Crocodilopolis (Shedet, modern Medinet el-Fayum) held the main temple; Kom Ombo’s double temple survives in superb ‌relief, its columns carrying intact crocodile capitals. Crocodile cemeteries at Tebtunis and el-Shams el-Qawassim held mummified sacred crocodiles by the hundreds, each an embodied Sobek buried with honours.

Across Cultures

Sobek absorbed and was absorbed: as Sobek-Ra he took solar status, while earlier he had fused with Horus as Sobek-Horus, lord of the ​Fayunite unity of king and river. The Greeks called his city Crocodilopolis and identified him with their own river-figures rather than a single Olympian; Roman-period Fayum mummy portraits ​from his cities remain among Egyptology’s most familiar images.

Kindred figures in the PuniCodex cross-tradition index include Anāhitā, Auðhumla, Ọbalúayé, Bꜣstt, Cernunnos, and Cōātlīcue, each linked through earth / ‌mother / fertility.

Cultural Legacy

The name Sobek survives in the modern scholarly vocabulary of the Nile and appears throughout Egyptological literature as the archetype of Egyptian theriolatry ​— the worship of a dangerous animal not despite but because of its power. The Fayum’s temple of Medinet Madi preserves the longest surviving sacred hymn cycles addressed to ‌a crocodile.
