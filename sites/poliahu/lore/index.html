Mythology

Stories of Poliahu

Poliahu's mythology is preserved chiefly in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Hawaiian-language and Hawaiian-authored texts — above all Emma Nakuina's Hawaii, Its People, Their Legends (1904), synthesized in Beckwith's Hawaiian Mythology (1970). Its centerpiece is the great contest of snow and fire for the mountain, a duel that Hawaiians read in the island's very lava flows and snowfields to this day.

The Sled Race

The Stranger on the Mountain

On the slopes of Mauna Kea, Poliahu and her snow-sister friends were challenged to a hōlua sled race by a beautiful stranger. The stranger lost — and flung off her disguise: she was Pele, goddess of volcanoes, and she erupted in fire down the mountainside (Nakuina, Hawaii, Its People, Their Legends, 1904; retold in Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, and Westervelt, Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes, 1916). Poliahu rose to her full height and answered with snow and hail, beating the flames back. The race ran on the great hōlua slide of the mountain's flank, whose stone track can still be traced — the legend pins its scene to a course its first audience knew. The Snow Sisters

The Household of the Peak

Poliahu does not keep the summit alone. Her companions are her sisters — Lilinoe, who rules the mountain's mists, Waiau, who dwells in the sacred lake that bears her name, and Kahoupokane — together the cold maidens of the high mountain (Nakuina 1904; Beckwith 1970). Waiau's water lies nearly four thousand meters above the sea and never truly warms; tradition held it cold and pure as the goddess herself, and chiefs drank of it only after ceremony. Lake Waiau, among the highest lakes in Oceania,Lake Waiau, among the highest lakes in Oceania, was kapu — its water reserved for the highest chiefs and for the goddess's own uses. The mythology does the work of a natural history: it distributes the summit's phenomena — snow, mist, lake, wind — among a family, making the mountain a household rather than a heap of rock.

The Frozen Suitor

Poliahu and ʻAiwohikupua

The Kauaʻi chief ʻAiwohikupua came to the mountain and won the goddess's love — but he was betrothed to a woman of his own island, and Poliahu discovered the truth. Her anger fell on him like weather: he shivered at her touch, and when he fled homeward she sent the cold after him — snow fell on Kauaʻi's heights, a sign of her reach (the legend cycle as recorded in Beckwith 1970, from the Hawaiian-language newspapers; Westervelt 1916 gives a parallel). The courtship also explains the weather: the snow that touches Kaua'i's peaks on the rarest nights is Poliahu's answer still, and old Kaua'i families retold it as a standing warning to oath-breaking chiefs. Fire Against Snow

The Great Battle for the Mountain

The full combat of Poliahu and Pele — the longest form of the legend — raged from Mauna Kea down toward the sea: Pele vomiting rivers of lava, Poliahu calling up snowstorms and hail, the land between them torn and remade, until the fire was driven to its own country in the south and the white mountain stood (Nakuina 1904; Beckwith 1970). Modern kūpuna retell the battle as an account of balance — that the island needs both fire and snow, and that it is the goddesses' long truce, not either one's victory, which keeps the land alive. Hawaiians watching real eruptions reach toward Mauna Kea's snowsHawaiians watching real eruptions reach toward Mauna Kea's snows had the myth for what they saw. Read geologically, the story is a memory of the islands' living duel of elements; read theologically, it grants the cold goddess and the hot one equal sovereignty — fire rules the rift, snow rules the summit, and the island is their treaty line.

02

Poliahu

Snow

Poliahu is the Hawaiian goddess of snow — the beautiful and powerful mistress of Mauna Kea's summit, whose white mantle crowns the highest mountain in the Pacific and whose duel with the volcano goddess Pele for possession of the mountain is one of Hawai'i's most beloved and most retold legends.

Mistress of Mauna Kea

She dwells on the summit of the White Mountain, wrapping its peak in snow and mist.

The Snow Mantle

Her cloak of snow — beauty that can fall as hail and blizzard when she is angered.

Rival of Pele

She met Pele in the legendary hōlua sled race and drove the fire goddess back with snow and hail.

Guardian of the Heights

Lake Waiau and the summit region are her household — the sacred cold at the island's crown.

Sacred Symbols

The Snow Mantle (Kahiko) Her garment of snow spread over Mauna Kea — the 'cloaked bosom' of her name
The Hōlua Sled Her racing sled from the great contest with Pele — beauty in swift, sure motion
Lake Waiau The sacred summit lake, home of her sister Waiau — one of Hawai'i's most revered waters
The Summit Shrines (Ahu) The stone shrines of Mauna Kea's peak, built within her sight over a thousand years
Hail and Mist Her two tempers — the soft mist of welcome and the stinging hail of her anger

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Poliahu

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Poliahu is the standard Polynesian romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Cloaked bosom”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual polynesian names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

poliahu

The plain poliahu form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Pronunciation

How Poliahu was spoken

/po.li.ˈa.hu/ Approximate Hawaiian phonemic rendering
po- Short o as in 'more'; Hawaiian syllables are always open.
-li- Light li; the name is usually parsed poli 'bosom' + ahu 'garment, cloak.'
-A- The stressed syllable — Hawaiian stress falls on the penult here.
-hu Final hu with a soft, breathy h.

Modern Approximation

'poh-lee-AH-hoo' — stress on the third syllable; every vowel is sounded separately.

Extended Lore

Poliahu embodies a wonder unique in the Pacific: snow in the tropics. Hawaiians, meeting white on a tropical summit, made it a goddess — and the making says something precise about Polynesian theology, which found the sacred in the anomalous and the extreme. Where Pele is appetite and eruption, Poliahu is reserve and stillness; the two goddesses are the island's two temperatures, and the myth grants each her jurisdiction. There is also a geography lesson folded into the myth: the legend's battle-scarred terrain matches real volcanic features reaching toward the snows, so the story functions as memory of eruptions older than any chant. But beyond scholarship, Poliahu endures because she is beautiful in the strict sense — a presence that caps the island with white, that can bless a sled race or bury a valley in hail. She is what a mountain means when the mountain is loved.
