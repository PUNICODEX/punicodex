Poliahu: the stories first, then the spelling

The marks in Poliahu make the most sense standing next to the myths they have to carry. So the myths come first.

The Stories That Carry the Name

The Stranger on the Mountain. On the slopes of Mauna Kea, Poliahu and her snow-sister friends were challenged to a hōlua sled race by a beautiful stranger. The stranger lost — and flung off her disguise: she was Pele, goddess of volcanoes, and she erupted in fire down the mountainside (Nakuina, Hawaii, Its People, Their Legends, 1904; retold in Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, and Westervelt, Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes, 1916). Poliahu rose to her full height and answered with snow and hail, beating the flames back.

The Household of the Peak. Poliahu does not keep the summit alone. Her companions are her sisters — Lilinoe, who rules the mountain's mists, Waiau, who dwells in the sacred lake that bears her name, and Kahoupokane — together the cold maidens of the high mountain (Nakuina 1904; Beckwith 1970). Waiau's water lies nearly four thousand meters above the sea and never truly warms; tradition held it cold and pure as the goddess herself, and chiefs drank of it only after ceremony.

Poliahu and ʻAiwohikupua. The Kauaʻi chief ʻAiwohikupua came to the mountain and won the goddess's love — but he was betrothed to a woman of his own island, and Poliahu discovered the truth. Her anger fell on him like weather: he shivered at her touch, and when he fled homeward she sent the cold after him — snow fell on Kauaʻi's heights, a sign of her reach (the legend cycle as recorded in Beckwith 1970, from the Hawaiian-language newspapers; Westervelt 1916 gives a parallel). The courtship also explains the weather: the snow that touches Kaua'i's peaks on the rarest nights is Poliahu's answer still, and old Kaua'i families retold it as a standing warning to oath-breaking chiefs.

The Name Travels

Christianization in Hawaiʻi suppressed the goddess cults, but Poliahu survived in a characteristic Hawaiian mode: through the Hawaiian-language newspapers of the nineteenth century and through authors such as Emma Nakuina — a Hawaiian woman writing in English in 1904 — who preserved the legends while Victorian ethnographers were flattening them. In the twentieth century, a modern form of identification emerged: Poliahu became the patroness of Mauna Kea's defense.

The Address It Earned

The temple of Poliahu stands at /poliahu/ — lore, scholars, patterns — because a name carrying this much story deserves more than a parking page. The restoration keeps one feature the ASCII form drops. One is enough to matter, and the label says so plainly: Tier-2.
