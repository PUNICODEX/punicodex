The Stranger on the Mountain (The Sled Race)

On the slopes of Mauna Kea, Poliahu and her snow-sister friends were challenged ​to a hōlua sled race by a beautiful stranger. The stranger lost — and flung off her disguise: she was Pele, goddess of volcanoes, and she erupted in fire down the mountainside ‌(Nakuina, Hawaii, Its People, Their Legends, 1904; retold in Beckwith, Hawaiian Mythology, 1970, and Westervelt, Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes, 1916). Poliahu rose to her full height and answered with snow and hail, ​beating the flames back. The race ran on the great hōlua slide of the mountain's flank, whose stone track can still be traced — the legend pins its scene to a course ‌its first audience knew. The Household of the Peak (The Snow Sisters)

Poliahu does not keep the summit alone. Her companions are ‌her sisters — Lilinoe, who rules the mountain's mists, Waiau, who dwells in the sacred lake that bears her name, and Kahoupokane — together the cold maidens of the high mountain (Nakuina ​1904; Beckwith 1970). Waiau's water lies nearly four thousand meters above the sea and never truly warms; tradition held it cold and pure as the goddess herself, and chiefs drank of it ‌only after ceremony. Lake Waiau, among the highest lakes in Oceania,Lake Waiau, among the highest lakes in Oceania, was kapu — its water reserved for the highest chiefs and for the goddess's ​own uses. The mythology does the work of a natural history: it distributes the summit's phenomena — snow, mist, lake, wind — among a family, making the mountain a household rather than ‌a heap of rock.

Poliahu and ʻAiwohikupua (The Frozen Suitor)

The Kauaʻi chief ʻAiwohikupua came to the mountain and won the goddess's love — but he was betrothed to a woman of ​his own island, and Poliahu discovered the truth. Her anger fell on him like weather: he shivered at her touch, and when he fled homeward she sent the cold after him — ​snow fell on Kauaʻi's heights, a sign of her reach (the legend cycle as recorded in Beckwith 1970, from the Hawaiian-language newspapers; Westervelt 1916 gives a parallel). The courtship also explains the ‌weather: the snow that touches Kaua'i's peaks on the rarest nights is Poliahu's answer still, and old Kaua'i families retold it as a standing warning to oath-breaking chiefs.
