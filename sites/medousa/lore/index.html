The Authentic Name

Why Médousa.com is the correct form

Original Script

Μέδουσα

The name in its original Greek form. Médousa (Μέδουσα) is attested as gorgon, guardian — “Guardian, ruler (from μέδω)”. Its diphthongs and acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

medousa

Reduced to plain medousa, the name loses everything that made it specific: diphthongs and acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Médousa

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Médousa restores diphthongs and acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Médousa.com → xn--mdousa-bva.com

The non-ASCII characters in Médousa are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Médousa.

Pronunciation

How Médousa was spoken

/'mé.ðu.sa/ Attic Greek Reconstruction
Mé- Short e, pitched upward with the acute — the sound of a warning being called out. The M is resonant, humming. It is the syllable of alarm, of the moment before you understand the danger. It rises like a hand raised to stop you.
-dou- The diphthong ou — sustained, dark, like breath echoing in a cave. The d is soft, almost a th sound in Greek. This syllable does not hurry. It lingers. Like the Gorgon herself: you see her, and there is time to understand what is happening before it is finished.
-sa Short a, the hiss of the final s. It is the sound of stone settling into place. Of breath stopping. Of the body becoming what the eyes have already seen. The name concludes with finality. There is no ambiguity in médousa. There is only consequence.

Modern Approximation

"MEH-dthoo-sah" — with the first syllable carrying a slight rise in pitch, like a warning. The d is soft, closer to "th" as in "this." The ou is like "oo" in "moon," sustained and dark.

Etymological Notes

  • Meaning — μέδω (medo), "to guard," "to protect"
  • Also linked to μέδομαι (medomai), "to think," "to plan"
  • The Guardian — she was a priestess before she was a monster
  • The Protectress — her image was used to ward off evil across the Greek world

The Tier Identity

Because the acute falls on a short vowel (the é) and there are no long vowels requiring macrons, this is a Tier-2 form — accent preservation only. médousa is the single authentic restored form. The PUNYCODEX owns the true name.

04

The Myths

Stories of violation, transformation, and the price of being seen

The Priestess

Before the Serpents

médousa was not always a Gorgon. She was a priestess of Athena — beautiful, devout, sworn to virginity. She served in the goddess's temple on the acropolis. She tended the sacred flame. She wore the veil. She was good. And her goodness is what made what happened next a crime not just against her, but against the order of things. The gods do not always protect their own.

The Violation

Poseidon and the Temple

Poseidon saw her. He desired her. He found her in Athena's temple — the most sacred space, the place where she should have been untouchable. He raped her there, on the floor of the sanctuary, beside the goddess's altar. When Athena discovered what had happened, she did not punish Poseidon. The sea-god was too powerful, too necessary. Instead, she punished médousa. She transformed her beautiful hair into serpents. She made her face so terrible that to look upon it was death. She was punished for being the victim. This is the oldest injustice.

The Exile

The Island of the Gorgons

médousa fled to a distant island, where her sisters — Stheno and Euryale, immortal Gorgons from birth — already lived. They were monsters by nature. She was a monster by punishment. The difference mattered to no one. Heroes came to kill her. They all failed. Their bodies littered her cave, turned to stone by her accidental gaze. She did not want to kill them. She did not want to be looked at. She wanted only to be left alone. But the world does not leave beautiful things alone. It consumes them, then calls them monsters for surviving.

The Beheading

Perseus and the Reflection

Perseus was sent by Polydectes to retrieve her head — a suicide mission, designed to be rid of him. But the gods aided him. Hermes gave him winged sandals. Hades gave him the Helm of Darkness. Athena gave him a polished bronze shield. He found médousa sleeping among her sisters. He did not look at her. He looked only at her reflection in the shield — the only way to face her without dying. He cut off her head. From her neck sprang Pegasus, the winged horse, and Chrysaor, the warrior with the golden sword. Even in death, she created beauty. Even in death, she gave the world wonders. Her blood became both poison and medicine. Her severed face became the world's most powerful protection. The monster was more generous in death than the gods ever were in life.
