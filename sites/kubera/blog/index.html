Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /kuːˈbɛrə/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Kubera).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ku- — Short u as in “put”; k sharp and unaspirated.

Mythology

Kubera’s mythology is a success story: the demon-chief who became a god. The Veda knows him only as Vaiśravaṇa, the powerful ​one of the dark; the epics made him the friend of Rāma’s line, the builder of Laṅkā before Rāvaṇa took it, and the guardian whose aerial chariot Pushpaka still serves the righteous.

The ‌Lord of Laṅkā Before Rāvaṇa (Rāmāyaṇa)

Kubera built the golden city of Laṅkā on the world’s far shore, served by the yakṣas and filled with his hoard. His half-brother Rāvaṇa, through austerities ​and violence, took the city and the flying chariot Pushpaka from him; Kubera withdrew north to mount Gandhamādana, and Laṅkā passed to the demons — the great city’s golden age remembered as ‌his.

The Aerial Car Pushpaka (Mahābhārata)

The epic’s greatest vehicle belonged to Kubera: the self-moving palace-car Pushpaka, won back from Rāvaṇa by Rāma and returned in the epic’s closing book to its maker’s ​friend. To ride Pushpaka is to command the wealth of the world from above — and Rāma, refusing it, set the moral: treasure serves, the righteous decline it.

The Demon Who Won ‌a God’s Favour (Śiva Cycle)

The Purāṇas tell how Kubera, in an earlier cosmic round, performed austerities until Śiva granted him guardianship of the north and lordship of wealth. Some tellings begin ​him darker: a gluttonous yakṣa king whose eye was burnt by Paraśurāma’s glance — the scar that marks his images’ asymmetric face.

Jambhala, the Buddha’s Treasurer (Buddhist Transformation)

Buddhism adopted him outright as ‌Jambhala — black or golden, eared with the mongoose that spits gems, seated on a citron fruit. In this form he became the wealth-god of Tibetan and Himalayan Buddhism, his mantras recited ​in monasteries that formally renounce what he provides.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Kubera concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The Money-Pot — His ​mongoose ejects jewels from its mouth in Buddhist form; his belly is the vault he guards.

Across Cultures

Kubera is Vaiśravaṇa in Buddhist lands, Jambhala in Tibet, and the model of every treasurer-god from the ​yakṣa-cults to the Japanese Bishamonten — the same northern regent under three names. The Greeks heard Plutus in him; the comparatists hear the Celtic Dagda’s cauldron in his inexhaustible pot.

Within the Sanskrit ‌tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.
