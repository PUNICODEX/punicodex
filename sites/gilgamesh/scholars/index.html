01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Gilgameš (gilgamesh) — King of Uruk · He Who Saw the Deep — belongs to the Mesopotamian tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Hero, King of Uruk". The name means "The old man became young"[1].

Gilgameš, king of Uruk, 'two-thirds god and one-third man', is the hero of the oldest great epic we possess. He builds walls, slays the guardian of the Cedar Forest, spurns a goddess, loses his friend, and walks to the end of the world to ask the flood's survivor the only question that matters: must we die?[2]

PuniCodex restores the name as Gilgameš and serves its temple at gilgameš.com. The restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses, and exactly one historically valid Unicode restoration exists, which places the name in Tier 1. The plain ASCII form gilgamesh survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete[3].

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name is attested in Cuneiform as 𒄑𒂆𒈦. Etymologically it means "The old man became young"[1].

The ASCII form gilgamesh survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Gilgameš recovers the full diacritic detail of the scholarly transliteration directly in the address bar. The restoration preserves at least one distinctive feature — a diacritic or a distinctive letter — that the ASCII form loses, and exactly one historically valid Unicode restoration exists, which places the name in Tier 1.

The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • g → G — Same, capitalized
  • i → i — Same
  • l → l — Same
  • g → g — Same
  • a → a — Same
  • m → m — Same
  • e → e — Same
  • s → š — Special character
  • h → — — Dropped

The project holds the domain gilgameš.com (xn--gilgame-wqb.com) as the canonical home of this name[2].

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ɡil.ɡa.mɛʃ/ — Assyriological Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Gil- — Voiced velar stop with close front vowel [ɡil] — firm, like the name of a builder.
  • -ga- — The second velar with open [a] — the name's hinge.
  • -meš — Final [mɛʃ] — the hushing sibilant š, 'sh' as in 'ship', the sound the ASCII spelling loses.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'gil-ga-MESH' — three even, heavy syllables; the final 'sh' is soft, like sand through fingers.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Sumerian — Bilgames (Bil₃-ga-mes₃), the older form of the name, interpreted as 'the old man became young'
  • Akkadian — Gilgameš, the standard form of the epic's language — the name the epic itself echoes in its plant of youth
  • Learned tradition — written with the divine determinative in later texts — the hero reckoned among the gods and a judge of the dead

Gilgameš is Tier 1 by the non-Greek rule: the restoration preserves the caron š, a distinctive letter the ASCII 'Gilgamesh' loses along with its redundant h. The name is Sumerian at root; the Akkadian epic made it the most famous royal name of Mesopotamia.

Sources

  1. Epic of Gilgamesh, Standard Babylonian version, 1300 BCE. ↗

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name is preserved in Cuneiform as 𒄑𒂆𒈦 — Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform, attested Sumerian / Old Babylonian – Neo-Assyrian, c. 2600–600 BCE, in Mesopotamia. The script is written left-to-right / top-to-bottom.[1]

The scholarly transliteration is Gilgameš (Sumerian logogram + Akkadian scholarly), giving the normalized reading /ˈɡil.ɡa.meʃ/.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • Sumerogram GILGAMESH (𒄑𒂆𒈦)
  • Standard royal name of the king of Uruk
  • The final š corresponds to Akkadian /š/

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography associated with Gilgameš concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:[1]

  • The wild bull — His own title — the goring wild bull, son of Lugalbanda and the goddess Ninsun
  • The walls of Uruk — His true immortality, named in the epic's first and last lines
  • The cedar — The timber of the Cedar Forest, cut for the great door of Uruk
  • The plant of youth — 'The old man becomes young' — rejuvenation won from the sea's floor and lost to the serpent
  • The deluge — The flood of Utnapishtim, the deep he alone among men saw and survived to tell

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

The Standard Babylonian epic, 'He who saw the deep', runs from the streets of Uruk to the waters of death. Its twelve tablets hold the first full arc of friendship, loss, and wisdom in world literature.[1]

Enkidu Comes from the Wild (The Friend)

Uruk groans under its restless young king, and the gods answer by fashioning Enkidu from clay — a wild man who runs with gazelles and frees the trapper's game. The harlot Šamhat civilizes him with bread and beer; he comes to Uruk and blocks Gilgameš at the wedding-house door. They wrestle until the doorframe shakes, and the struggle ends in embrace: from that day the king has a second self.[2]

The Cedar Forest and Humbaba (The Journey)

Hungry for a lasting name, Gilgameš proposes the expedition to the Cedar Forest, where the monstrous Humbaba guards the trees for the god Enlil. Through dream-omens and terror the two friends go on; with the aid of Šamaš's thirteen winds they bring Humbaba down and fell the great cedar. The glory is real — and fatal. For this, and for the spurned Ištar's Bull of Heaven, the gods decree that one of the two must die: Enkidu sickens, and dies.

Across the Waters of Death (The Quest)

Clothed in a lion's skin, Gilgameš wanders beyond the world: through the twin mountain where scorpion-men guard the sun's road, along the tunnel of darkness to the garden of jeweled trees, to Šiduri the ale-wife, who tells him what every wisdom text will say after her — eat, drink, hold your child's hand. He crosses the waters of death with the ferryman Ur-šanabi and finds Utnapishtim, the flood's survivor, who tells him the story of the ark and sets him the test of staying awake. He fails.

The Old Man Becomes Young (The Plant)

Utnapishtim gives him one last chance: a thorny plant at the bottom of the sea, 'the old man becomes young'. Gilgameš ties stones to his feet, dives, and wins it — then stops to bathe at a pool, and a serpent smells the plant, takes it, and sheds its skin. The king sits down and weeps. Then he returns to Uruk, brings Ur-šanabi up onto the wall, and shows him the brickwork: this, the epic says, is what a man leaves.

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The epic itself was an international text: copies in Akkadian, Hittite, and Hurrian lay in the archives of Hattusa, and scribes from Ashur to Ugarit learned it by heart. Its flood story adapts the older Atra-ḫasīs epic and stands, at a further remove, behind the biblical account of Noah — George Smith's reading of the eleventh tablet in 1872 caused a sensation for exactly that reason. Gilgameš lived on in the margins of later religions: a judge of the dead in Mesopotamian incantations and visions of the netherworld, and, astonishingly, a named giant in the Book of Giants known from Qumran and from Manichaean fragments, where Humbaba's name survives as Ḥobabish.[1]

Within the Mesopotamian tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Anû, Apsû, Aššur, Ēa, Enlīl, and Ištar.

09

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Rediscovered in the ruins of Nineveh, Gilgameš became the measure of how old literature is: every generation since 1872 has re-translated him, from scholarly editions to novels and graphic retellings. He anchors the modern study of the flood traditions, of heroic friendship, and of the wisdom genre. Uruk — Warka in Iraq, a UNESCO World Heritage site — still raises its walls where the epic begins and ends. To write Gilgameš with the caron is a small act of philological justice: the š is the sound of the epic's own language, and the ASCII 'sh' is a translator's patch.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

The epic's text is archaeology's gift: the fullest copies are the twelve tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (seventh century BCE), excavated in the nineteenth century and held by the British Museum, where George Smith read the flood tablet in 1872. Behind them lie Old Babylonian fragments (the Pennsylvania and Yale tablets), Middle Babylonian copies from Ugarit and Emar, translations from Hattusa in Anatolia, and, oldest of all, the Sumerian Bilgames poems from Nippur and Ur. The historical kernel is modest but real: the Sumerian King List names Gilgameš as fifth king of the First Dynasty of Uruk, and the vast walls of Uruk (Warka, Iraq) still stand where the epic says he built them.[1]

Sources

  1. Epic of Gilgamesh, Standard Babylonian version, 1300 BCE. ↗

Cuneiform Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The epic reaches us through four layers of clay. Deepest are the Sumerian Bilgames poems — Gilgamesh and Agga, Gilgamesh and Huwawa, Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven, Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld, and The Death of Gilgamesh — preserved on Old Babylonian school tablets chiefly from Nippur and Ur, the independent tales the later epic drew upon.[1] Next come the Old Babylonian Akkadian fragments, early in the second millennium, in which the connected epic is first recognizable: the 'Pennsylvania tablet' in Philadelphia, with Enkidu's coming to Uruk, and the 'Yale tablet' in New Haven, with the Cedar Forest journey.[2] The Middle Babylonian periphery then shows the text traveling: copies in Akkadian from Ugarit and Emar, and translations and adaptations in Hittite and Hurrian from the archives of Hattusa, where the epic was school-text and literature at once.[3] Fullest and latest is the Standard Babylonian version — the twelve-tablet recension tradition ascribes to the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni — known above all from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh (seventh century BCE), excavated in the nineteenth century and now in the British Museum; it was on its eleventh tablet that George Smith, in 1872, read the flood account and announced it to a stunned world.[4] Tablet XII, a translation of the Sumerian Netherworld poem appended to the cycle, stands outside the main arc — an ancient appendix, honestly marked.[1]

Sources

  1. ETCSL, Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature, Oxford. ↗
  2. Epic of Gilgamesh (the Old Babylonian 'Pennsylvania' and 'Yale' tablets).
  3. Kramer, The Sumerians; CAD (the peripheral witnesses).
  4. Epic of Gilgamesh (the Standard Babylonian recension of Sîn-lēqi-unninni; the Nineveh tablets).

Enūma Eliš

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Gilgamesh epic and the Enuma Elish are neighbors in the library, not parent and child. The Enuma Elish is the Babylonian creation epic — the exaltation of Marduk, his defeat of Tiāmat, and the fashioning of the cosmos from her body — composed to ground the theology of Babylon; Gilgamesh is a heroic wisdom poem about friendship and death, in which Marduk never appears and the creation of the world never begins.[1] No line of Gilgamesh adapts the creation epic, and the traffic runs elsewhere: Gilgamesh's great borrowing is the flood story of Tablet XI, which descends from Atra-hasis, not from the Enuma Elish.[2] The two texts share a cosmos and a vocabulary instead: the freshwater Apsû that the creation epic makes the first casualty of divine politics — slain by Ea, who builds his dwelling upon it — is the same deep at whose bottom Gilgameš dives for the plant of youth.[3] And they share an afterlife in stone: both were copied for Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, where the twelve tablets of Gilgameš lay alongside the seven of creation until the nineteenth century brought them up together into the light.[1]

Sources

  1. Enuma Elish (the Babylonian creation epic); Epic of Gilgamesh.
  2. Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI; Lambert & Millard, Atra-ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood.
  3. Enuma Elish, Tablet I (Apsû); Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI (the plant from the deep).

Atra-Ḫasīs

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Behind Tablet XI stands the older poem. Atra-hasis, the Babylonian flood epic known from Old Babylonian copies of the early second millennium, tells the whole arc — the creation of humankind from clay and a slain god's flesh, the noise of multiplying humanity that vexes Enlīl, the escalating punishments of plague, drought, and famine, and finally the flood itself, from which Ea saves his devotee Atra-hasis, 'Exceeding Wise', by speaking to the reed wall of his house so that the man overhears and builds the boat.[1] The Gilgamesh poet took that flood narrative and set it in a new mouth: the survivor, renamed Utnapishtim, tells it himself, across the waters of death, to the one man who has come to ask whether he must die.[2] The borrowings are exact — the warning delivered obliquely to the wall, the boat's dimensions and the sealing of its door, the landing on the mountain, the birds sent out in sequence, the sacrifice whose smoke gathers the hungry gods — and the younger poet adds his own image to it: the gods crowded over the offering 'like flies'. What was a myth of human origins becomes, in the retelling, an argument about human ends.[2] The direction of influence is certain, from the older Atra-hasis to the younger epic; and the same tradition, at a further remove, underlies the biblical account of Noah — which is why George Smith's reading of the eleventh tablet in 1872 caused a sensation far beyond Assyriology.[3]

Sources

  1. Lambert & Millard, Atra-ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood.
  2. Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI (the flood account); Dalley, Myths from Mesopotamia.
  3. Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI; Kramer, The Sumerians.
15

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

The epic of Gilgameš begins with a wall and ends with a wall, and between them lies the whole education of a human being. At first the king believes what strong young kings believe: that the enemy is distance — the Cedar Forest, the far shore, the undying man beyond the sea. Each victory over distance only brings the real enemy closer, until it stands in his own bedchamber: the fact that he will die. The scorpion-men, the ale-wife, the ferryman, and the flood-hero all tell him the same thing in different voices, and he cannot hear it until the serpent takes the plant. Only then, weeping at the water's edge, does he become the hero of wisdom rather than of strength.

What he brings home is not immortality but an account: 'he who saw the deep' — the epic's first line names him by what he saw and reported, not by what he kept. The plant was for the body; the tale is for the city. And so the last gesture of the poem is a tour of masonry: here are the walls, the brickwork, the threshold. Four thousand years have proven the bargain fair. Uruk's walls are dust, but the tale has outlasted every cedar, every serpent, and every king who heard it and was, for a moment, less afraid.[1]
