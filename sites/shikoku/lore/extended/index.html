Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script 四国 Shikoku — "Four provinces"
Unicode Restoration Shikoku Canonical scholarly spelling — ASCII-identical
Modern ASCII shikoku Plain-ASCII fallback

The name Shikoku reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the japanese tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Shikoku in Later Traditions

Shikoku's religious landscape is layered rather than replaced. Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines share precincts along the henro, and the island's kami — the mountain powers of Ishizuchi, the sea deities of the whirlpool coast — are read through the honji-suijaku logic as local manifestations of buddhas and bodhisattvas. The pilgrimage itself absorbed folk religion: osettai is a village institution older than any temple guild, and the Daishi is at once a historical monk, an incarnation of a cosmic buddha, and a helpful stranger on the road. In the modern era the henro has been recast for international walkers, secular hikers, and 'power spot' tourists without losing its ritual grammar, and churches and new religions appear among the givers of osettai. Syncretism here is not a single historical event but the island's permanent condition: every age walks the same road and calls the companion by a different name.

Modern Legacy

The Shikoku Henro remains one of Japan's most important pilgrimages, walked by tens of thousands each year — on foot, by bus, in section over a lifetime — and studied worldwide as a model of living religious practice (Statler, Japanese Pilgrimage, 1983; Reader & Tanabe, Practically Religious, 1998). The island's rural culture, its udon, its vine bridges and thatched Iya villages are celebrated as the 'hidden Japan' of slow travel. Kūkai's bequest runs beyond religion: tradition credits him with perfecting the kana syllabaries and founding Japan's first school open to commoners, and the Daishi's name opens esoteric rites across the country. Shikoku has become the national emblem of a counter-rhythm — the island where the answer to speed is not nostalgia but a walking staff.

In the Earth

The material record of the island's sacred culture is continuous and visible. Zentsū-ji (temple 75) preserves the Kūkai birth precinct, the Miedō hall with the birth room still shown to pilgrims, and Tairyū-ji (temple 21) on its eastern ridge guards the vajra said to have flown there when the young Kūkai prayed for a sign. Henro temples from Ryōzen-ji (temple 1, early 9th c.) onward preserve Daishi halls where food is offered daily to Kūkai as one still present — because he is believed to be. On Ishizuchi-san, the summit shrine and the chained final ascent mark the Shugendō layer of the cult. Across the water, the Okunoin of Kōya-san — Kūkai's mausoleum, part of the UNESCO World Heritage 'Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range' (2004) — is the circuit's gravitational center, where the lanterns before the master are said to have burned for twelve centuries.

The Meditation

What Shikoku means — beyond the dictionary

Shikoku teaches that a place can become sacred through walking. The island is not merely the setting of the pilgrimage; the pilgrimage is the island's true form. Every mountain pass, fishing hamlet, and henro trail between the temples is part of the rite, and the islanders' osettai makes the whole population liturgists. To walk the henro is to let the land shape the soul at walking pace — the pace at which things actually change. The circuit also makes an audacious claim about numbers: eighty-eight is not a route length but a diagnosis, one temple for each delusion of the human heart. You do not walk the island to see Kūkai's sites; you walk it so that the walking, repeated daily for weeks, wears the self down to something lighter. In an age of acceleration, Shikoku's quiet insistence is that the shortest distance to the sacred is the long way round.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Shikoku, Smallest Main Island, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Shikoku?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Shikoku is /ɕi.ko.kɯ/ — approximately 'SHEE-koh-koo' — three crisp syllables.

02What does Shikoku mean?

Shikoku means Four provinces in the japanese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Shikoku?

Shikoku is associated with Pilgrim's staff and sedge hat (The kongō-zue staff rings for the dead who walk with the henro, and the sugegasa shades the white-robed pilgrim.), Osettai (The free food, lodging, and small gifts islanders offer walkers — the gift economy that sustains the circuit.), Eighty-eight temples (The sacred circuit that defines Shikoku's spiritual geography, one temple for each of the bonnō.), Naruto whirlpools (The famous tidal whirlpools at the island's eastern edge, where the Seto Inland Sea meets the Pacific.), The white robe (byakue) (The pilgrim's garment, worn as a funeral shroud for the self — the walker dies to ordinary life at temple one.).

04Why restore Shikoku in Unicode?

Shikoku is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Shikoku?

“Kūkai: The Boy of Zentsū-ji” — Shikoku's mythology is the mythology of Kūkai and the Buddhist pilgrimage that bears his memory — documented history, medieval hagiography, and mountain legend fused into a single sacred geography that thousands of pilgrims in white still walk each year, temple by temple, around the island.
