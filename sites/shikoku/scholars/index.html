Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Shikoku ​(shikoku) — ‌Smallest Main ‌Island ​· Four ​provinces ​— belongs ​to the ​Japanese ‌tradition, where ​it ​is catalogued ‌under the ​domain ‌"Smallest Main ​Island". ‌The name ​means "Four ​provinces"[1].

​Shikoku is ​Japan's ​smallest main ‌island, a ​place ‌of mountains, ​coast, ‌and rural ​quiet. For ‌more ​than a ‌thousand ​years it ​has been ‌walked ‌by pilgrims ‌on ​the henro, ​the 88-temple ‌circuit ​associated with ​Kūkai, ‌the founder ​of Shingon ​Buddhism. ‌Shikoku means ​'four ‌provinces,' and ​its identity ​is ​inseparable from ​the ‌spiritual geography ‌of the ​pilgrimage.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the ​name as ‌Shikoku and ‌serves ‌its temple ‌at ​its temple. ‌The restoration ​aSCII-equivalent ​form — ​a ‌description computed ​from the ​displayed ​form itself. ‌The ​plain ASCII ​form shikoku ​survives ‌as a ​modern ‌convenience imposed ​by the ‌early ​domain-name system; ‌the ‌restoration, not ​the fallback, ​is ​the form ​the ‌project defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

The ​reconstructed ‌pronunciation of ‌the ​name ​is ​/ɕi.ko.kɯ/ — ​Modern ​Standard ‌Japanese (Hepburn).[1]

​Phoneme ​by ‌phoneme:

  • ​Shi- — ‌Voiceless ​alveolo-palatal ‌fricative ​[ɕ] plus ​close ​front ​[i]; from ​四 ‌'four'
  • ​-ko- ‌— Voiceless ​velar ‌stop ​[k] ‌plus open-mid ​[o]; ‌from ​国 'province'

  • ​Provinces ‌— ​Awa, Tosa, ‌Iyo, ​and ‌Sanuki

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Shikoku's mythology is the mythology ​of Kūkai and the Buddhist pilgrimage that bears his ‌memory — documented history, medieval hagiography, and mountain legend ‌fused into a single sacred geography that thousands ​of pilgrims in white still walk each year, temple ​by temple, around the island.[1]

Kūkai: The Boy of ​Zentsū-ji (Buddhist history)

Kūkai (774–835), later venerated as Kōbō ​Daishi, was born in the village of Byōbugaura in ​Sanuki province — present-day Zentsū-ji city, where temple ‌75 still marks the family home. The medieval biographies ​wrap his life in marvel: as a youth he ​was sent by a despairing kinsman into the mountain ‌wilderness, and there, tradition says, the esoteric teachings came ​to him in visionary form. The documentary core ‌is solid: in 804 he sailed to Tang China, ​studied Sanskrit and esoteric Buddhism at the Qinglong monastery ‌in Chang'an under the Indian master Huiguo, returned in ​806 laden with scriptures and ritual implements, founded the ​Shingon school, and in 816 received imperial leave ​to open a mountain monastery on Kōya-san. His own ​early writings — the Sango Shiiki of 797 and ​the Shōrai Mokuroku of 806 — are among the ‌founding documents of Japanese Buddhist literature. When he died ​on Kōya-san in 835, tradition refused the word: ‌the master had entered eternal samādhi in the Okunoin ​and would sit there until the coming of the ‌future Buddha. The hagiography makes the point the pilgrims ​still act on: on Shikoku, Kūkai never left.[2]

The ‌Eighty-Eight Temples and the Companion Beside You (Pilgrimage)

​The Shikoku Henro links 88 temples in a circuit ‌of some 1,200 kilometres, and the number is itself ​theology: in Shingon numerology there are eighty-eight bonnō, the ​delusions that bind the heart, and each temple is ‌understood to release one. The circuit as pilgrims walk ‌it took shape in the Edo period, when ‌temple associations, printed guidebooks such as the Shikoku Reijōki, ​and the island-wide institution of osettai — free food ​and lodging offered to walkers — made the henro ‌a mass practice. Pilgrims wear the white byakue and ​sugegasa hat and carry a kongō-zue staff whose ​metal rings announce the dead traveling with them, and ‌they address the Daishi not as a memory but ​as a companion: the walk's first principle is dōgyō ​ninin, 'two traveling together' — yourself and Kūkai. Walking ‌tales insist the Daishi appears along the road ​as a child, a beggar, or a stranger who ‌vanishes around the bend. The circuit turns a whole ​island into one ritual body: to walk Shikoku is ​to walk through Kūkai's biography, one delusion at a ​time.

En no Gyōja and the Peaks of ​Ishizuchi (Shugendō)

Long before the eighty-eight temples were numbered, ‌Shikoku's mountains were training grounds. The great peak Ishizuchi-san ‌(1,982 m) in Ehime is a principal center of ​Shugendō, the mountain-ascetic tradition, and its founding legend belongs ‌to En no Gyōja — the seventh-century wonder-working ​ascetic whom the court exiled and who, tradition says, ​flew over the capital and tamed demons into Buddhism. ‌Shugendō communities hold that En trained on Ishizuchi and ‌sealed its mountain spirits; the summit still keeps a ‌shrine to the Tengu, the red-faced spirit who tested ‌and then served him, and ascetic rites continue ​there in season. Several henro temples preserve the same ‌mountain character — none more than Yokomine-ji, temple 60, ​which clings to a ridge at seven hundred metres ​and was rebuilt from near-abandonment in the 1970s by ​its yamabushi community. The island's sacred geography thus ‌runs on two engines: Kūkai's circuit below and En's ​peaks above, the walking pilgrimage and the climbing one.

​Four Provinces and the Man Who Moved the World ​(History)

Shikoku — literally 'four countries' — is an ‌island the ancient state made: the ritsuryō provincial ​reforms of the seventh century carved it into Awa, ​Tosa, Iyo, and Sanuki, the four kuni that gave ​the island its name and that the pilgrimage later ‌stitched back into a single sacred circuit. For most ​of its history the island was a maritime ‌world of fishing ports, steep fields, and castle towns ​— Tokushima, Kōchi, Matsuyama, Takamatsu — reachable from the ‌mainland only by sea. In the nineteenth century one ​Shikoku son helped end the age he was born ‌into: Sakamoto Ryōma (1836–1867), a low-ranking samurai of ‌the Tosa domain, brokered the secret alliance of Satsuma ​and Chōshū against the shōgunate and sketched a Japan ​governed by councils of daimyō and court nobles rather ​than by a military dictator — a vision he ​lived just long enough to see begun. The ‌island's modern identity holds both halves at once: the ‌henro's slow walking world, and the Tosa-born revolutionary who ​could not wait for it.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Shikoku's ​religious landscape ‌is layered ‌rather than ​replaced. Buddhist ​temples and ​Shinto shrines ​share precincts ​along the ‌henro, and ​the island's ​kami — ‌the mountain ​powers of ‌Ishizuchi, the ​sea deities ‌of the ​whirlpool coast ​— are ​read through ​the honji-suijaku ​logic as ‌local manifestations ​of buddhas ‌and bodhisattvas. ​The pilgrimage ‌itself absorbed ​folk religion: ‌osettai is ​a ‌village institution ​older than ​any temple ‌guild, and ‌the Daishi ‌is at ​once a ​historical monk, ‌an incarnation ​of a ​cosmic buddha, ‌and a ​helpful stranger ​on the ‌road. In ​the modern ‌era the ​henro has ​been recast ​for international ​walkers, secular ‌hikers, and ‌'power spot' ​tourists without ‌losing its ​ritual grammar, ​and churches ‌and new ‌religions appear ‌among ‌the givers ​of osettai. ‌Syncretism here ​is not ​a single ​historical event ‌but the ​island's permanent ​condition: every ​age walks ‌the same ​road and ​calls the ​companion by ‌a different ​name.[1]

Kindred ‌figures in ​the PuniCodex ‌cross-tradition index ​include Delphoí, ‌Fuji, Ise, ‌Kumano, Nagoya, ​and Omphalós, ​each linked ​through sacred ​center / ‌world navel ‌/ axis ​mundi.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

The ​Shikoku Henro ‌remains ‌one ​of Japan's ​most ​important pilgrimages, ​walked ​by ‌tens of ​thousands ​each year ‌— ​on foot, ‌by ​bus, ‌in section ​over ​a lifetime ​— ​and ​studied worldwide ‌as ​a model ‌of ​living ‌religious practice ​(Statler, ‌Japanese Pilgrimage, ​1983; ‌Reader ​& Tanabe, ​Practically ‌Religious, 1998). ‌The ‌island's rural ​culture, ​its ‌udon, its ​vine ​bridges and ‌thatched ​Iya ​villages are ‌celebrated ​as the ‌'hidden ​Japan' ​of slow ​travel. ​Kūkai's bequest ‌runs ‌beyond ​religion: tradition ‌credits ​him with ​perfecting ‌the kana ‌syllabaries ‌and ‌founding Japan's ​first ‌school open ​to ​commoners, ​and the ‌Daishi's ​name opens ​esoteric ​rites ‌across the ​country. ​Shikoku has ​become ‌the ​national emblem ‌of ​a counter-rhythm ‌— ​the island ‌where ‌the ​answer to ​speed ​is not ​nostalgia ‌but ‌a walking ​staff.[1]

Sources

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

The ​material record ‌of the ‌island's ​sacred culture ​is continuous ​and visible. ​Zentsū-ji ​(temple 75) ‌preserves the ​Kūkai ​birth precinct, ‌the Miedō ​hall ‌with the ​birth room ‌still ​shown to ​pilgrims, and ​Tairyū-ji (temple ​21) ​on its ‌eastern ridge ​guards ‌the vajra ​said to ‌have ​flown there ‌when the ​young ‌Kūkai prayed ​for a ​sign. Henro ‌temples ‌from Ryōzen-ji ‌(temple 1, ​early ​9th c.) ‌onward preserve ​Daishi ​halls where ‌food is ​offered ​daily to ‌Kūkai as ​one still ‌present ​— because ​he is ​believed ​to be. ‌On Ishizuchi-san, ‌the ​summit shrine ‌and the ​chained ​final ascent ‌mark the ‌Shugendō layer ‌of ‌the cult. ​Across the ‌water, ​the Okunoin ​of Kōya-san ​— ‌Kūkai's mausoleum, ​part of ​the ​UNESCO World ‌Heritage 'Sacred ​Sites and ​Pilgrimage ​Routes in ‌the Kii ​Mountain ‌Range' (2004) ​— is ‌the ​circuit's gravitational ‌center, where ‌the ​lanterns before ​the master ​are said ​to ‌have burned ‌for twelve ​centuries.[1]

Kojiki & Nihon Shoki

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Japan's two earliest chronicles, the Kojiki (712 CE) and Nihon Shoki (720 CE), record Shikoku in mythic narratives of cosmogony and imperial descent. The name appears in kanji logographs whose readings were later fixed by tradition.

The restoration Shikoku preserves the romanized scholarly form derived from those classical sources.[1]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Shikoku ​teaches that ‌a place ‌can ​become sacred ​through walking. ​The island ​is ​not merely ‌the setting ​of ​the pilgrimage; ‌the pilgrimage ​is the ‌island's ​true form. ‌Every mountain ​pass, ​fishing hamlet, ​and henro ​trail ​between the ‌temples is ​part of ‌the ​rite, and ‌the islanders' ​osettai ‌makes the ​whole population ‌liturgists. ​To walk ​the henro ‌is to ‌let ‌the land ​shape the ​soul ‌at walking ​pace — ​the pace ‌at ​which things ​actually change. ‌The ​circuit also ‌makes an ​audacious ​claim about ​numbers: eighty-eight ​is not ‌a ‌route length ​but a ‌diagnosis, ​one temple ​for each ‌delusion of ‌the ‌human heart. ‌You do ​not ‌walk the ​island to ​see ​Kūkai's sites; ‌you walk ​it so ​that ​the walking, ‌repeated daily ​for ​weeks, wears ​the self ‌down ​to something ‌lighter. In ​an age ‌of ​acceleration, Shikoku's ‌quiet insistence ‌is ​that the ​shortest distance ​to the ​sacred ‌is the ‌long way ​round.[1]

Sources

  1. Kūkai, Sango Shiiki (797) and Shōrai Mokuroku (806).
