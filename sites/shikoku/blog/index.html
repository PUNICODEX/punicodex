Shikoku in 2026: why scholars still care

In 2026, names are treated as data points. Shikoku is a reminder that they are also cultural artifacts — and that the difference matters for search engines, AI training corpora, and anyone who types the name of a Japanese figure into a browser. Scholars never stopped caring about the difference between shikoku and Shikoku; the web simply made that care actionable. What follows is the full scholarly picture — name, script, sound, myth, cult, and legacy — followed by the engineering compromise that lets a restored spelling live at a real address. The question is not whether the name is old. It is whether the digital world is old enough to hold it.

Overview

Shikoku (shikoku) — Smallest Main Island — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "Four provinces".

Shikoku is the smallest of Japan's four main islands and the setting of the great eighty-eight-temple Buddhist pilgrimage.

PuniCodex restores the name as Shikoku. The plain ASCII form shikoku is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 四国. Etymologically it means "Four provinces".

Shikoku means "four provinces," from shi (four) and koku (province). The name refers to the four ancient provinces of Iyo, Tosa, Sanuki, and Awa that comprised the island. It is the smallest of Japan's four main islands and is most famous today for the eighty-eight-temple Buddhist pilgrimage.

The ASCII form shikoku survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Shikoku gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is Tier 2 because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 四国 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Shikoku (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //ɕi.ko.kɯ//.

The characters 四国 are semantic: 四 (four) and 国 (province/land). The name is a geographical designation rather than a personal deity, but the island has become sacred through the Henro pilgrimage.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /ɕi.ko.kɯ/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'SHIKOKU' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Shikoku is Tier 2 because the ASCII form shikoku already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short syllable receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as separate morae. The name Shikoku therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English speaker stresses one syllable heavily. The Hepburn romanization used here records the modern standard pronunciation; it does not preserve the historical pitch accent of the word, which differs across dialects. Mythology

The mythology of Shikoku is inseparable from the life of Kūkai (774–835), the founder of Shingon Buddhism. Tradition holds that he established the eighty-eight temples, and pilgrims today believe that he walks with them in spirit. The island's mountains, capes, and springs are dotted with sites where Kūkai is said to have practised austerities or performed miracles.

The narrative of Shikoku participates in the larger mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of shikoku illustrates how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

Kūkai and Shingon (Buddhist history)

Kūkai (774–835), posthumously known as Kōbō Daishi, was born in Sanuki province on Shikoku. He founded the Shingon school of esoteric Buddhism, established the monastery on Mount Kōya, and is credited with countless legends. After his death, he was believed to remain in eternal meditation, guiding pilgrims who walk the 88 temples.

Kūkai as Living Presence (Folk belief)

Many pilgrims believe that Kūkai walks the henro with them, appearing as a helpful stranger, a child, or a beggar. This belief transforms the pilgrimage from a historical commemoration into an ongoing encounter with the sacred.

Archaeology & Evidence

The eighty-eight-temple route preserves medieval and early-modern temple architecture, pagodas, and pilgrimage infrastructure. Sites such as Kōya-san (though technically in Kishū) and the Henro-michi trails have been studied for their historical and religious significance.

Realm & Domain

Shikoku is the domain of pilgrimage, ascetic practice, and rural sacred landscape. The eighty-eight temples associated with Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi) make it one of the world's great pilgrimage circuits. The island also preserves ancient Shinto shrines, kabuki traditions, and a distinctive dialect and food culture.

The domain of Shikoku is understood within the broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, buddhas, and local spirits. Shrines, temples, and household rites all invoke the figure in ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Shikoku therefore names not only a mythic person but a living field of ritual activity: pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. This combinatory or "syncretic" context is essential for interpreting the domains listed above.

Across Cultures

Shikoku exemplifies the interplay of Buddhism and Shinto in Japanese sacred geography. Many of the eighty-eight temples have chinjusha Shinto subsidiary shrines, and the Henro culture blends esoteric Buddhism, mountain asceticism, and local folk belief.

Cultural Legacy

The Shikoku Pilgrimage (Henro) is one of Japan's best-known religious experiences, attracting pilgrims and tourists from around the world. The island's identity is deeply shaped by Kūkai, the o-henro culture, and a proud sense of rural independence.
