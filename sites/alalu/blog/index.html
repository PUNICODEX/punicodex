Overview

Alalu (alalu) — Primordial King, Heaven · The first king of the Hittite gods — belongs to the Hittite tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Primordial King, ​Heaven". The name means "Unknown".

Alalu is the first king of the gods in Hittite and Hurrian mythology, the original occupant of the celestial throne before Anu the sky-god and long before the ​storm-god Teššub. His reign is narrated in the 'Kingship in Heaven' myth, a Near Eastern succession story that parallels the Greek sequence of Ouranos, Kronos, and Zeus. For nine years Alalu rules in ​heaven; then his servant Anu rises against him, and Alalu flees to the dark earth below. The kingship he loses becomes the inheritance that shapes the entire Hittite pantheon.

Mythology

Alalu's mythology is almost entirely contained in the 'Kingship in Heaven' narrative, but ‌that single text is enough to make him one of the most important figures in Hittite and comparative mythology. He is the beginning of the dynasty of heaven.

The First Reign (Kingship in ​Heaven)

The Hittite 'Kingship in Heaven' myth (CTH 344) opens with Alalu seated on the throne in heaven. He is the first to hold kingship among the gods, and for nine years his ‌word is law. The text, translated from a Hurrian original, presents kingship not as eternal but as a rotating office: even the highest god may be replaced when his term is fulfilled. Alalu's ​reign is therefore the prototype of legitimate rule, not its negation.

Anu Rises Against Alalu (Overthrow)

After nine years, Alalu's servant Anu confronts him and defeats him. Alalu flees 'down to the dark ‌earth' — a descent that removes him from active rule but does not annihilate him. Anu then takes the kingship and rules in turn for nine years. The social inversion is striking: ​the servant becomes the master, and the first king becomes a chthonic memory. The pattern will repeat when Kumarbi, Anu's servant, overthrows Anu in turn.

Alalu in the Dark Earth (Descent)

Alalu's withdrawal ‌to the dark earth is the Hittite equivalent of the deposed primordial father in other Indo-European and Near Eastern mythologies. Unlike Kronos, who is imprisoned in Tartaros, or Tiamat, who is killed and ​divided, Alalu simply departs. His continued existence below the ordered world gives him the quality of an ancestor rather than a destroyed enemy: he is past power, not present chaos.

From Alalu to ‌Teššub (Legacy)

The full succession runs Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teššub. Kumarbi, who bites off Anu's genitals and becomes pregnant with the storm-god, is the son or ally of Alalu; through ​him the defeated first king becomes the ancestor of the reigning storm-god. This genealogy means that every thunderbolt Teššub hurls carries, in the distant background, the memory of Alalu's first throne.

Symbols & ​Iconography

The iconography associated with Alalu concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Nine years ‌— The fixed term of Alalu's reign and the cyclic rhythm of succession
  • Dark earth — The underworld or chthonic realm to which the fallen first king withdraws

Archaeology ‌& Evidence

The 'Kingship in Heaven' myth is preserved in Hittite cuneiform tablets excavated at Boğazköy-Hattusa, the Hittite capital, notably KUB 33.120 with its duplicate and continuation. The tablets belong to the archives ​of the Hittite royal citadel and represent a Hittite translation or adaptation of a Hurrian original. No independent Hurrian manuscript has survived, so the Hittite copies are the sole direct witnesses to Alalu's ​myth.

Across Cultures

Alalu belongs to a widespread ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean pattern of primordial king-gods who are overthrown by their successors.

The closest parallel is the ‌Greek succession Ouranos → Kronos → Zeus: Ouranos, like Alalu, is the first sky/celestial ruler who is deposed by a younger power and withdraws from the active cosmos. The Hittite-Hurrian version, however, makes ​the process explicit and contractual: each king rules for nine years before being replaced. Roman Saturnus preserves a memory of the golden-age king deposed by Jupiter. In Mesopotamian tradition, the succession of divine ​kingship from Anu to Enlil and later to Marduk follows a comparable logic, though Alalu himself is foreign to Sumerian and Akkadian sources. The name Alalu has no secure etymology; it may ​be Hurrian, Hattic, or a substrate survival from an earlier Anatolian layer.

Within the Hittite tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Telipinu.

Cultural Legacy

Alalu survives chiefly as a comparative-mythological type: ​the first king of heaven.

Modern scholars cite the 'Kingship in Heaven' text as a key witness for the Near Eastern background of Greek theogony, and Alalu is regularly compared to Ouranos and ‌Saturnus. He appears in encyclopedias of mythology and in studies of Hittite religion, but has little direct afterlife in popular culture — precisely because his myth is about abdication and memory rather than ‌heroic action. A Meditation

Alalu matters to the modern reader precisely because he did nothing heroic. He held a throne for nine years, yielded it when his time was done, and withdrew into ​the dark earth — and for that withdrawal he became the patriarch of an entire pantheon. In an age that treats power as something to be seized, held, and defended to the last, ‌Alalu embodies a radically older idea: that sovereignty is an office, not a possession, and that legitimacy includes the willingness to vacate.

The 'Kingship in Heaven' text makes this explicit in a way ‌no other ancient theogony dares. Alalu is not murdered, mutilated, or imprisoned; he is succeeded. His fall is written into the order of things — a cosmic constitution in which every reign has ‌a term, and every successor was once a servant standing in the courtier's posture, cup in hand. Revolution, the text says, is not the breakdown of order but its deep structure.

And there ‌is a quieter lesson in the dark earth below. Alalu does not vanish when defeated; he endures, remembered, ancestral, the name from which the storm-god's whole lineage descends. He asks us to consider ​what becomes of leaders who learn to retire — and of cultures that know how to retire them. Perhaps a mandate is only truly legitimate if it can imagine its own end: the ‌throne is sacred because it will one day hold someone else.
