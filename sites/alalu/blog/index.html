Overview

Alalu (alalu) — Primordial King, Heaven · Unknown — belongs to the Hittite tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Primordial King, Heaven". The name means "Unknown".

Alalu is the first king of the gods in Hittite and Hurrian mythology, the original occupant of the celestial throne before Anu the sky-god and long before the storm-god Teššub. His reign is narrated in the 'Kingship in Heaven' myth, a Near Eastern succession story that parallels the Greek sequence of Ouranos, Kronos, and Zeus. For nine years Alalu rules in heaven; then his servant Anu rises against him, and Alalu flees to the dark earth below. The kingship he loses becomes the inheritance that shapes the entire Hittite pantheon.

This temple treats Alalu as the archetype of primordial sovereignty: the ruler whose authority is ancient, legitimate, and yet destined to be overthrown. He is not a storm-god or a creator; he is the first holder of the mandate of heaven, the figure against whom every later king defines himself.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Cuneiform (Hittite/Hurrian cycle) as 𒀭𒀀𒆷𒇻 — Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform (Hittite scribal tradition), attested Hittite Empire, c. 1400–1180 BCE, in Anatolia / north Syria. The script is written left-to-right.

The scholarly transliteration is ᵈa-la-lu (gen. ᵈa-la-lu-wa-aš) (Hittitological), giving the normalized reading /ˈalalu/.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈa.la.lu/ — Hittite/Hurrian Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • A- — Open vowel [a] — the first syllable is light, as befits a name whose origin remains unknown.
  • -la- — Lateral [l] between two open vowels, the liquid center that carries the name.
  • -lu — Final open syllable with rounded back vowel [u], closing the name on a soft resonance.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'AH-lah-loo' — three even syllables; the Hittite/Hurrian original is irrecoverable, so the reconstruction is conservative.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Hittite — Alalu, the first king of the gods in the 'Kingship in Heaven' myth (CTH 344)
  • Hurrian — Alaluwa, the Hurrian form underlying the Hittite rendering
  • Cuneiform — 𒀭𒀀𒆷𒇻 (a-la-lu), written with the divine determinative dingir

Alalu is Tier 2: the restored form Alalu preserves only the capital A and the Latin alphabet; it carries no distinctive diacritic or non-ASCII letter. The name's Hittite/Hurrian pronunciation is unknown, and the plain form is the most defensible display.

Mythology

Alalu's mythology is almost entirely contained in the 'Kingship in Heaven' narrative, but that single text is enough to make him one of the most important figures in Hittite and comparative mythology. He is the beginning of the dynasty of heaven.

The First Reign (Kingship in Heaven)

The Hittite 'Kingship in Heaven' myth (CTH 344) opens with Alalu seated on the throne in heaven. He is the first to hold kingship among the gods, and for nine years his word is law. The text, translated from a Hurrian original, presents kingship not as eternal but as a rotating office: even the highest god may be replaced when his term is fulfilled. Alalu's reign is therefore the prototype of legitimate rule, not its negation.

Anu Rises Against Alalu (Overthrow)

After nine years, Alalu's servant Anu confronts him and defeats him. Alalu flees 'down to the dark earth' — a descent that removes him from active rule but does not annihilate him. Anu then takes the kingship and rules in turn for nine years. The social inversion is striking: the servant becomes the master, and the first king becomes a chthonic memory. The pattern will repeat when Kumarbi, Anu's servant, overthrows Anu in turn.

Alalu in the Dark Earth (Descent)

Alalu's withdrawal to the dark earth is the Hittite equivalent of the deposed primordial father in other Indo-European and Near Eastern mythologies. Unlike Kronos, who is imprisoned in Tartaros, or Tiamat, who is killed and divided, Alalu simply departs. His continued existence below the ordered world gives him the quality of an ancestor rather than a destroyed enemy: he is past power, not present chaos.

From Alalu to Teššub (Legacy)

The full succession runs Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teššub. Kumarbi, who bites off Anu's genitals and becomes pregnant with the storm-god, is the son or ally of Alalu; through him the defeated first king becomes the ancestor of the reigning storm-god. This genealogy means that every thunderbolt Teššub hurls carries, in the distant background, the memory of Alalu's first throne.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Alalu concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Throne — The first seat of divine kingship in heaven
  • Nine years — The fixed term of Alalu's reign and the cyclic rhythm of succession
  • Dark earth — The underworld or chthonic realm to which the fallen first king withdraws
  • Divine determinative — The cuneiform dingir (𒀭) that marks Alalu as a god in Hittite texts
  • Succession chain — Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teššub, the Hittite parallel to Greek Ouranos → Kronos → Zeus

Archaeology & Evidence

The 'Kingship in Heaven' myth is preserved in Hittite cuneiform tablets excavated at Boğazköy-Hattusa, the Hittite capital, notably KUB 33.120 with its duplicate and continuation. The tablets belong to the archives of the Hittite royal citadel and represent a Hittite translation or adaptation of a Hurrian original. No independent Hurrian manuscript has survived, so the Hittite copies are the sole direct witnesses to Alalu's myth.

Across Cultures

Alalu belongs to a widespread ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean pattern of primordial king-gods who are overthrown by their successors.

The closest parallel is the Greek succession Ouranos → Kronos → Zeus: Ouranos, like Alalu, is the first sky/celestial ruler who is deposed by a younger power and withdraws from the active cosmos. The Hittite-Hurrian version, however, makes the process explicit and contractual: each king rules for nine years before being replaced. Roman Saturnus preserves a memory of the golden-age king deposed by Jupiter. In Mesopotamian tradition, the succession of divine kingship from Anu to Enlil and later to Marduk follows a comparable logic, though Alalu himself is foreign to Sumerian and Akkadian sources. The name Alalu has no secure etymology; it may be Hurrian, Hattic, or a substrate survival from an earlier Anatolian layer.

Within the Hittite tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Telipinu.

Cultural Legacy

Alalu survives chiefly as a comparative-mythological type: the first king of heaven.

Modern scholars cite the 'Kingship in Heaven' text as a key witness for the Near Eastern background of Greek theogony, and Alalu is regularly compared to Ouranos and Saturnus. He appears in encyclopedias of mythology and in studies of Hittite religion, but has little direct afterlife in popular culture — precisely because his myth is about abdication and memory rather than heroic action. For PuniCodex, Alalu is a restoration of the origin-point of kingship: the name that marks where the mandate of heaven begins. The name still means: the throne before the throne.
