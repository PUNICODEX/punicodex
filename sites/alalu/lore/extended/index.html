Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Alalu is the first king of the gods in Hittite and Hurrian mythology, the original occupant of the celestial throne before Anu the sky-god and long before the storm-god Teššub. His reign is narrated in the 'Kingship in Heaven' myth, a Near Eastern succession story that parallels the Greek sequence of Ouranos, Kronos, and Zeus. For nine years Alalu rules in heaven; then his servant Anu rises against him, and Alalu flees to the dark earth below. The kingship he loses becomes the inheritance that shapes the entire Hittite pantheon.

This temple treats Alalu as the archetype of primordial sovereignty: the ruler whose authority is ancient, legitimate, and yet destined to be overthrown. He is not a storm-god or a creator; he is the first holder of the mandate of heaven, the figure against whom every later king defines himself.

Alalu in Later Traditions

Alalu belongs to a widespread ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean pattern of primordial king-gods who are overthrown by their successors.

The closest parallel is the Greek succession Ouranos → Kronos → Zeus: Ouranos, like Alalu, is the first sky/celestial ruler who is deposed by a younger power and withdraws from the active cosmos. The Hittite-Hurrian version, however, makes the process explicit and contractual: each king rules for nine years before being replaced. Roman Saturnus preserves a memory of the golden-age king deposed by Jupiter. In Mesopotamian tradition, the succession of divine kingship from Anu to Enlil and later to Marduk follows a comparable logic, though Alalu himself is foreign to Sumerian and Akkadian sources. The name Alalu has no secure etymology; it may be Hurrian, Hattic, or a substrate survival from an earlier Anatolian layer.

Modern Legacy

Alalu survives chiefly as a comparative-mythological type: the first king of heaven.

Modern scholars cite the 'Kingship in Heaven' text as a key witness for the Near Eastern background of Greek theogony, and Alalu is regularly compared to Ouranos and Saturnus. He appears in encyclopedias of mythology and in studies of Hittite religion, but has little direct afterlife in popular culture — precisely because his myth is about abdication and memory rather than heroic action. For PuniCodex, Alalu is a restoration of the origin-point of kingship: the name that marks where the mandate of heaven begins. The name still means: the throne before the throne.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Alalu in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Alalu, Primordial King, Heaven, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Alalu?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Alalu is /ˈa.la.lu/ — approximately 'AH-lah-loo' — three even syllables; the Hittite/Hurrian original is irrecoverable, so the reconstruction is conservative..

02What does Alalu mean?

Alalu means Unknown in the hittite tradition.

03What are the symbols of Alalu?

Alalu is associated with Throne (The first seat of divine kingship in heaven), Nine years (The fixed term of Alalu's reign and the cyclic rhythm of succession), Dark earth (The underworld or chthonic realm to which the fallen first king withdraws), Divine determinative (The cuneiform dingir (𒀭) that marks Alalu as a god in Hittite texts), Succession chain (Alalu → Anu → Kumarbi → Teššub, the Hittite parallel to Greek Ouranos → Kronos → Zeus).

04Why restore Alalu in Unicode?

Plain ASCII alalu strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Alalu?

The Hittite 'Kingship in Heaven' myth (CTH 344) opens with Alalu seated on the throne in heaven. He is the first to hold kingship among the gods, and for nine years his word is law. The text, translated from a Hurrian original, presents kingship not as eternal but as a rotating office: even the highest god may be replaced when his term is fulfilled. Alalu's reign is therefore the prototype of legitimate rule, not its negation.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Alalu and related cults.
  • The 'Kingship in Heaven' myth is preserved in Hittite cuneiform tablets excavated at Boğazköy-Hattusa, the Hittite capital, notably KUB 33.120 with its duplicate and continuation. The tablets belong to the archives of the Hittite royal citadel and represent a Hittite translation or adaptation of a Hurrian original. No independent Hurrian manuscript has survived, so the Hittite copies are the sole direct witnesses to Alalu's myth.
