Setting the Wheel in Motion (The First Sermon)

After the awakening beneath the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gayā, ‌the tradition recorded in the Mahāvagga of the Vinaya says the Buddha hesitated to teach: the Dhamma he had seen was subtle, peaceable, beyond the net of opinion, and beings were pleasure-bent. ​The god Brahmā Sahampati descended from his heaven to beg him to open the doors of the Deathless, and the Buddha surveyed the world with the awakened eye and saw beings ‌'with little dust in their eyes' — enough to warrant the effort. He walked from Gayā to the deer park of Ṛṣipatana outside Benares, where the five ascetics who had attended him ​— Koṇḍañña, Vappa, Bhaddiya, Mahānāma, and Assaji — had sworn to shun him for abandoning severe austerity, yet rose to receive him. To them he preached the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11): the ‌two extremes to avoid, the middle way, the four noble truths, and the three turnings of the wheel in their twelve modes. Koṇḍañña's mind opened with the dustless, stainless eye of ​Dhamma — 'whatever is subject to arising is subject to cessation' — and the gods of ten thousand world-systems cried that the unsurpassed wheel had been set rolling. In a single sermon ‌in a deer park, a private awakening became a public teaching and a solitary sage became the head of a community: that is Sarnath's primacy.

Where the Sages Fell (Ṛṣipatana)

The commentarial ​tradition explains the ancient name Ṛṣipatana — Pali Isipatana, 'the place where the sages fell (rained down)' — with a legend: five hundred paccekabuddhas, solitary enlightened beings, foresaw that a fully ‌awakened Buddha would arise and preach upon that very ground. At the appointed hour they rose into the air, flew through the sky, and descended in the deer park; having paid homage ​to the ground and circled it, they passed into final nirvāṇa together. Hence the ground where they 'fell' was called Ṛṣipatana. Whether read as legend or as etiological tale, the story fixes ​Sarnath's identity in Buddhist memory: not merely a pleasant grove outside Benares but a pre-appointed stage, reserved across cosmic time for the one sermon that would matter most. Modern philology reads ‌the name more soberly — the 'fall' may mark where sages descended to attain the ground, or simply where a hermitage stood — but the tradition itself tells the grander version, and ‌it is the tradition that drew pilgrims for two thousand years.

The Deer King's Mercy (The Banyan Deer)

The deer park's gentleness had a model in the Jātakas, the vast corpus of ‌the Buddha's former lives. The Nigrodhamiga Jātaka (no. 12) tells of a time when the Bodhisatta was born as the golden king of a deer herd in a royal park at ​Benares. The king's kitchen daily took one deer; the Bodhisatta persuaded him to draw lots instead, so that herds and families would not be broken indiscriminately. When the lot at last fell ​on a pregnant doe, the deer king stepped forward in her place — and the king, struck that a beast would give its life for another's unborn, granted safety to all deer, ‌then to every creature of his realm. A deer park is thus, in Buddhist imagination, the one landscape where the law of the stronger is lawfully suspended. The tradition attached this ​ideal to Sarnath's Mṛgadāva: the park where deer grazed unafraid was the fitting auditorium for a teaching that would extend the Bodhisatta's self-replacement to all beings — and the pair of deer ​flanking the wheel on Aśoka's own art is that Jātaka's ethic in stone.
