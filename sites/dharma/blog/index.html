Mythology

Dharma’s ​mythology is the epics’ moral machinery: the god of righteousness fathers the eldest Pāṇḍava, tests his devotees in disguise, and — in the Nala story — teaches dice to a king as the ​instrument of ruin. He has no cult-image because he is what every cult-image stands for; yet the tradition gave him a family and a biography.

The Father of Yudhiṣṭhira (Mahābhārata)

When Pāṇḍu was ‌cursed to die on his wedding night, his queens invoked the gods: Dharmarāja himself, the god of righteousness, fathered Yudhiṣṭhira on Kuntī. The eldest Pāṇḍava is thus dharma’s son — which is why ​the epic keeps asking whether righteousness itself can survive the dice-game and the war it forces.

The Tests in Disguise (Dharma-Upākhyāna)

The epic’s forest-books stage Dharma’s examinations: as a stork demanding why Yudhiṣṭhira ‌would not abandon a fallen brother; as a Yakṣa whose riddles the king answers with the epic’s most quoted verses; as a dog who follows the Pāṇḍavas to heaven’s gate and is refused ​entry — until Yudhiṣṭhira refuses heaven without it. The dog was Dharma; the gate opens.

The God Who Taught Dice (Nala Cycle)

In the Nala episode, Kali — the demon of the Kali ‌Yuga — begs Dharma for a means to enter the righteous king Nala; Dharma, inexplicably, teaches him dice. The story’s commentators wrestled for centuries with the god of righteousness arming the demon of ​strife: the standard resolution — even dharma’s permission is a test that righteousness must survive.

The Son of Brahmā (Purāṇic Personification)

The Purāṇas give Dharma genealogy: born of Brahmā’s breast as one of ‌the mind-born sons, husband of Dhṛti and father of the personified virtues — Kāma (desire), Lobha (greed), Darpa (pride) and the rest. The abstraction’s family-tree is the Indian psyche’s map of its own ​forces.

Across Cultures

Dharma and the Buddha’s Dhamma share a word and a mission: the Buddha claimed the title Dharmarāja, ​king of the law, and made dharma’s wheel — the dharmacakra — his teaching’s emblem. The Zoroastrian daēnā and the Roman Fas are the comparison-points scholars cite; in India itself dharma absorbed every ‌local law-code, from the Vedic ṛta to the medieval digests.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.

Cultural Legacy

The word is ​untranslatable and indispensable — every rendering (‘law’, ‘duty’, ‘religion’, ‘righteousness’) is a fraction. The four feet of dharma’s bull are India’s theory of history; the Bhagavadgītā’s ‘better one’s own dharma, though defective’ is ​among the most quoted ethical sentences on earth. India’s modern name for itself, ‘Hindu Dharma’, keeps the word at the nation’s centre.
