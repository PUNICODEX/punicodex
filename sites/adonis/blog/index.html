​Mythology

Ádōnis's myth turns on beauty that cannot be kept. He is desired, born, killed, and divided — a story the Greeks borrowed from the Near East and made their own.

Born ‌from Myrrh (Origin)

Myrrha, daughter of King Kinyras, conceived an incestuous passion for her father. When she was transformed into a myrrh tree as punishment, the tree split open after ten months and ​Ádōnis was born. Aphrodítē, pierced by Eros's arrow, hid the infant in a chest and gave him to Persephonē to raise.

Divided Between Queens (Judgment)

When Persephonē opened the chest and ‌saw Ádōnis, she refused to return him. Zeus decreed that he would spend a third of the year with Aphrodítē, a third with Persephonē, and a third where he chose — he chose ​Aphrodítē, for love of beauty and the living world.

The Boar (Death)

Despite Aphrodítē's warnings, Ádōnis hunted a boar. The beast gored him in the thigh. From his blood the anemone rose, ‌and Aphrodítē's grief became the template for the women's Adṓnia festival.

The Adṓnia (Cult)

Across the Greek world women planted 'Gardens of Adonis' — lettuces and herbs in shallow trays that sprouted ​quickly and withered. They carried images of the dead god to the sea, lamenting and celebrating the cycle of growth and loss.

Across Cultures

Ádōnis is transparently the Greek adaptation of the Semitic Tammuz (Sumerian Dumuzi), the dying-and-rising vegetation god mourned by women in ‌Mesopotamia and the Levant. The name itself comes from the Canaanite/Phoenician ʾādōn, 'lord'. In Roman poetry he becomes a byword for youthful male beauty — Venus and Adonis is a staple of Ovid ​and later painters. The figure also overlaps with Near Eastern dying-god typologies studied by Frazer, though modern scholars resist collapsing every vegetation deity into a single pattern.

Within the Greek tradition, closely related ​names in the corpus include Achérōn, Achilleús, Adámas, Aḗr, Aígyptos, and Aithḗr.

Cultural Legacy

Ádōnis survives in the anemone, in the phrase 'Adonis' applied to handsome young men, and in the ​long Western tradition of Venus and Adonis from Ovid to Shakespeare to Titian. His cult is one of the clearest cases of religious borrowing across the Aegean and Levant, and the Adṓnia gives ​rare evidence for women's religious practice in classical Greece. In modern wellness and beauty branding, his name still signals youth, desire, and the fleeting bloom of physical perfection.

A Meditation

Ádōnis is the ​god of what cannot last. Every spring his myth asks: why do the most beautiful things die first? The answer is not theological; it is seasonal. He is the flower, the harvest, the ‌young lover, the grief that returns every year. To worship Ádōnis is to consent to love something you will lose.

Yet the loss is not final. The anemone returns. The women plant new ​gardens. The rite enacts not despair but cyclical hope — beauty dies, beauty returns, and the lament itself becomes the vessel of return. Ádōnis is therefore a god not of endings but of ​the interval between endings, the green moment before the heat of summer takes it away.
