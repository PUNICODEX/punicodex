Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Nǚwā ​(nuwa) — ‌The Mender ‌of ​the Sky ​— belongs ​to ​the Chinese ​tradition, where ‌it ​is catalogued ​under the ‌domain ​"Creation, Mending ‌Heaven". The ​name ‌means "Woman ​wa"[1].

Nǚwā ​is ​the great ​repairer: the ​goddess ‌who melted ​five-colored stones ‌to ​patch the ‌broken sky, ​cut ‌off the ​giant turtle's ‌legs ​to prop ​the corners ‌of ‌the world, ‌and — ​on ​a quieter ‌day — ​knelt ​by the ‌Yellow River ​and ​made the ‌first human ​beings out ‌of ​mud.[2]

PuniCodex ​restores the ​name ​as Nǚwā ‌and serves ‌its ​temple at ‌nǚwā.com. The ​original ​preserves the ‌tone mark ‌on ‌ǚ and ‌the long ​ā ‌— distinctive ​features the ​ASCII ​form loses ‌— which ​places ​the name ​in Tier ‌1. ​The plain ​ASCII form ​nuwa ‌survives as ​a modern ‌convenience ​imposed by ‌the early ​domain-name ‌system; the ‌restoration, not ​the ​fallback, is ​the form ​the ‌project defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

Sources

  1. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗
  2. Anne Birrell, Chinese Mythology: An Introduction, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993.
  3. Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian), Sima Qian; Chinese Text Project, 91 BCE. ↗

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested ‌in Chinese ​characters ​as 女媧. ​Etymologically ​it means ​"Woman ‌wa"[1].

The ​ASCII ​form nuwa ‌survives ​only because ‌the ​early domain-name ‌system ​could not ​carry ​diacritics; it ​is ​a technological ‌compromise, ​not ‌an ancient ​spelling. ‌The Unicode ​restoration ‌Nǚwā recovers ​both ‌the stress ​accent ​and the ‌vowel ‌length of ‌the ​original directly ​in ‌the address ​bar. ​The original ‌preserves ​the tone ​mark ‌on ǚ ​and ‌the long ​ā ​— distinctive ​features ​the ASCII ‌form ‌loses — ​which ‌places the ​name ​in Tier ‌1.

‌The letter-by-letter ‌transformation ‌runs:

  • ​n ‌→ N ​— ​Same, capitalized

​- ‌u ​→ ǚ ​— ​Stress on ‌u
​- w ​→ ​w — ‌Same
​- a ‌→ ​ā — ‌Long ​vowel

The ‌project ‌holds the ​domain ​nǚwā.com (xn--nw-ela08f.com) ​as ​the canonical ‌home ‌of this ​name[2].

Sources

  1. Shanhaijing.
  2. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed pronunciation ‌of ‌the ​name is ​/ny˨˩˦ ​wa˥/ — ​Mandarin ​(Hanyu ‌Pinyin with ​tones).[1]

​For ‌the modern ​speaker, ‌the closest ​approximation ‌is: ​nyoo-wah, low-dipping ​tone ​on Nǚ, ​high ​level ‌on Wā.

​Kindred ‌and ​historical forms ‌of ​the name:

‌- ​Chinese ‌— 女媧
​- ​Her title ‌— ‌媧皇 ‌(Wāhuáng)

  • ​Owned ​form ‌— Nǚwā

​Nǚwā ​女娲 bears ‌the ​third ​tone on ‌nǚ ​— the ‌vowel ​ü ​written with ​its ​two dots, ‌which ‌the ​restoration keeps ‌and ​plain ​ASCII drops. ‌Wā ‌is the ‌level-toned ‌archaic ​graph for ‌this ​goddess alone: ​a ​character ‌invented for ​her ​name, ​used for ‌nothing ​else in ​the ​language. ‌The ü ​is ‌the front ​rounded ‌vowel ​— the ‌sound ‌French ​writes u ​— ​which plain ​ASCII ‌loses ‌to plain ​u.

Sources

  1. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name ‌is ‌written ​in ​Chinese ​characters ​as ​女媧. ‌This original ​script ​is ‌the ​form ‌against ​which ‌the ​ASCII ​fallback ​nuwa ​and ​the ‌PuniCodex ​restoration ‌Nǚwā ​are ‌measured: the ​restoration ‌preserves ​both ‌its ​pitch ​accent ‌and ‌its ‌vowel ​quantity ​of ‌the ​written ​form, ‌so ​that ​a ‌reader typing ​the ‌modern ​address ​still ​speaks ​the ‌ancient ‌name.[1]

​Written ‌女娲: ​nǚ ​(woman) ‌plus ‌wā ‌— ‌a ​graph ‌so specific ​to ​her ​that ‌dictionaries ​list ​almost ​no ‌other ​word ​under ​it. ‌The ​ü ‌of ​the ‌restoration ​is the ‌sound ‌itself; ​without ​it, ​the ​name ‌is ‌another ​word.

Sources

  1. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Nǚwā ​is the ‌great ‌repairer: ​the goddess ​who ​melted five-colored ​stones ​to patch ‌the ​broken ​sky, cut ‌off ​the giant ‌turtle's ​legs to ‌prop ​the ​corners of ​the ​world, and ​— ‌on ​a quieter ‌day ​— knelt ‌by ​the Yellow ‌River ​and ‌made the ​first ​human beings ‌out ‌of mud.[1]

‌### ​The ​Five-Colored Stones

‌Melted ​and poured ​into ‌the ​sky's wound ​— ‌the world's ​first ‌and greatest ​repair.

​### ​The Serpent ​Body

‌Her form ‌in ​Han ‌art: human ​head, ​serpent body ‌— ‌earth's maker ‌in ‌earth's ​image.

‌The ​Rope of ​Mud

​The rich ‌she ​shaped ​by hand, ​the ‌poor she ​flung ​from ​a rope ‌— ​humanity's two ‌fortunes.

​### The ‌Marriage ​Rites

‌She instituted ‌marriage ​and is ​prayed ​to for ​children ‌— ‌society's first ​legislator.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Nǚwā's two ​great myths answer ‌the two great ‌questions: where people ​came from, and ​why the sky ​holds. Both are ​told in the ​earliest strata of ‌Chinese myth and ​never improved upon.[1]

​### The Mending ‌of the Sky ​(The Catastrophe)

The ‌Huáinánzǐ tells it: ​in high antiquity ‌the four pillars ​snapped, the nine ​provinces split; fire ​raged unquenched, waters ​rose undrained; fierce ​beasts devoured the ‌people. Nǚwā ​melted stones of ‌five colors to ​mend the azure ‌sky, cut the ​legs of the ‌great turtle to ​set the four ‌pillars, slew the ​black dragon to ​save Jì province, ‌and heaped reed-ashes ‌to dam the ‌flood. Then — ​and only then ​— the world ‌was habitable, and ​the beasts went ​quiet.[2]

People ‌of Yellow Earth ​(The Creation)

The ​later compendia preserve ‌the gentler myth: ​Nǚwā, walking alone ‌in the empty ​world, knelt by ​the river and ​shaped human beings ​from yellow clay. ‌The work delighted ‌but wearied her ​— so she ‌trailed a rope ​through the mud ​and flung the ‌drops, which became ‌people too. The ‌handmade figures became ‌the rich and ​noble; the rope-flung ‌drops, the poor ​and common — ​China's oldest reflection ​on how inequality ‌began.

​The First Matchmaker ​(The Institution)

Having ​made people singly, ‌she made them ​continuous: Nǚwā instituted ​marriage and the ​go-between, so that ‌humanity could reproduce ​itself without her ‌kneeling by the ​river forever. She ‌is therefore prayed ​to for children ‌and for marriages ‌— the goddess ​who outsourced creation ​to love. The ​tradition names her ​among the Three ‌August Ones: progenitor, ‌legislator, and engineer ​in one.

Sources

  1. Anne Birrell, Chinese Mythology: An Introduction, Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993.
  2. Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian), Sima Qian; Chinese Text Project, 91 BCE. ↗

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

She ​pairs ‌with ‌Fúxī ​as ​sister-wife ​in ​the Han ​synthesis ‌— ​their ​intertwined ‌serpent ​bodies, ‌compass ​and ‌square ​in ​hand, ​form ​the ​canonical ‌image of ​cosmic ‌order ​— ‌and ​she ‌absorbs ​older ‌creation ​figures ​as ‌the ‌myth ‌spread ​south ​and north. ‌Modern ​readers ​note ‌her ​kinship ​with ‌every ​world-mother ‌from ​Gaia ​to ​the ​Navajo ‌Changing Woman, ‌but ​the ‌Chinese ​tradition ​keeps ‌her ‌distinct ‌signature: ‌she ​is ‌not ​just ​a ​mother ‌but a ​repairer, ​the ​goddess ‌of ​maintenance.[1]

​Within ​the ‌Chinese ​tradition, ‌closely ​related ‌names ​in ‌the ‌corpus include ​Bāguà, ​Fúxī, ​Guānyīn, ​Lóng, ‌Méngpó, ‌and ​Pángǔ.

Sources

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Her ​stones ‌became ‌literature: ​the ​great ​​novel ​Dream ‌of ​the ​Red ‌Chamber ​(Hónglóu ‌mèng) ​opens ‌with ​the ​one ​​stone ​Nǚwā ‌rejected ​for ‌the ​sky ‌— ​the ‌stone ​that ‌becomes ​​the ‌hero's ‌jade ‌and ​the ​book ‌itself. ​"Nǚwā ​mends ‌the ​sky" ​is ‌​the ‌standing ​idiom ​for ​heroic ​repair; ‌engineers, ‌surgeons, ​and ‌diplomats ​are ​all ‌‌praised ‌with ‌it. ​Temples ‌to ​her ​survive ​across ‌North ​China ​— ​‌Shexian's ​cliff-hanging ​Wahuang ​Palace ‌in ​Hebei ‌is ​the ‌grandest ​— ‌where ‌she ​​is ​still ​prayed ‌to ‌for ​children.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

The Wahuang ​Palace at ‌Shexian — ‌pavilions chained ​to a ​cliff above ​the Zhang ​River, founded ​in the ‌Northern Qi ​dynasty (6th ​century) — ‌is her ​principal surviving ‌sanctuary and ​one of ‌China's architectural ​marvels. Han ​pictorial stones ​pair her ​with Fúxī ​from Shandong ‌to Sichuan; ​the Astana ‌cemetery silk ​banner carried ‌the image ​to the ‌Silk Road's ​far end. ‌Her myth's ​textual strata ​are mapped ‌from the ‌Huáinánzǐ (139 ‌BCE) backward ​into Warring ​States fragments ‌and forward ​into the ​Tang flood-cycle ‌accounts.[1]

The ​Wahuang Palace ​at Shexian ‌(Hebei) is ​her architectural ‌marvel: pavilions ​chained to ​the sheer ​cliff of ​Phoenix Mountain, ‌founded under ‌the Northern ​Qi (6th ‌century) and ​rebuilt across ​fifteen centuries ‌— the ‌'hanging temple ‌of Empress ‌Wa,' still ​crowded at ‌her third-month ​festival. Tianshui ​in Gansu ​claims her ‌birth beside ​Fúxī's, and ​the paired ​shrines of ‌the two ​serpent-ancestors mark ​the silk-road ​towns westward. ‌Han pictorial ​stones from ‌Shandong's Wu ​Liang shrine ‌to the ​Astana cemetery ‌of Xinjiang ‌show her ​with compass ​raised beside ​her brother-husband ​— the ‌oldest surviving ‌portraits of ​the mender.

Sources

  1. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗
  2. The Shexian Wahuang Palace records; Wu Hung, The Wu Liang Shrine.

Daoist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Daoist ​registers ‌place ‌Wāhuáng ​​among ​the ​high ​antiquity ‌goddesses: ​the ​cosmological ‌treatises ​‌pair ​her ‌with ​Fúxī ​as ​the ​measure-bearers ​of ‌​the ‌first ​order, ‌and ​the ‌inner-alchemical ​tradition ‌reads ​​the ‌five-colored ‌stones ‌through ​the ​five ‌phases ​(wǔxíng) ​‌— ​her ​repair ‌of ​the ‌sky ​as ​​the ​cosmos' ‌own ‌elemental ​rebalancing. ‌Temple ​registers ​of ‌‌the ‌integrated ‌Three ​Teachings ‌give ​her ​altars ​as ‌​the ​mender-mother, ​and ‌the ​precious ​scrolls ​retell ‌the ​‌sky-mending ​as ‌a ​parable ‌of ‌restoration ​that ​the ​​Daoist ‌healing ‌liturgies ​echo.

Calligraphy & Script

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

女娲: ​nǚ ‌(女) ‌is ​the ​kneeling-woman ​graph, ​among ​the ‌oldest ​in ​‌the ​script; ‌wā ​(媧) ‌was ​made ​for ​her ​alone ​— ‌woman-radical ​plus ‌the ​phonetic ‌呙 ​— ‌a ​character ‌with ​essentially ​no ‌‌other ‌word ​to ​spell, ‌which ​is ​itself ‌a ​kind ​of ‌monument. ​Seal-script ‌forms ​of ​the ​pair ​appear ‌in ‌the ​Han ‌stone ​​tradition ‌beside ‌her ‌serpent-bodied ‌portraits, ​and ‌the ​great ​calligraphy ​of ‌the ​Wahuang ​Palace ​cliffs ‌at ​Shexian ​keeps ​her ‌name ​carved ‌where ​‌her ​temples ‌hang: ‌the ​word ​and ​the ​shrine ‌in ‌one ​stone.

Sources

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Nǚwā ​is the ‌goddess of ‌the ​second day ​— the ​one ​after the ​catastrophe. Creation, ‌in ​her myth, ​is not ‌a ​single word ‌but a ​continuous ‌mending: sky ​patched, pillars ​reset, ​people shaped ​and taught. ​She ‌asks the ​question ‌every survivor, ​every parent, ‌every ​civilization must ‌answer: the ​world ‌broke once; ​what, with ​your ‌own two ‌hands, have ‌you ​repaired?

The ​rope and ‌the ​handmade figures ​remain the ‌tradition's ​quiet self-critique: ​some are ‌shaped ​with care, ‌some flung ​from ​a rope ​— and ​the ‌goddess who ‌made both ​is ‌the one ​who mends ​the ‌sky for ‌all of ‌them. ‌Repair is ​her gospel ‌and ​her rebuke: ​the world ​will ‌break — ​pillars ​snap, heavens ​leak — ‌and ​the measure ​of the ​maker ‌is not ​the first ‌creation ​but the ‌mending. She ​is, ‌for every ‌restorer, nurse, ​and ​engineer of ​last resort, ​the ‌patron of ‌the second ​day.[1]

Sources

  1. Huainanzi, Liu An; Chinese Text Project, 139 BCE. ↗
