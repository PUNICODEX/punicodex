01

Mythology

Stories of Njord

Njǫrðr’s mythology is the peace-myth: the hostage-exchange that ended the gods’ first war, and the marriage that failed across the divine factions. His two stories — the Æsir–Vanir peace and the nine nights with Skaði — are the Norse pantheon’s domestic politics, and both end in the same lesson: some bonds hold, and some were never possible.

Gylfaginning

The Æsir–Vanir Peace

The first war in the world — Æsir against Vanir — ended not in victory but in exchange: Njǫrðr and his children went to the Æsir, and the Vanir received Hœnir and Mímir in return. Snorri notes the exchange’s unevenness (the Vanir beheaded Mímir in disgust) and its durability: Njǫrðr became the Æsir’s own sea-lord, the living treaty between the divine races.

Skáldskaparmál

The Nine Nights with Skaði

Skaði, the ski-goddess, chose her husband by his feet among the veiled gods — she reached for Baldr and got Njǫrðr. Their marriage split on geography: he could not sleep in her mountain halls, where the wolves’ howling kept him from rest; she could not sleep by the sea, where the gulls’ crying drove her mad. Nine nights in each home, then the parting — the gods’ most honest divorce.

Lokasenna

The Flyting’s Accusations

Loki’s taunting catalogue in the gods’ hall accuses Njǫrðr of fathering his children on his own sister — the Vanir custom the Æsir condemned — and of letting the hostage’s price be paid in shame. Njǫrðr’s answer is dignity itself: ‘small harm in a lover, when sisters and husbands are beyond the gods’ borders’ — the hostage’s quiet defense of his people’s ways.

Tacitus’ Nerthus

The Earth-Before-the-Sea

Tacitus’ Germania describes the North’s first-century goddess Nerthus — an earth-deity drawn in a covered wagon, feasted, and drowned in her lake in secret rites. Linguists and historians of religion long ago set Njǫrðr and Nerthus side by side: the same divine name, perhaps, before the sea claimed the land’s share — the gender and the element exchanged across the centuries.

02

The Sea, Wealth, the Vanir Hostage

God of the Shore and the Fair Wind

Njǫrðr is the Norse sea-god of the safe shore: the Vanir hostage who chose the sea-cliffs over the mountains, father of Freyr and Freyja, giver of wealth and fair winds to sailors and merchants. He is the gods’ peacemaker — the hostage-exchange that ended the Æsir–Vanir war made him the divine guarantee that treaties hold, and the sea’s temper mirrors his: calm, rich, and implacable when crossed.

Lord of the Coast

He rules the sea’s edge and the fair wind — sailors and merchants call on him for safe passage and good trade.

The Hostage of Peace

Given to the Æsir to seal the peace with the Vanir; the gods’ own guarantee that oaths bind.

Father of the Twins

Freyr and Freyja are his children by his sister — the Vanir marriage-custom the Æsir could not accept.

Giver of Wealth

Sea-wealth flows from him — the Norse word for wealth and the sea’s gift are his domain together.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Njord

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Njord is the standard Norse romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Variant: plain anglicization (no o-slash)”. The spelling uses ordinary Latin letters, so the canonical form and the ASCII form coincide.

The original script for this norse name has not yet been added to PUNICODEX. The form shown is a scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

njord

The plain njord form shares the canonical spelling. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no recovered diacritics are required; capitalization follows the proper-noun convention.

Pronunciation

How Njord was spoken

/ˈnjɔːrd/ Old Norse (normalised)
Nj- The nj is a palatalised n — “ny” as in “canyon”; the vowel å as in “or”.
-ǫrð The final ð is the soft “th” of “this” — “NYORTH”. The anglicised “Njord” keeps only the n.
The Old Norse form carries the palatal and the soft ð; the plain anglicisation is a separate lexicon entry.

Modern Approximation

“NYORTH” — palatal n, soft th ending
