The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

CLEMENTIA

The name in its original Greek form. Clementia (CLEMENTIA) is attested in the source tradition — “Mildness, mercy”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

clementia

The plain clementia form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Clementia

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Clementia. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Clementia.com → clementia.com

The non-ASCII characters in Clementia are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Clementia.

The Accent / Script Rule

Clementia is a Latin word and concept. It has no 'original script' other than the Latin alphabet. The ASCII form clementia is already the Latin spelling; the restoration capitalises the proper-noun form. Because no distinctive diacritic or special letter is added, the entry is Tier 2.

05

Mercy and Clemency

The Virtue of Restrained Power

Clementia is the Roman personification and virtue of mercy, mildness, and clemency. She represents the deliberate restraint of power: the emperor who pardons a defeated enemy, the judge who tempers justice with compassion, the victor who refuses to humiliate the vanquished. In a culture that valued severity and strength, Clementia named the rare and admirable quality of showing mercy without weakness.

Mercy

The willingness to spare those who have done wrong or been defeated.

Clemency

The moderation of punishment by those who have the power to punish severely.

Forgiveness

The release of a debtor or offender from the full weight of their debt.

Restrained Power

The distinctive Roman idea that mercy is most virtuous when power is greatest.

Sacred Symbols

Olive branch The universal sign of peace and the offer of mercy.
Patera The sacrificial bowl from which mercy is poured as a libation.
Kneeling suppliant The figure who receives clemency, emphasising the power differential mercy bridges.
Cornucopia The abundance that flows from a rule that is just but not cruel.
06

Mythology

Stories of Clementia

Clementia is not a narrative goddess with a family tree of myths; she is a personified virtue whose 'mythology' is the history of Roman political ethics. Her story is told in senatorial debates, imperial propaganda, and the coins on which emperors advertised their mercy to the empire.

Julius Caesar

The Clemency of Caesar

Julius Caesar made clementia a central virtue of his public image. After defeating his enemies in the civil war, he famously pardoned many of them, including Brutus and Cicero. The temple of Clementia was vowed in his honour. Caesar's clemency was praised by some as magnanimity and denounced by others as calculated domination — the mercy of a man who could afford to be merciful.

Imperial cult

Clementia Augusti

Under the empire, Clementia became an official virtue of the emperor. Coins and inscriptions proclaimed the *Clementia Augusti*, the mercy of Augustus and his successors. The virtue served both as a moral ideal and as propaganda: the emperor was powerful enough to destroy his enemies but chose to spare them.

Seneca

On Clemency

Seneca wrote his *De Clementia* for the young emperor Nero, arguing that clemency is the most becoming virtue of a ruler. True clemency, he insists, is not weakness but the restrained use of absolute power. The treatise is at once a philosophical essay and a work of political advice, warning Nero that cruelty destroys the very security it seeks to protect.

Christianity

Mercy and the Divine

Christian Latin adopted clementia as a name for God's mercy toward sinners. The word appears in hymns, prayers, and theological treatises, where it translates Greek eleos and Hebrew hesed. Clementia thus moved from imperial ideology to devotional language, retaining its sense of unmerited compassion shown by one who has power to punish.

Extended Lore

Clementia is the virtue of those who have power and choose not to use all of it. That choice is harder than it sounds, because power tends to justify its own extremes. To show mercy is to acknowledge that the person before you is more than their offence, more than their defeat, more than their debt.
