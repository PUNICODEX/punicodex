The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Efnysien

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Efnysien is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Double enemy”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

efnysien

The plain efnysien form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Pronunciation

How Efnysien was spoken

/ɛvˈnɪs.jɛn/ Modern Welsh
E- Short open-mid front [ɛ], as in English 'elf'; the light, unstressed first syllable.
-f- Voiced labiodental [v]: Welsh writes the v-sound with a single f — the name's first trap for English eyes.
-nis- Alveolar nasal [n], short [ɪ] (the Welsh y of a non-final syllable), and [s]; this is the stressed syllable — Welsh words carry their weight on the penult.
-ien Palatal glide [j] and short open [ɛ] closing on [n]; the final syllable falls away unstressed, 'yen' kept light.

Modern Approximation

'ev-NIS-yen' — the f sounds as v, the stress lands on the middle syllable, and the last syllable is a quick, light 'yen'.

Etymological Kin

  • Middle Welsh Efnisien, son of Euroswydd and Penarddun, in the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest
  • Modern Welsh Efnisien, the standard modern spelling
  • The named opposite Nisien, his brother — the tale presents the two as one contrast: peace-maker and strife-maker
  • English translations Efnisien (Guest), Efnysien — the same name carried into English prose

The Accent / Script Rule

Efnysien is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the Middle Welsh manuscript form is written in plain Latin letters, and normalized Welsh orthography attaches no diacritic to the name — there is nothing for the ASCII alphabet to lose. The capital alone distinguishes the temple form from the input; the figure's weight in the tradition is beside the point.

05

Trouble, Mischief

The Strife-Maker of the Second Branch, and the Cauldron He Died Breaking

Efnysien is the destroyer of the Mabinogi: the man the Second Branch introduces in a single balanced sentence — his brother Nisien could make peace between two armies at their most embittered; Efnysien could make two brothers quarrel when they loved each other best. One act of his, the maiming of the Irish king's horses, sets two kingdoms on the road to ruin; his last act, the breaking of the Cauldron of Rebirth, costs him his heart and ends the slaughter he began.

The Maimed Horses

At the wedding feast of Aberffraw, in a rage that no one had asked his consent, he cuts the lips, ears, tails, and eyelids of Matholwch's horses — the insult that compensation could not bury.

The Cauldron of Rebirth

Pair Dadeni — the vessel Brân gives in compensation: a slain man thrown in today rises tomorrow a fighting man, but speechless. Efnysien dies stretching it to pieces.

The Hundred Bags

In the great house the Irish built for Brân, a hundred armed men hide in flour-bags hung on the pillars. Efnysien walks the row, asks what is in each bag, and crushes a skull through the sacking with his fingers.

The Boy in the Fire

At the peace feast his nephew Gwern — Branwen's child, newly made king — goes from lap to lap; at Efnysien's lap he is seized and thrown into the flames. After that there is no peace to be made.

Sacred Symbols

The mutilated horses Pride made visible — an insult so literal that no payment could make the injured king forget it
The Cauldron of Rebirth (Pair Dadeni) War stripped of death's limit — the gift that became a weapon, until the one who broke it broke himself
The flour-bags on the pillars Treachery hidden in hospitality — seen through only by the man whose own mind worked that way
Gwern cast into the flames The murder of the heir at the moment of his investiture — the point past which the tale permits no return
The broken heart His only death-wound: he stretched himself inside the cauldron until it burst into four pieces, and his heart broke with it
06

Mythology

Stories of Efnysien

Efnysien belongs to one tale and fills it: the Second Branch of the Mabinogi, Branwen ferch Llŷr. The branch is the story of a compensation that failed, and Efnysien is the reason — twice over the author of the injury, and at the end the author of the only sacrifice that stopped the ruin.

Mabinogi, Second Branch

The Two Brothers

The tale introduces him by contrast. Penarddun ferch Beli, mother of Brân the Blessed, Branwen, and Manawydan by Llŷr, bore two sons to Euroswydd: Nisien and Efnysien. Of Nisien the branch says he was a good man and a gentle youth, who could bring peace between two armies at their most embittered; of Efnysien it says he would set two brothers at strife when they loved each other most. The whole Second Branch is the working-out of that one sentence.

Mabinogi, Second Branch

The Maiming of the Horses

When Brân gave his half-sister Branwen to Matholwch, king of Ireland, the wedding feast was held at Aberffraw, and the horses of the Irish king were stabled for the night. Efnysien asked why they were there, and was told they belonged to Matholwch, who had married his sister. 'Is this what they have done with a maiden as fine as her, my own sister — given her away without asking me?' In his rage he went under the horses and cut off their lips to the teeth, their ears to the head, their tails to the rumps, and their eyelids to the bone. Matholwch rose in silence and made for his ships; only Brân's embassy — Manawydan among them — and the offer of honour-price brought him back: a silver staff as tall as himself, a gold plate as broad as his face, fresh horses, and the Cauldron of Rebirth, which Brân had from Llasar Llaes Gyfnewid and Cymidei Cymeinfoll, wanderers who had fled to his court out of Ireland.

Mabinogi, Second Branch

The Hundred Bags of Flour

The compensation did not hold. In Ireland the insult was remembered, Matholwch was shamed by his own nobles, and Branwen was sent to the kitchen with a box on the ear from the butcher every day — until she tamed a starling and sent word across the sea. Brân waded the channel, for no ship could carry him, and lay across the river when the Irish destroyed the bridge: 'He who would be a leader, let him be a bridge.' The Irish sued for terms: the kingship would pass to Gwern, Branwen's son, and they would build Brân a house great enough to hold him — the first house he had ever entered. But in that house they hung a hundred bags, an armed man in each. Efnysien walked the row ahead of the feast, laid his hand on each bag — 'What is in here?' 'Flour.' — and squeezed each head through the sacking until he felt the skull give, a hundred times down the hall.

Mabinogi, Second Branch

The Boy in the Fire

At the feast of reconciliation the boy Gwern was invested as king, and — charming everyone, the branch says — he went from lap to lap among his kinsmen, from Brân to Manawydan to Nisien, until he came to Efnysien's lap. 'Why does my nephew not come to me?' Efnysien asked; and when the boy was put into his arms he seized him by the feet and threw him headlong into the great fire. Branwen would have leapt in after her son, but Brân caught her with one hand and took up his shield with the other, and the hall rose in war. It is the tale's darkest sentence, and its hinge: after Gwern, no negotiation is possible.

Mabinogi, Second Branch

The Breaking of the Cauldron

In the battle that followed, the Irish had the advantage that could not be beaten: every night they cast their slain into the Cauldron of Rebirth, and every morning the dead rose as fighting men again — speechless, but unkillable in any final sense. Then Efnysien, seeing the ruin he had made, did the one thing only he could do. He lay down among the Irish dead as if he were a corpse, was thrown into the cauldron with them, and stretched himself out inside it until the vessel burst into four pieces — and his own heart burst with it, and so he died. The branch gives him no speech of repentance and no eulogy: the destroyer ends the war his own hand started, at the price of his life, and the narration passes on. Of the hosts of Britain only seven men came home, bearing Brân's head; Branwen died of a broken heart on the shore of Anglesey and was buried in a four-sided grave on the bank of the Alaw.

Extended Lore

Efnysien is what a story looks like when it refuses to flatter anyone — including itself. The Second Branch never explains him. He is not possessed, not wronged first, not mad; the tale simply pairs him with Nisien and lets the contrast stand: what one brother binds, the other unbinds, and the world has both kinds of man in it. His evil is small in scale and total in consequence — a night with a knife in a stable, and two kingdoms bleed for a generation. That is the branch's hardest teaching about trouble: it does not need to be great, only timely, and no compensation is large enough to make an injured king forget.
