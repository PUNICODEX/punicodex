Efnysien is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the Middle Welsh manuscript form is written in plain Latin letters, and normalized Welsh orthography attaches no diacritic to the name — there is nothing for the ASCII alphabet to lose. The capital alone distinguishes the temple form from the input; the figure's weight in the tradition is beside the point.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Middle Welsh — Efnisien, son of Euroswydd and Penarddun, in the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest
  • Modern Welsh — Efnisien, the standard modern spelling
  • The named opposite — Nisien, his brother — the tale presents the two as one contrast: peace-maker and strife-maker
  • English translations — Efnisien (Guest), Efnysien — the same name carried into English prose

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Efnysien is the destroyer of the Mabinogi: the man the Second Branch introduces in a single balanced sentence — his brother Nisien could make peace between two armies at their most embittered; Efnysien could make two brothers quarrel when they loved each other best. One act of his, the maiming of the Irish king's horses, sets two kingdoms on the road to ruin; his last act, the breaking of the Cauldron of Rebirth, costs him his heart and ends the slaughter he began.

Efnysien in Later Traditions

The comparison that follows Efnysien everywhere is with [[loki|Loki]]: the malice inside the house, the sworn kin whose mischief costs the gods their peace, the trickster the tale can neither spare nor save. MacKillop and the handbooks register the parallel as typological rather than genealogical — the Mabinogi's strife-maker is a human prince of the Children of Llŷr, not a god, and his story answers to Welsh concerns: the honour-price, the insult remembered, the compensation that fails. The truer Celtic company is the cauldron-complex: the Cauldron of Rebirth belongs to the same deep stock as the Dagda's cauldron from Murias in the Lebor Gabála Érenn, from which no company went away unsatisfied, and the branch itself gives the vessel an Irish provenance through the fugitive Llasar Llaes Gyfnewid. Within the corpus his closest kin are [[nisien|Nisien]], the brother he was written as the opposite of, and [[matholwch|Matholwch]], the king his pride destroyed; [[manawydan|Manawydan]] and [[taliesin|Taliesin]] are among the seven who carried Brân's head home.

Modern Legacy

Lady Charlotte Guest's translation of the Mabinogion (1838–49) carried the Second Branch into the English-speaking world, and Efnysien's maiming of the horses and death in the cauldron became set-pieces of the Victorian Celtic revival. Evangeline Walton retold the branch at full length in The Children of Llyr (1971), the second volume of her Mabinogion tetralogy, and gave him the modern novel's inside view without softening him. His widest-reaching legacy is the cauldron itself: Lloyd Alexander drew the deathless cauldron-born warriors of his Prydain chronicles — The Black Cauldron (1965), filmed by Disney in 1985 — directly from the Welsh matter, where a cauldron that returns the slain to silent service is broken at terrible cost. In Welsh memory the branch left two landmarks: the proverb 'A fo ben, bid bont' — he who would be a leader, let him be a bridge — and Bedd Branwen, the grave by the Alaw where the tradition buried the sister his acts helped kill.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Efnysien in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Efnysien, Trouble, Mischief, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Efnysien?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Efnysien is /ɛvˈnɪs.jɛn/ — approximately 'ev-NIS-yen' — the f sounds as v, the stress lands on the middle syllable, and the last syllable is a quick, light 'yen'..

02What does Efnysien mean?

Efnysien means Double enemy in the celtic tradition.

03What are the symbols of Efnysien?

Efnysien is associated with The mutilated horses (Pride made visible — an insult so literal that no payment could make the injured king forget it), The Cauldron of Rebirth (Pair Dadeni) (War stripped of death's limit — the gift that became a weapon, until the one who broke it broke himself), The flour-bags on the pillars (Treachery hidden in hospitality — seen through only by the man whose own mind worked that way), Gwern cast into the flames (The murder of the heir at the moment of his investiture — the point past which the tale permits no return), The broken heart (His only death-wound: he stretched himself inside the cauldron until it burst into four pieces, and his heart broke with it).

04Why restore Efnysien in Unicode?

Plain ASCII efnysien strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Efnysien?

The tale introduces him by contrast. Penarddun ferch Beli, mother of Brân the Blessed, Branwen, and Manawydan by Llŷr, bore two sons to Euroswydd: Nisien and Efnysien. Of Nisien the branch says he was a good man and a gentle youth, who could bring peace between two armies at their most embittered; of Efnysien it says he would set two brothers at strife when they loved each other most. The whole Second Branch is the working-out of that one sentence.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Efnysien and related cults.
  • Efnysien is an entirely literary figure: no inscription, cult site, or votive deposit names him, and the Welsh theonymic record of the period survives in literature rather than stone. His evidence is manuscript evidence — the Second Branch complete in the White Book of Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, NLW Peniarth MSS 4–5, c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, c. 1382–1410), with the tales' composition argued to the later eleventh or early twelfth century. The geography of his story is real and walkable: Aberffraw, the royal court of Gwynedd where the horses were maimed; Harlech, where Brân's court sat; and the shore of Anglesey where the survivors landed. The tradition's one material anchor is Bedd Branwen, a cairn on the bank of the Afon Alaw in Anglesey identified since the Middle Ages with Branwen's 'four-sided grave' — excavated by Frances Lynch in 1967–68 and found to hold Early Bronze Age cremations, far older than any Mabinogi event: the identification is the folklore, not the archaeology, and it is precisely that honesty the record keeps.
