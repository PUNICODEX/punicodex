The hidden history behind Efnysien

Some restorations rescue a diacritic from the address bar. Efnysien is the other kind of temple: a name Middle Welsh already wrote in unbroken ASCII, attached to one of the darkest figures in medieval literature. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, sound, the one branch that owns him, and the scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the Mabinogi's destroyer has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII efnysien.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Efnysien (efnysien) — Trouble, Mischief · Double enemy — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Trouble, Mischief". The name's traditional gloss is "Double enemy", following MacKillop's rendering, though its etymology is genuinely debated.

Efnysien is the destroyer of the Second Branch of the Mabinogi, Branwen ferch Llŷr: half-brother of Brân the Blessed, Branwen, and Manawydan, introduced in a single balanced sentence — his brother Nisien could make peace between two armies at their most embittered; Efnysien could set two brothers at strife when they loved each other most. His one unprovoked act, the maiming of the Irish king Matholwch's horses at the wedding feast of Aberffraw, dooms two kingdoms; his last act, breaking the Cauldron of Rebirth from within, ends the slaughter at the price of his own heart.

PuniCodex restores the name as Efnysien — identical to the ASCII input, because Middle Welsh writes the name in plain Latin letters with no diacritic to recover. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the figure. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII efnysien.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Middle Welsh as Efnisien — the modern standard spelling; the temple's Efnysien carries the same name with the common Welsh i/y spelling variation — son of Euroswydd and Penarddun ferch Beli, in the Second Branch of the Mabinogi. Its etymology is one of the honest open questions of the Mabinogi scholarship, and this temple registers the debate rather than settling it.

What is secure is the literary construction. The branch pairs Efnisien with his brother Nisien as a named opposition — peace-maker and strife-maker — and the handbooks read the pair as an embodiment of contraries, with Efnisien's name traditionally glossed on the 'enemy, hostile' side of the contrast. The lexicon's gloss "Double enemy" follows MacKillop. The deeper etymology is argued, not proven; the figure, at least, lives exactly up to the hostile reading.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Middle Welsh manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Efnisien — attested in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1382–1410), recording material composed centuries earlier, most often dated by argument to the later eleventh or early twelfth century.

The rendering is straightforward because Welsh needs no rescuing here: normalized Middle Welsh orthography writes the name in plain Latin letters, and no diacritic attaches to it in any witness. What the script does carry are Welsh letter-values the ASCII eye misreads: the single f is the voiced v-sound (Welsh writes f as ff), and the y of a non-final syllable is the short i of 'pin'. The manuscript spelling, not a lost letter, is the primary witness — and it is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The pronunciation of the name in Modern Welsh is /ɛvˈnɪs.jɛn/ — Modern Welsh; the Middle Welsh value is held to be substantially the same.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: ev-NIS-yen — the f sounded as v, the stress on the middle syllable (Welsh words carry their weight on the penult), and the final syllable a quick, light 'yen'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Middle Welsh — Efnisien, son of Euroswydd and Penarddun, in the White Book and the Red Book
  • Modern Welsh — Efnisien, the standard modern spelling
  • The named opposite — Nisien, his brother: the tale's peace-maker set against its strife-maker
  • English translations — Efnisien (Guest), Efnysien — the same name carried into English prose

The first syllable is light, the f is a v, and the whole name leans forward onto the middle beat — fittingly, the syllable that names the strife.

Mythology

Efnysien's mythology is one tale — the Second Branch of the Mabinogi — and within it he is the engine of both the catastrophe and its ending.

The Two Brothers (Second Branch)

Penarddun ferch Beli bore to Llŷr the three great children — Brân the Blessed, Branwen, and Manawydan — and to Euroswydd two more sons: Nisien and Efnysien. Nisien could make peace between two armies at their most embittered; Efnysien would set two brothers at strife when they loved each other best. The whole branch is the working-out of that sentence.

The Maiming of the Horses (Second Branch)

When Brân, the crowned king of Britain and a giant no ship could hold, gave Branwen to Matholwch, king of Ireland, the feast was held at Aberffraw and the Irish horses stabled for the night. Efnysien asked whose they were, and was told they belonged to the man who had married his sister — without asking him. In his rage he went under the horses and cut off their lips to the teeth, their ears to the head, their tails to the rumps, and their eyelids to the bone. Matholwch rose in silence and made for his ships; only Brân's honour-price — a silver staff, a gold plate, fresh horses, and the Cauldron of Rebirth, which had come to him from Llasar Llaes Gyfnewid and Cymidei Cymeinfoll, fugitives out of Ireland — brought him back to the feast.

The Starling and the Crossing (Second Branch)

The compensation did not hold. In Ireland the insult was remembered, Matholwch was shamed by his own nobles, and Branwen was sent to the kitchen, the butcher boxing her ear every day — until she reared a starling, taught it speech, and sent a letter under its wing to Brân. He mustered Britain and waded the sea; when the Irish destroyed the bridge over the river, he lay down across it himself: 'He who would be a leader, let him be a bridge.'

The Hundred Bags (Second Branch)

The Irish sued for terms: the kingship would pass to Gwern, Branwen's son, and they would build Brân a house great enough to hold him — the first house he had ever entered. In that house they hung a hundred bags on the pillars, an armed man in each. Efnysien walked the row ahead of the feast, laid his hand on each bag — 'What is in here?' 'Flour.' — and crushed each skull through the sacking with his fingers. Treachery undone by the one mind that thinks in treachery.

The Boy in the Fire and the Breaking of the Cauldron (Second Branch)

At the peace feast the boy Gwern was invested as king and went, charming everyone, from lap to lap — Brân, Manawydan, Nisien — until he came to Efnysien's lap. Efnysien seized him by the feet and threw him headlong into the fire, and the hall rose in war. In the battle the Irish could not be beaten: each night they cast their slain into the Cauldron of Rebirth, and each morning the dead rose as fighting men, speechless but whole. Then Efnysien, seeing the ruin he had made, lay down among the Irish dead as a corpse, was thrown into the cauldron with them, and stretched himself out until the vessel burst into four pieces — and his heart burst with it, and so he died. The branch gives him no speech of repentance and no eulogy.

The Aftermath (Second Branch)

Ireland was wasted; of Brân's host only seven men came home — Pryderi, Manawydan, Taliesin, and four companions — bearing Brân's head, for the king had taken a poisoned spear in the foot and commanded his own beheading. Branwen died of a broken heart at Aber Alaw and was buried in a four-sided grave on the riverbank; the head went at last to the White Mount in London, buried facing France as a ward against invasion, until Arthur dug it up to defend the island by his own strength alone.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Efnysien is narrative rather than cultic — the branch fixed around him a small set of images no retelling can omit:

  • The maimed horses — pride made visible: an insult so literal that no payment could make the injured king forget it
  • The Cauldron of Rebirth (Pair Dadeni) — the gift that became a weapon: war stripped of death's limit, until the man who breaks it breaks himself
  • The hundred flour-bags — treachery hidden in hospitality, seen through only by the guest whose own mind worked that way
  • The fire and the child — Gwern cast into the flames at the moment of his investiture: the point past which the tale permits no return
  • The broken heart — his only death-wound, self-dealt inside the cauldron he burst

Archaeology & Evidence

Efnysien is an entirely literary figure: no inscription names him, no cult site claims him, and the Welsh theonymic record of the period survives in literature rather than stone. His evidence is manuscript evidence — the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1382–1410), with earlier fragments in Peniarth 6. The geography of his story is real and walkable: Aberffraw, the royal court of Gwynedd; Harlech, where Brân's court sat; the Anglesey shore where the survivors landed. The tradition's one material anchor is Bedd Branwen, a cairn on the bank of the Afon Alaw identified since the Middle Ages with Branwen's 'four-sided grave' — excavated by Frances Lynch in 1967–68 and found to hold Early Bronze Age cremations, far older than any Mabinogi event. The identification is the folklore, not the archaeology, and the record is the more valuable for keeping the two distinct.

Realm & Domain

Efnysien's domain is Trouble, Mischief — and the sources earn both terms, and then go past them.

Strife-Making

His defining act needs no army and no grievance: a night with a knife in a stable, and two kingdoms bleed for a generation. The branch's hardest teaching about trouble is that it does not need to be great, only timely.

The Sight That Sees Traps

In Ireland it is Efnysien alone who reads the great house correctly — a hundred flour-bags, a hundred armed men — and walks the row crushing skulls. The tale knows what such a mind is for, even as it cannot forgive what such a mind does.

The Unforgivable Act

Gwern in the fire is the point of no return: after the heir is murdered from his own uncle's lap, no negotiation is possible, and the branch stops pretending otherwise.

Destruction Turned on Destruction

His last act is the only constructive one of his life, and the Welsh text declines to call it redemption. The cauldron broke and his heart broke; the narration passes on. The destroyer ends the war his own hand started, at the price of everything he has.

Across Cultures

The comparison that follows Efnysien everywhere is with Loki: the malice inside the kin-group, the trickster the tale can neither spare nor save. The handbooks register the parallel as typological rather than genealogical — the Mabinogi's strife-maker is a human prince, not a god, and his story answers to Welsh legal concerns: the honour-price, the insult remembered, the compensation that fails. The truer Celtic company is the cauldron-complex: the Cauldron of Rebirth belongs to the same deep stock as the cauldron of the Dagda from Murias in the Lebor Gabála Érenn, and the branch itself gives the vessel an Irish provenance through the fugitive Llasar. Within the corpus his closest kin are Nisien, the brother he was written as the opposite of, and Manawydan, the brother who survived to carry the head home.

Cultural Legacy

Lady Charlotte Guest's translation of the Mabinogion (1838–49) carried the Second Branch into the English-speaking world, and Efnysien's maiming of the horses and death in the cauldron became set-pieces of the Victorian Celtic revival. Evangeline Walton retold the branch at full length in The Children of Llyr (1971), giving the destroyer a modern novel's interior view without softening him. His widest-reaching legacy is the cauldron itself: Lloyd Alexander drew the deathless cauldron-born warriors of his Prydain chronicles — above all The Black Cauldron (1965), filmed by Disney in 1985 — directly from the Welsh matter, where a vessel that returns the slain to silent service can be stopped only from within, at terrible cost. And in Welsh memory the branch left the proverb 'A fo ben, bid bont' — he who would be a leader, let him be a bridge.

The Scholarly Record

The account of Efnysien given in this edition rests on the Mabinogi in its modern editions and translations — Sioned Davies's Oxford translation and Ifor Williams's standard Welsh text — with Mac Cana's Branwen Daughter of Llŷr (1958) as the foundational study of the branch's composition and Irish affinities, Mac Cana's The Mabinogi for the wider frame, Koch's Celtic Culture and MacKillop's Dictionary of Celtic Mythology for the name and its gloss, the Geiriadur Prifysgol Cymru for the Welsh record, and Frances Lynch's excavation report for Bedd Branwen.

A Meditation

Efnysien is what a story looks like when it refuses to flatter anyone — including itself. The Second Branch never explains him: not possessed, not wronged first, not mad. It pairs him with Nisien and lets the contrast stand — what one brother binds, the other unbinds, and the world has both kinds of man in it. And then the ending, which no one is allowed to call redemption: the destroyer lies down among the enemy dead to be thrown into the cauldron with them, and buys the war's end with the one coin he has. The Mabinogi does not promise that the man who sets the fire can put it out. It shows him choosing to burn with it.

The Unicode Restoration

Efnysien is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Middle Welsh Efnisien carries none of these. The manuscripts write plain Latin letters, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the figure; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.
