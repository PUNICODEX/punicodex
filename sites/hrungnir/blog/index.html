The hidden history behind Hrungnir

Some names in the lexicon arrived famous; Hrungnir arrived armed. He is the strongest of the jötnar, the stone-hearted giant who wagered his head that his gold-maned stallion could outrun Sleipnir, drank Thor's liquor in the hall of the gods, and died at Grjótúnagarðar in the most fully narrated single combat of the whole Norse corpus. Yet his name is a philological blank — no agreed etymology, no diacritic, no distinctive letter, plain ASCII from the manuscript to the keyboard. There is nothing to restore and a great deal to explain. This post walks the full record — name, runes, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the strongest giant of the North has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII hrungnir.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Hrungnir (hrungnir) — Giant, Duel with Thor · Brawler — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Giant, Duel with Thor". The name's traditional project gloss is "Brawler", though its actual etymology is one of the corpus's open questions.

Hrungnir is the strongest of the jötnar: heart of stone, three-cornered and sharp-edged, head of stone, shield of stone. His story is a single arc — the wager of heads over Gullfaxi's speed, the drunken boasting in Valhöll, the appointed duel at Grjótúnagarðar, the whetstone shattered against Mjöllnir, and the strangest aftermath in the corpus, when all the Æsir together cannot lift the dead giant's foot off Thor's neck and a three-night-old god does it alone.

PuniCodex restores the name as Hrungnir — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the giant. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII hrungnir.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in normalized Old Norse as Hrungnir (nominative; genitive Hrungnis), and its etymology is an honest blank. De Vries registers it without a secure derivation; Simek and Orchard repeat the verdict — unexplained. The PuniCodex lexicon's gloss, 'Brawler', is a project rendering of the figure's role, not a claimed derivation: the strongest of the giants, remembered entirely for the fight he picked and lost. This temple reports the gloss and the uncertainty together, because that is what the record holds.

The final -r is the Old Norse masculine nominative ending — and here it survives even in the ASCII form, which needs no repair. The letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • r → r — Same

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᚼᚱᚢᚴᚾᛁᛦ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which it would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs h-r-u-k-n-i-R.

That detail matters in the usual way: the runic row underspecifies what the manuscripts fix. The ᚴ rune writes both k and g, so the carving alone could not tell you the medial stop is voiced, and ᛦ carries the final nominative -r. No Viking-Age carving of the name is known — the rendering is a normalized reconstruction, and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Prose Edda, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈhruŋɡ.nir/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: HRUNG-nir — a breathy h rolled straight into the r, a short 'oo' as in 'put', both the ng and the g sounded as in 'finger', and a trill at the end.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Hrungnir, strongest of the jötnar; no agreed etymology
  • Younger Futhark — ᚼᚱᚢᚴᚾᛁᛦ, the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Skaldic attestation — Hrungnir, named in Þjóðólfr of Hvin's Haustlöng (early 10th c.), the name's oldest sustained witness

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. The initial hr- cluster is genuine — Old Norse kept the aspirate that English later silenced in words like 'ring' — and the medial -ng- sounds both its nasal and its stop.

Mythology

Hrungnir's mythology is a single arc — wager, boast, duel, aftermath — told once at length in Skáldskaparmál and vouched for by the oldest mythological shield-poem we have.

The Wager of Heads (Skáldskaparmál 17)

Riding Sleipnir through Jötunheimr, Óðinn meets the giant Hrungnir, who asks who the gold-helmed rider is and praises the horse. Óðinn answers with the stake that decides everything: he wagers his head that no horse in Jötunheimr is Sleipnir's equal. Hrungnir springs to his own stallion Gullfaxi, the gold-maned, and gives chase in a fury — and, carried past his own turning, gallops through the gates of Ásgarðr itself and in among the gods.

Drunk in the Hall of the Gods

The Æsir receive their uninvited guest with grim hospitality: they set before him the great horns Thor drank from, and Hrungnir empties them one after another. Drunk, he lets the boasts out — he will carry Valhǫll off to Jötunheimr, sink Ásgarðr, kill every god save Freyja and Sif, whom he will carry home, and drink all the mead himself. The Æsir name Thor. Þórr comes in swinging his hammer, demanding who gave jötnar leave to drink in Valhǫll — and Hrungnir, standing under Óðinn's safe-conduct, claims the rights of an unarmed man: little honour in killing him as he is. Let Thor dare single combat instead, at the frontier, at Grjótúnagarðar. Thor, Snorri says, would by no means refuse.

The Whetstone and the Hammer (Skáldskaparmál 17–19)

The giants make much of the appointment — Hrungnir is their strongest, and they expect the outcome to decide everything. For his second they build a champion of clay: Mǫkkurkálfi, nine leagues tall and three leagues broad across the chest, with no heart big enough for the frame — so they give him a mare's heart, and it quakes within him when Thor comes. Hrungnir stands with his stone heart, stone head, and stone shield, holding his hone. Þjálfi runs ahead and calls out that Thor is coming from below, and the giant sets the shield beneath his feet and stands on it. Thor comes in lightning and hurls Mjöllnir from afar; Hrungnir throws the hone with both hands, and the weapons meet in the air. The hone bursts: one half falls to earth — from it come all the whetstone rocks in the world — and one half drives into Thor's head, and he falls forward. But the hammer does not stop: it crushes Hrungnir's skull, and the giant falls forward across Thor, his foot on the god's neck. Þjálfi goes at Mǫkkurkálfi, and the clay giant falls with little glory.

The Foot No God Could Lift

Then the strangest aftermath in the corpus: Thor lies pinned, and neither he nor Þjálfi can shift the dead giant's foot. All the Æsir come, and together they cannot lift it. At last comes Magni, Thor's son by the giantess Járnsaxa — three nights old — and throws the foot off his father, saying it is a pity he came so late: he thinks he would have struck the giant dead with his fist. Thor gives him Gullfaxi for the deed, and Óðinn objects that the fine horse has gone to a giantess's son rather than to his own father.

Gróa's Forgotten Spell

The hone stays in Thor's head. The völva Gróa, wife of Aurvandill the Bold, comes to sing it loose with her spells, and the stone is already working free — until Thor, to please her, tells how he carried Aurvandill out of Jötunheimr in a basket, and how the man's frozen toe, broken off and thrown into the sky, became the star called Aurvandill's Toe. Gróa rejoices so greatly at the news that she forgets her spells; the hone stirs in Thor's head to this day. Therefore, Snorri warns, whetstones must never be thrown across the floor.

The Skaldic Witness (Haustlöng)

Snorri names his source, and the source is remarkable: Þjóðólfr of Hvin's Haustlöng, a shield-poem composed in the early tenth century, among the oldest mythological verse we have. The combat, the shaking earth, the whetstone, and the fall are all there in dense dróttkvætt — proof that the story was already old and fixed three centuries before Snorri wrote it down, and that a painted shield could carry it as court art within the lifetime of the pagan North.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Hrungnir is the iconography of stone — the corpus dresses him in his own geology:

  • The whetstone — his hone, hurled with both hands against Mjöllnir: the stone whose purpose is sharpening other things, shattered on the first iron it met; its fallen half fathers every hone-rock, its flying half still sits in Thor's skull
  • Gullfaxi — the gold-maned stallion whose speed was worth a giant's head, and which ends the story in the hands of a three-night-old god
  • The three-cornered stone heart — courage of the wrong material: hard, sharp-edged, and entirely without flexibility
  • Mǫkkurkálfi — the nine-league clay champion with a mare's heart: the archetype of size without courage
  • The fallen foot — the leg across Thor's neck that all the Æsir together could not shift: the weight of the foe after the foe is dead

Archaeology & Evidence

Hrungnir is an entirely textual figure: no runestone names him, no votive deposit invokes him, and no picture stone can be securely tied to the duel. The material witness is instead a poem about an object — Haustlöng was composed for a painted shield, a Viking-Age prestige object bearing mythic scenes, and the stanzas Snorri preserves are our evidence that the Hrungnir combat belonged to that visual repertoire in the early tenth century. The myth's own materiality is geological: Snorri derives every hone-rock in the world from the whetstone's fallen half, and the household taboo on thrown whetstones carried the story into Scandinavian folk practice. The literary witnesses hang by the usual threads: Skáldskaparmál in the Codex Regius of the Prose Edda and its sister manuscripts, and the Haustlöng stanzas surviving nowhere but in Snorri's quotation.

Realm & Domain

Hrungnir's domain is Giant, Duel with Thor — and the sources earn both halves of it.

The Giant

Snorri's formula is that Hrungnir was the strongest of all the jötnar, with heart, head, and shield of stone: the mountainside given intent, a giant made of his own weapon's material.

The Duel

The combat at Grjótúnagarðar is the corpus's most complete single fight: appointed ground, seconds, terms, and a throw-for-throw exchange of missiles narrated like a formal hólmganga between worlds.

The Wager

Before the duel comes the race — Óðinn's head against Hrungnir's pride on the question of a horse, the boast that carries the giant past his own frontier and into the hall of the gods.

The Aftermath

The myth keeps its strangest weight for after the killing: the foot no god could lift, until three-night-old Magni threw it off and rode home on Gullfaxi.

Across Cultures

Hrungnir resists the usual syncretic moves. The temptation to read him as a reflex of the Indo-European chaos-combat — a stone-bodied being struck down by the thunderer's missile, on the Vedic Vṛtra pattern — has been tried, but the fit is partial: he steals nothing, hoards nothing, and holds back no waters. His truer company is within the corpus itself, beside Thor's other named giant-fights — Geirröðr, killed by a glowing iron thrown back at its sender, and the near-fatal fishing for the World Serpent. The shard lodged in Thor's skull has been compared with the embedded, unremovable weapons of other traditions, but no source demonstrates a borrowing; the handbooks register Hrungnir as a distinctively northern invention, a giant built of the landscape the North knew: stone, clay, and weather. Within the pantheon, his closest kin are the figures his fight sets in motion: Þórr, who wins the duel and keeps the wound, and Magni, who inherits the horse.

Cultural Legacy

Hrungnir's afterlife runs through the skalds before it runs through anyone else: Haustlöng made his fall one of the foundation-texts of Old Norse poetics, and Snorri's quotation of it as documentary proof is an unusually transparent piece of medieval source-criticism. The myth's debris entered folklore — the aetiology of the hone-rocks and the taboo on thrown whetstones survived in Scandinavian folk collections as thunder-lore long after the god faded. In modern reception, Hrungnir is the template for every stone-hearted colossus the fantasy genre has built since: games and novels reach for his duel whenever a hero must fight a mountain that can throw back. His name never entered living use the way Baldr's or Thor's did; it remains exactly what the Edda made it — the name of the strongest thing the hammer ever had to kill.

Skáldskaparmál 17–19 supplies the narrative; Þjóðólfr's Haustlöng vouches for its age; Cleasby–Vigfusson and Zoëga secure the name's form; de Vries, Simek, Orchard, and Lindow register the unresolved etymology and arbitrate the combat's place in the corpus.

A Meditation

Hrungnir is the Edda's essay on confidence without flexibility. Every part of him is the hardest available material — heart of stone, head of stone, shield of stone — and the myth's arithmetic is that hardness is not the same as strength. The whetstone is the perfect weapon for him: a stone whose entire purpose is to sharpen other things, hurled once and shattered on the first iron it meets. Against him the story sets two counter-images: Mǫkkurkálfi, nine leagues of clay around a mare's heart, the warning about size without courage; and Magni, three nights old, lifting what all the Æsir together could not, the warning about measuring strength by age, rank, or expectation. Hrungnir wagered his head on a horse race and lost it in a duel. The name survives, etymology unknown, as the corpus's permanent reminder that being the strongest thing in the room is a wager too — and someone always holds the hammer.

The Unicode Restoration

Hrungnir is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Norse Hrungnir carries none of these. The manuscripts write eight plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the giant; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.
