ASCII Constraint

The plain hrungnir form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Hrungnir

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Hrungnir. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Pronunciation

How Hrungnir was spoken

/ˈhruŋɡ.nir/ Old Norse Reconstruction
Hr- Aspirate [h] running straight into the trilled [r] — an initial cluster Old Norse kept but English dropped, as in hringr 'ring' versus its silent-h descendants.
-u- Short close back rounded [u], the vowel of 'put', held brief between the r and the nasal.
-ng- Velar nasal [ŋ] plus a fully sounded stop [g] — Old Norse sounded both, as in English 'finger', never the bare nasal of 'singer'.
-nir Second [n], short [i], and the final nominative -r, lightly trilled — the masculine agent ending that marks him as a doer.

Modern Approximation

'HRUNG-nir' — begin with a breathy h, roll the r straight into a short 'oo' as in 'put', sound both the ng and the g as in 'finger', and trill the final r.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Hrungnir, the strongest of the jötnar; no secure cognate or agreed etymology survives in the handbooks.
  • Younger Futhark ᚼᚱᚢᚴᚾᛁᛦ (h-r-u-k-n-i-R), the Viking-Age carving of the name; ᚴ writes both k and g, and ᛦ the final nominative -r.
  • Skaldic attestation Hrungnir, named and fought in Þjóðólfr of Hvin's Haustlöng (early 10th c.), the oldest sustained witness to the duel.

The Accent / Script Rule

Hrungnir is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The name's obscurity is a statement about etymology, never about the giant: the handbooks register Hrungnir's origin as uncertain while his duel with Þórr is among the best-attested single combats in the corpus.

05

Giant, Duel with Thor

The Stone Heart, the Golden Mane, and the Wager of a Head

Hrungnir is the strongest of the jötnar — the stone-hearted giant who rode his gold-maned stallion Gullfaxi into Ásgarðr on a wager of heads, drank Thor's liquor from the god's own horns, boasted he would carry off Valhǫll, and met Mjǫllnir at Grjótúnagarðar with nothing in his hand but a whetstone. His duel is the most fully narrated single combat between god and giant in the Old Norse corpus.

Gullfaxi

'Golden mane' — the stallion Óðinn envied aloud, whose speed Hrungnir wagered his head against Sleipnir to disprove.

The Whetstone

His weapon of stone — hurled against Mjǫllnir, it shattered in mid-air; one shard fathered every whetstone rock on earth, one lodged in Thor's skull.

The Stone Heart

Three-cornered, sharp-edged, and hard — the Edda says his heart, head, and shield were all of stone, a giant made of his own weapon's material.

Grjótúnagarðar

The 'stone-fence-yard' at the edge of heaven and giantland, where the single combat was appointed — the oldest named duelling-ground in the myths.

Sacred Symbols

The whetstone The honing-stone hurled against the hammer — stone against iron, and the shard still buried in Thor's head
Gullfaxi The gold-maned stallion whose speed was worth a giant's head — and ended in the hands of three-night-old Magni
The three-cornered stone heart Courage of the wrong material: resolve that is hard, sharp, and entirely without flexibility
Mǫkkurkálfi The nine-league clay giant with a mare's heart — the giants' engineered champion, terror to look at and nothing within
The fallen foot Hrungnir's leg across Thor's neck, which all the Æsir together could not lift — the weight of the foe after the foe is dead
06

Mythology

Stories of Hrungnir

Hrungnir has essentially one story, and it is the greatest fight in the Edda: the wager, the drunken boasting in the hall of the gods, the appointed duel at Grjótúnagarðar, the shattered whetstone, and the fall that left even victorious Thor pinned beneath his enemy's leg. Snorri tells it in Skáldskaparmál, and the skald Þjóðólfr of Hvin had carved it into verse three centuries earlier.

Skáldskaparmál 17

The Wager of Heads

It begins with a boast Óðinn could not keep to himself. Riding Sleipnir through Jötunheimr, he met the giant Hrungnir, who asked who this man was with the golden helmet, riding so well over sea and air, and praised the horse. Óðinn answered with the stake that decides everything after: he wagered his head that no horse in Jötunheimr was Sleipnir's equal. Hrungnir sprang to his own stallion Gullfaxi, the gold-maned, and gave chase in a fury. The race ran to the very gates of Ásgarðr — and Hrungnir, carried past his own turning, galloped through them and in among the gods.

Skáldskaparmál 17

Drunk in the Hall of the Gods

The Æsir received him with the hospitality due an uninvited guest: they set before him the great horns Thor himself drank from, and Hrungnir emptied them one after another. Drunk, he let the boasts out — he would take Valhǫll up and carry it off to Jötunheimr, he would sink Ásgarðr, he would kill all the gods, save only Freyja and Sif, whom he would carry home; and the mead, he said, he would drink up all by himself. The Æsir, tired of the noise, named Thor; and Thor came in swinging his hammer, demanding who had given jötnar leave to drink in Valhǫll. Hrungnir, looking at him with anything but friendly eyes, answered that Óðinn had invited him and he stood under Óðinn's protection — then claimed his rights: Thor would gain little honour from killing him unarmed. It would be a greater trial of courage, he said, if Thor dared meet him in single combat at the frontier, at Grjótúnagarðar. And Thor, Snorri says, would by no means refuse a challenge to single combat.

Skáldskaparmál 17–19

Grjótúnagarðar: The Whetstone and the Hammer

The giants made much of the appointment, for Hrungnir was the strongest of them and they expected one outcome or the other to be decisive. For his second they built a champion of clay: Mǫkkurkálfi, nine leagues tall and three leagues broad across the chest, with no heart big enough for the frame — so they gave him a mare's heart, and it quaked within him when Thor came. Hrungnir stood with his heart of stone, three-cornered and sharp-edged, his head of stone, and his broad shield of stone, holding his hone — the whetstone — before him. Þjálfi ran ahead and called out that Thor was coming from below, and Hrungnir, fearing a strike through the earth, set the shield beneath his feet and stood on it.

Then Thor came in an ásmegin-fury, lightning running before him, and hurled Mjǫllnir from afar. Hrungnir threw the hone with both hands, and the weapons met in the air. The hone burst in two: one half fell to earth, and from it come all the whetstone rocks in the world; the other half drove into Thor's head, and he fell forward. But Mjǫllnir did not stop — it struck Hrungnir's head and crushed the skull to small pieces, and the giant fell forward across Thor, his foot lying on Thor's neck. Þjálfi went at Mǫkkurkálfi, and the clay giant fell with little glory.

Skáldskaparmál 19

The Foot No God Could Lift

Then the strangest aftermath in the corpus: Thor lay pinned, and neither he nor Þjálfi could shift the giant's foot from his neck. All the Æsir came, when they learned, and together they could not lift it. At last came Magni, son of Thor and the giantess Járnsaxa — three nights old — and threw Hrungnir's foot off his father, saying it was a pity he had come so late: he thought he would have struck the giant dead with his fist. Thor gave him Gullfaxi for the deed, and Óðinn objected that he had given the fine horse to a giantess's son rather than to his own father. The hone stayed in Thor's head. The völva Gróa, wife of Aurvandill the Bold, came to sing it loose with her spells, and the stone was already working free — until Thor, to please her, told how he had carried Aurvandill in a basket out of Jötunheimr, and how the man's frozen toe, broken off and thrown into the sky, became the star called Aurvandill's Toe. Gróa rejoiced so greatly at the news that she forgot her spells; the hone stirs in Thor's head to this day. Therefore, Snorri warns, whetstones must never be thrown across the floor.

Haustlöng

The Skaldic Witness

Snorri quotes his sources, and for this myth the source is Þjóðólfr of Hvin's Haustlöng — a shield-poem composed in the early tenth century, among the oldest mythological verse we have. Þjóðólfr tells the whole combat in dense dróttkvætt: the 'defier of the gods' who rode to the fight, the earth shaking beneath the giant's host, the whetstone bitten from the hard stone of the heart, the hammer answering. That a skald within living memory of the Viking Age's pagan courts could assume his hearers knew the whetstone, the clay giant, and the fall proves the story was not Snorri's construction: it was already old, already fixed, already the standard account of the hardest fight Thor ever fought.

Extended Lore

Hrungnir is the Edda's essay on confidence without flexibility. Every part of him is the hardest available material — heart of stone, head of stone, shield of stone — and the myth's arithmetic is that hardness is not the same as strength. The whetstone is the perfect weapon for him: a stone whose entire purpose is to sharpen other things, hurled once, shattering on the first iron it meets. Against him the Edda sets two counter-images. Mǫkkurkálfi, the engineered champion, enormous and empty, a mare's heart quaking in nine leagues of clay — the warning about size without courage. And Magni, three nights old, lifting what all the gods together could not — the warning about measuring strength by age, rank, or expectation. Hrungnir wagered his head on a horse race and lost it in a duel. The name survives, etymology unknown, as the corpus's permanent reminder that being the strongest thing in the room is a wager too — and someone always holds the hammer.
