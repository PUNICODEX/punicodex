Ebisu: the stories first, then the spelling

You cannot weigh an accent without knowing what it carries. Here is what Ebisu has been carrying.

The Stories That Carry the Name

Hiruko, the Leech Child. The Kojiki tells that Izanagi and Izanami's first child, Hiruko, was born malformed and set adrift in a boat of reeds. He grew up to become associated with the sea and with outsiders, and later tradition identified him with Ebisu. His survival from abandonment makes him a god of those who make their own way.

Kotoshironushi. Kotoshironushi, the son of Ōkuninushi, was a god of oracles and fishing who surrendered the land to Amaterasu's descendants and then withdrew to the sea. Some traditions merge him with Ebisu, deepening the connection between the fortune god and the maritime realm.

Ebisu the Smiling Merchant. By the Edo period, Ebisu had become the patron of shops, fish markets, and the common trades. His image — smiling, holding a fishing rod and a sea bream — was placed near cash registers and shop entrances. The Toka Ebisu festival in Ōsaka remains one of Japan's largest celebrations of commerce and good fortune.

And Here Is What the Spelling Preserves

Against those stories, the attestation: 恵比寿, on the evidence Kojiki (Hiruko and Kotoshironushi episodes) and Nihon Shoki (land-transfer narratives) set down. The name means "The foreigner, straggler".

The Name Travels

Ebisu is a composite. His roots are in the Kojiki figures Hiruko and Kotoshironushi, but his popular form was shaped by merchant culture and the cult of the Seven Gods of Fortune.
