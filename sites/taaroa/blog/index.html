Before There Was Anything ​(The Self-Existent)

The old scholars of Tahiti told the missionary John Davies — and Teuira Henry heard it again two generations later — that before there was sky, sea, land, light, or any ​other being, Taaroa was. He had no father and no mother, for nothing existed before him that could beget him; his cult name Ta'aroa-'onohi-'ote-na'i-'atu means 'Ta'aroa, the self-existing, the origin of all things.' ‌He dwelt in the primal darkness called Te Pō, wrapped in his own shell like a chick in the egg, calling into the void — and no one answered, because there was ​no one else to answer. The whole of the world-to-come lay folded inside him, as the tree lies folded in its seed, and the first act of the cosmos was that loneliness outgrew ‌its shell. (Davies' account, preserved in Ellis, Polynesian Researches I; Henry, Ancient Tahiti 336–338.) The myth makes the first moment of existence a soliloquy: creation begins as a being who has no one ​else to speak to, speaking himself into company.

Sky from the Upper Half, Earth from the Lower (The Shell)

Weary of confinement, Ta'aroa broke his shell open. The upper half of it he ‌raised high above him and made the sky; the lower half he spread out beneath him and made the earth. From himself he brought forth the sea, the springs, the rocks and ​sands, the trees and plants, the fish and birds, and the land animals — the whole inventory of the world drawn, item by item, out of his own being, nothing borrowed and nothing ‌left over. In Henry's version he then turned to its government, which is where the third myth begins. (Henry, Ancient Tahiti 336–349; Ellis, Polynesian Researches I.) The image is precise and strange: the ​cosmos is not built from outside materials but hatched — the world as the broken shell of a god who could no longer bear to be alone.

The Maker of Gods and of ‌Men (Progenitor)

In the fuller tellings, Ta'aroa did not keep the world to himself. He fashioned the other great gods — Tane of the forests and birds, Rono of peace and cultivated ​food, Tū of war and of humankind, Ta'ata, and the young warrior god 'Oro who would inherit the great national marae — and set each over his portion of creation. He then made ​men and women to people the earth he had spread. The sources are early and do not always agree on the details — Davies' version, gathered in 1818, is sober where Henry's, drawn ‌from priestly families of Papeari, is expansive — but all agree on the order: gods first, then people, and Ta'aroa before and above both. (Davies in Ellis, Polynesian Researches I; Henry, Ancient Tahiti.) ‌The theology is remarkably austere: a single unbegotten power delegates everything, and rules by having made the rulers.
