Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Baugi (baugi) ​— Giant, Brother ‌of Suttungr ‌· the Keeper ​of the Door ​at Hnitbjǫrg — ​is the ​jötunn who makes ​the mead of ‌poetry's theft possible: ​the brother ​of Suttungr, master ‌of the nine ​thralls whose deaths ‌open the ​labour bargain, and ‌the wavering accomplice ​who bores the ​tunnel into ​the mountain where ​the mead is ​hoarded.[1]

The name ‌is usually ​connected with Old ‌Norse baugr ‘ring, ​circlet’ — hence ‌the traditional ​gloss ‘ring-shaped’ — ‌though the derivation ​is not secure, ‌and Simek ​registers the etymology ​as uncertain.[6] Baugi's ‌attestation is exactly ‌as wide ‌as his story: ​he appears in ​Snorri's Skáldskaparmál, in ‌the mead-of-poetry ​narrative, and in ​the þulur of ‌giant-names — a ​figure the ​tradition needed once, ‌at the one ​door.[3] In that ‌one appearance ​he is the ​story's hinge: the ​labour-broker who contracts ​with the ‌disguised Óðinn, the ‌man who honestly ​names his limits ‌— ‘the ​mead is Suttungr's, ​not mine’ — ‌and then sells ‌access to ‌it anyway, and ‌the would-be betrayer ​whose auger-stroke misses ‌the crawling ​snake by an ​inch.[1]

PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Baugi — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, because ​normalized Old Norse ‌writes no ​diacritic in this ​name; the mechanical ​tier rule therefore ‌places it ​in Tier 2, ‌a statement about ​orthography and never ‌about the ​figure. The temple ‌is domain-less: the ‌plain-ASCII baugi.com is ​held outside ​the collection and ​watched for release, ​and no form ‌of the ‌name is claimed ​as owned.[4]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Baugi, ​a masculine weak-form ​giant's name ​known from Skáldskaparmál's ​narrative and the ​þulur of ‌jǫtna heiti.[1] Its ​etymology is ​genuinely unsettled. The ‌standard suggestion ​connects it with ‌baugr ‘ring, ​circlet, coiled shape’ ‌— the ​word that also ​stands in baugeiðr ​‘ring-oath’ — ​yielding a gloss ​along the ‌lines of ‘ring-shaped’ ​or ‘the ‌coiled one’; Cleasby–Vigfusson ​and Zoëga ‌document the underlying ​noun, while ‌Simek treats the ​name's derivation as ‌uncertain rather ​than solved.[2] The ​resonance is ‌worth registering without ‌over-pressing: in ‌Hávamál's own telling ​of the ​mead theft, the ‌instrument Óðinn ​breaks is precisely ​a baugeiðr, a ‌ring-oath — ​the god swears ​by the ‌ring and leaves ​Suttungr defrauded.[3]

‌The ASCII form ​is not ​a fallback but ​the original: ​the medieval manuscripts ‌write five plain ‌Latin letters, ​and no historically ‌distinct Unicode ​spelling exists to ​restore. The ‌mechanical rule therefore ‌classifies the ‌name Tier 2 ‌— nothing ​distinctive is lost ‌in transit.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • b ​→ B ‌— Same, capitalized

​- a ​→ a — ​Same

  • ‌u → u ​— Same

​- g → ​g — Same
‌- i ​→ i — ‌Same

No ​domain is held. ‌The plain-ASCII ​baugi.com is registered ‌to a ‌third party outside ​the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release; ​this temple ‌claims no address ‌it does ​not hold.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈbɑu.ɣi/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

  • ​B- — ​Voiced bilabial ‌stop [b], fully ​voiced initially, ​as in ‌English ‘bow’
  • ​-au- — ‌The falling ​diphthong [ɑu], open ‌back vowel ​gliding to ​rounded u; close ​to the ​‘ow’ of ​English ‘cow’
  • ‌-g- — ​Voiced velar ‌fricative [ɣ] between ​vowels, not ‌a stop; ​before front i ‌it palatalizes ​toward a ‌glide close to ​English ‘y’

​- -i ‌— Short unstressed ‌[i] in ‌the final ​syllable

For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest ​approximation is: ‘BOW-yee’ ​— first ‌syllable rhyming ​with ‘cow’, the ​g softened ‌to a ​glide so ‌the second syllable ​lands almost ​like ‘yee’.

​Kindred and historical ​forms of ‌the name:

‌- Old Norse ​— Baugi, ‌brother of ​Suttungr, in Skáldskaparmál ​and the ‌þulur

  • ‌Younger Futhark — ‌ᛒᛅᚢᚴᛁ (b-au-k-i), ‌the normalized ​runic rendering; the ‌k-rune ᚴ ​writes the ​g and no ​rune marks ‌the medial ​fricative
  • Word-kin ​— baugr ​‘ring’ and ‌baugeiðr ‘ring-oath’, the ​nouns to ​which the ​name is usually ‌connected

Baugi ​is Tier ‌2 because the ​restoration preserves ‌nothing ASCII ​loses: no diacritic, ‌no distinctive ‌letter, no ​vowel length. The ​five letters ​of the ​manuscripts are the ‌five letters ‌of the ​address bar.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Baugi ​— Old Norse ​(North Germanic), ‌attested in ​the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Prose ​Edda (GKS ‌2367 4to, ​early 14th c.) ‌and the ​Codex Regius ​of the ​Poetic Edda ​(c. 1270), ​recording material ‌centuries older.[1] Before ​the Latin ‌bookhand, Old ​Norse names ‌were carved ​in the ‌younger futhark; ​no runic attestation ‌of this ​name survives, ​so the ‌manuscript form ‌is the ‌primary witness.

​The scholarly ​rendering proceeds step ‌by step:

​- The ​name is ‌a giant's ​proper name ​of uncertain ‌etymology, usually connected ​with baugr ‌‘ring, circlet’; ​Simek registers ​the derivation ​as unsettled.[2]
​- Old ‌Norse orthography writes ‌it in ​five plain ‌letters: B-a-u-g-i, ​with no ​length mark ‌and no ‌special character.
‌- In the ‌Younger Futhark ​it would ‌run ᛒᛅᚢᚴᛁ ​(b-au-k-i); the ​k-rune writes ​the g, ‌and the medial ​fricative [ɣ] ​that the ​spelling g ‌represents has ​no rune ​of its ​own.

  • The ‌Unicode form ​Baugi is ‌therefore identical ​to the ‌ASCII input ​— the ‌restoration is ‌a matter of ​canonical reference, ​not rescued ​letters, which ​is exactly ‌what the ‌Tier 2 ​classification records.[3]

  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Hall (D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Baugi; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. baugr.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Baugi ​is catalogued ‌under the ‌domain Giant, ​Brother of ​Suttungr ​— a ​relational domain, ​because the ‌tradition ​gives him ​no independent ‌portfolio: he ​exists exactly ‌where ​his brother's ‌hoard needs ​a door ​and a ​doorkeeper.[1]

​### The ​Giant

He ‌is of ​the ‌jǫtnar, listed ​among them ‌in the ​þulur, and ‌his ​household — ‌land, thralls, ​harvest — ​is ‌the working ‌farm on ‌which Óðinn's ​deception is ​staged.[2]

‌### The ​Brother of ​Suttungr

His ‌one genealogical ​fact ​is his ‌defining one: ​Suttungr's brother, ‌and ​therefore the ​one man ​with a ​legitimate path ‌toward ‌the mead ​that Suttungr ‌hoards in ​Hnitbjǫrg.

​The ‌Labour-Broker

His ‌nine thralls ‌die by ‌the ​whetstone trick, ‌and his ​need for ​nine men's ​harvest-work ‌becomes the ​contract that ​buys Bǫlverkr ​a ‌summer's access.

​### The ​Doorkeeper of ​Hnitbjǫrg

With ‌the ​auger Rati ‌in his ​hands he ‌bores the ​only ‌entrance the ‌mountain ever ​has — ​and ​then strikes ​at what ‌crawls through ‌it, and ​misses.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Baugi's ​iconography is ‌narrative, not ‌cultic — ​the ​corpus gives ​him no ​regalia, but ​the ‌heist fixed ​a small ​set of ‌objects ​around his ‌one episode:[1]

​- Hnitbjǫrg ‌— ​The mountain ​of the ​mead, hoard ​and ​fortress in ‌one; Baugi's ​auger makes ‌the ​only door ‌it ever ​had

  • ‌Rati ​— The ‌auger, its ​name connected ​with ‌the verb ‌rata ‘to ‌travel, find ​one's ​way’; tool ‌of the ​breach and, ​for ‌one stroke, ​almost the ​weapon that ‌ends ​the story

‌- The ​whetstone — ​Óðinn's ​thrown bait: ​the thralls ‌scramble for ‌it ​and cut ‌each other's ​throats, emptying ​Baugi's ‌fields of ‌nine workers ‌at a ‌throw
​- The ‌nine scythes ​— Harvest-tools ​honed ​too well; ‌honest labour's ​edge turned ​to ​mutual death ‌by a ​single act ​of ​sharpening

  • ‌Óðrœrir, Boðn, ​and Són ‌— ​The three ‌vessels of ​the mead ‌— ‌‘heart-stirrer’, ‘vessel’, ​and ‘reconciliation’ ​— that ​wait ​on the ‌far side ‌of Baugi's ​tunnel[2]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Baugi's mythology is ​a single episode of the mead-of-poetry ‌myth — but that episode is ‌the hinge on which the whole ​theft turns.[1]

The Mead Comes ​to Hnitbjǫrg (Skáldskaparmál)

Before Baugi enters, ​the mead has its own violent ​history: Kvasir, made from the peace ​of Æsir and Vanir, is killed ‌by the dwarfs Fjalar and Galar, ​who brew his blood with honey ​into the mead that makes poets; ‌the dwarfs drown the giant Gilling ​and kill his wife; Gilling's son ‌Suttungr takes the mead from them ​as blood-price, shuts it inside the ‌mountain Hnitbjǫrg, and sets his daughter ​Gunnlǫð to guard it. Only then ​does Snorri write the genealogy ​the story needs: Suttungr had a ​brother named Baugi.[1]

The Whetstone ​and the Nine Thralls (Skáldskaparmál)

Óðinn ‌comes to Baugi's land calling himself ​Bǫlverkr, ‘Evil-doer’ — a name that ‌announces rather than conceals. Nine of ​Baugi's thralls are mowing hay; Bǫlverkr ‌sharpens their scythes with a whetstone ​so good that all nine want ‌it, throws it up among them, ​and in the scramble the thralls ‌cut each other's throats. That evening ​the stranger lodges with Baugi and ​hears him lament: nine thralls dead, ‌the hay uncut. The vacancy the ‌god is about to fill is ‌one he made himself.[1]

​Nine Men's Work for One Draught ​(Skáldskaparmál)

Bǫlverkr offers the work of ‌nine men for the summer, his ​wage one drink of Suttungr's mead. ​Baugi gives the story's one honest ‌answer — the mead is not ​his to give, Suttungr alone controls ​it — and then commits himself ‌anyway: he will go with Bǫlverkr ​and they will try together. The ‌work is done; the pair go ​to Suttungr; the refusal is absolute. ​Baugi's brokerage has failed on its ​stated terms, and what remains is ​the half-promise of stratagems.[1]

Rati ‌and the Two Blowings (Skáldskaparmál)

Bǫlverkr ‌produces the auger Rati, and Baugi ​bores into the mountain. He ‌declares the hole through; Bǫlverkr blows ​into it and the chips fly ​back into his face — the ‌bore is blind, and Baugi meant ‌to betray him. Baugi bores again; ‌the chips fly inward. Bǫlverkr becomes ‌a snake and crawls through, and ​Baugi strikes after him with the ‌auger and misses. Inside, Óðinn lies ​three nights with Gunnlǫð, wins three ​draughts, empties all three vessels, and ​rises as an eagle with the ‌mead in him, Suttungr hard behind ​in eagle-shape; the Æsir set out ​their vats, and what Óðinn cannot ​hold falls backward — the poetasters' ‌share.[1]

Óðinn's Own Version ​(Hávamál 104–110)

Hávamál lets the god ​boast of the same theft in ​his own voice: Rati's mouth made ‌room for him and gnawed the ​rock; Gunnlǫð gave him the drink ‌from the golden seat; Óðrœrir came ​up among the Æsir; Suttungr was ‌left defrauded and Gunnlǫð weeping, the ​ring-oath broken.[2] But the poem never ‌names Baugi. In the god's telling ‌the doorkeeper has already vanished — ​contract, auger, and missed stroke absorbed ​into solitary cunning. Snorri's prose preserves ​what the boasting omits: the door ​had a keeper, the keeper had ‌a price, and at the last ‌moment he tried to close it ​on the snake.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987), pp. 61–64.
  2. Hávamál 104–110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Baugi himself ​has no ‌cross-border reflex ‌— no Old ​English or ​continental cognate ​figure, no cult, ​no charm; ​he is ‌a person the ​Icelandic tradition ​made for ‌one narrative. The ​myth he ‌serves, however, ​sits in one ‌of the ​oldest comparative ​dossiers in Indo-European ​study: the ​theft of ​the divine intoxicant. ‌The standard ​parallel is Vedic ‌— the ​śyena, the ‌eagle or falcon ​that snatches ‌the soma ​for the gods ‌(Rigveda 4.26–27) ​— set ​beside Óðinn in ‌eagle-shape carrying ‌off the ‌mead in his ​own body. ​The comparison ‌was drawn in ​the nineteenth ​century and ‌is still registered, ​with due ​caution, in ‌the modern handbooks: ​Lindow treats ‌the mead ​myth as the ​northern member ​of a ​genuine inheritance while ‌warning against ‌flattening its ​specifically Norse machinery ‌— the ​labour contract, ​the auger, the ‌broken oath.[1]

‌Baugi's role ‌belongs to that ‌northern machinery: ​the intermediary ‌who can be ​bought, the ​door that ​opens from the ‌inside. Within ​the corpus his ​closest structural ​kin are ‌the giants whose ​households Óðinn ​penetrates by ​guile — above ‌all his ​brother Suttungr ‌and the deep ​giant-lore descending ‌from Ymir ​— and the ‌wisdom-figures the ‌god elsewhere ​out-talls, Mimir among ​them. The ​mead he ​fails to keep ‌becomes the ‌drink of ​Bragi's art.[2]

  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Baugi, mead.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Baugi's afterlife ​is mostly ‌the afterlife ‌of the ​drink he ​failed to ​keep. Skaldic ​poetry — ​the whole ‌court-poetry tradition ​of the ​Viking and ‌medieval North ​— named ‌itself out ​of this ‌myth: Kvasir's ​blood, the ​dwarfs' drink, ​Suttungr's mead, ​Óðinn's theft, ​Óðrœrir's wave. ‌Every character ​of the ‌heist entered ​the poetic ‌vocabulary except, ​so far ‌as the ​corpus shows, ‌its doorkeeper: ​no surviving ​kenning mints ‌a ‘Baugi's ‌mead’.[1]

He ‌survives instead ​as the ​specialists' hinge-figure. ‌Every scholarly ​treatment of ​the mead ‌myth, from ​Finnur Jónsson ​onward, must ‌reckon with his ​two functions ‌— the ​labour-broker who ​makes the ​theft logistically ​possible, and ‌the wavering ‌accomplice whose ​blind bore-hole ‌and missed ​auger-stroke are ​the story's ‌closest brush ‌with failure. ‌In the ‌Norse-revival retellings ​and the ‌modern handbooks ​he appears ​under his ​own name, ‌always in ​the same ​posture: halfway ​through the ‌mountain, one ​hand on ​the auger. ​The myth ‌itself has ​become the ‌North's great ​parable of ‌inspiration as ​something hoarded ‌and stolen ‌rather than ​given — ​and Bragi, ​god of ​poetry, pours ‌out what ‌Baugi could ​not guard.[2]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Baugi ​is an ‌entirely textual ‌figure: no ​runestone invokes ​him, ​no picture ​stone has ​been convincingly ‌read as ​the drilling ​of ‌Hnitbjǫrg, and ​no place-name ‌or votive ​deposit carries ‌his ​name.[1] His ​attestation is ​exactly as ​wide as ​his story ‌— ​Snorri's Skáldskaparmál, ‌preserved principally ​in the ‌Codex Regius ​of ‌the Prose ​Edda (GKS ‌2367 4to, ​early fourteenth ​century), with ‌the ‌Codex Wormianus ‌and Codex ​Upsaliensis as ​the other ‌main ​witnesses, plus ​his entry ‌in the ​þulur of ​giant-names.

The ‌mead ​myth he ‌serves is ​materially older ​than its ​manuscripts. ​Kennings presupposing ‌it — ‌Kvasir's blood, ​Suttungr's mead, ‌Óðinn's booty ​— ​are already ‌at work ‌in ninth- ‌and tenth-century ‌skaldic ​verse, proving ‌the story's ​currency centuries ​before Snorri ​wrote it ‌down.[2] ​Hávamál's telling ​survives in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the ​Poetic Edda ​(c. 1270), ‌the same ​small codex ‌that carries ​nearly ‌all the ​mythological poems. ‌The runic ‌record is ​silent ​on the ​name, as ​it is ‌on nearly ‌all the ​jǫtnar.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never names ‌Baugi — ​and that ​silence is ​itself evidence. ​Hávamál 104–110 ​gives Óðinn's ‌own boasting ​account of ​the mead ‌theft, and ​it ‌names the ​doorkeeper's instruments ‌but not ​the doorkeeper: ​Rati, whose ​mouth ‘made ​room’ and ​gnawed the ‌rock (st. ​106); Gunnlǫð ‌on her ​golden ‌seat (st. ​105); Óðrœrir ‌come up ​among the ‌Æsir (st. ​107); and ​the famous ‌self-indictment of ‌st. 110 ‌— Óðinn ​swore a ​ring-oath, a ‌baugeiðr, ​and who ​can trust ‌him? Suttungr ​left defrauded ​of his ‌mead, Gunnlǫð ​left weeping.[1]

‌Two things ​follow. First, ​the poem ​attests the ​story's ‌core machinery ‌— the ​auger, the ‌mountain, the ​daughter, the ​broken oath ‌— independent ‌of Snorri's ‌prose, which ‌confirms that ​the Skáldskaparmál ‌narrative rests ​on ​older verse ​tradition rather ‌than antiquarian ​invention. Second, ​the omission ​of Baugi ‌is characteristically ​Óðinnic: in ​the god's ​own telling ‌there is ​no ‌broker, no ​contract, no ‌near-fatal auger-stroke ​— only ‌solitary cunning. ‌The doorkeeper ​survives only ​because the ​prose tradition ​refused to ‌let him ‌be edited ​out.[2]

Sources

  1. Hávamál 104–110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. mead of poetry.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Baugi's only ‌narrative home, ​and it ​is a ​remarkably complete ​one. Skáldskaparmál's ​mead-of-poetry section ‌gives him ​a full ​dramatic arc ‌in a ​few pages: ‌the genealogy ​(Suttungr's brother), ‌the household ​(nine thralls ​mowing hay), the ​disaster (the ​whetstone and ​the mutual ‌slaughter), the ​bargain (nine ‌men's work ​for one ‌draught), the ​honest disclosure ‌(the mead ​is Suttungr's, ‌not mine), ​the failed ​petition to ‌Suttungr, the ‌two borings ‌with Rati — ​the first ​deliberately blind, ‌the second ​true — ​and the ‌missed auger-stroke ​as the ​snake crawls ‌through.[1]

Snorri's ​craft deserves ‌note in ​the small ​details: Baugi's ​first bore ​is exposed ‌not by ‌inspection but ​by physics — ‌the chips ​fly back ​into the ‌blower's face ‌— and ‌the second ‌is verified ​the same ‌way. The ​auger Rati ​becomes, in ​Baugi's hands, ‌both the ​instrument of ​his complicity ​and the ‌instrument of his ​betrayal; the ​same tool ​opens the ‌door and ​nearly closes ‌it. Beyond ​the narrative, ‌the Skáldskaparmál ​þulur list ‌Baugi among ‌the jǫtnar, ​preserving the ​name in ​the catalogue ​with bureaucratic ‌fidelity beside ‌its single ​shining use.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987), pp. 61–64.
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur of giant-names, ed. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of ​Baugi. ​No inscription ​names ​the giant, ‌no ​amulet bears ​him, ‌and the ​name ‌does not ​appear ‌among ​the ordinary ​anthroponyms ​of the ​Scandinavian ​runic record ‌— ​a silence ‌that ​marks him ‌as ​a ‌purely literary-mythological ​figure, ‌attached to ​one ​story and ‌one ‌catalogue.[1]

What ‌the ​runic material ​does ‌illuminate ​is the ​name's ‌raw substance. ​In ​the Younger ‌Futhark ​the name ‌would ​have been ​carved ​ᛒᛅᚢᚴᛁ ​— b-au-k-i: ‌the ‌k-rune ᚴ ​writes ‌both k ​and ​g, and ‌no ‌rune distinguishes ‌the ‌voiced ​fricative that ‌the ​manuscript spelling ​g ​preserves between ‌vowels. ​The runic ​row ​underspecifies what ‌the ​Latin ​bookhand fixes; ​here ‌as everywhere, ​the ‌primary witness ​for ‌the name's ​form ‌is the ‌manuscript ​tradition ​of the ​Eddas, ​which writes ‌five ‌plain ASCII ​letters.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Baugi.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Baugi has ‌no saga ​career: the ​Icelandic family ​sagas and ​the ​kings' sagas ‌do not ​know him, ​and Saxo ‌Grammaticus, whose ​Gesta Danorum ‌euhemerizes ​so much ‌of the ​northern pantheon, ​tells no ​version of ​the ​mead theft ‌that includes ​him.[1] His ‌medieval footprint ​is the ‌Eddic one ​— ‌Snorri's narrative ​and the ‌þulur — ​and nothing ​more.

The ‌indirect afterlife ‌runs ‌through the ​skalds. Because ​the mead ‌myth was ​the foundation-myth ​of poetry ‌itself, ​its vocabulary ​saturated skaldic ‌diction from ​the ninth ‌century onward: ​poetry ​is Kvasir's ​blood, the ​dwarfs' sea, ‌Suttungr's mead, ‌Óðinn's booty, ​Óðrœrir's wave. ‌The ​skalds built ​a whole ‌technical lexicon ‌out of ‌the heist ‌Baugi enabled ​— ‌and built ​it, so ​far as ​the surviving ‌corpus shows, ​without once ​naming ​him. The ‌doorkeeper of ​Hnitbjǫrg is ​present in ​every stanza ‌that ​calls a ‌poem the ​giants' stolen ‌drink, and ​named in ‌none: his ‌legacy ​is structural, ​the condition ​of the ​tradition rather ‌than a ‌word in ​it.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Baugi, mead of poetry.
  2. Faulkes, Anthony, introduction to Snorri Sturluson, Edda (Everyman, 1987), on the skaldic kennings for poetry.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Baugi ​is the ‌indispensable man ‌nobody remembers. ​Strip him ​out and ​the myth ​stops cold: ​without the ‌thralls' deaths ​there is ​no vacancy, ‌without the ​contract there ‌is no ​access, without ‌the auger ​in his ​hand there ​is no ​door — ​and without ‌the ​door there ‌is no ​mead, and ‌by the ​tradition's own ‌accounting, no ​poetry at ‌all.[1]

Yet ​he is ​not the ‌hero of ‌his one ‌story, nor ​even its ​villain. He ‌is something ​rarer in ​the giant-world ‌of the ​Eddas: a ​middleman. He ‌names ​the limits ‌of his ​power honestly ​— the ​mead is ​Suttungr's, not ‌mine — ‌and then ​sells access ‌to what ​is not ​his anyway. ‌He helps ‌bore the ‌hole and ‌then tries ​to kill ‌what crawls ​through it. ​The tradition ​leaves his ‌motives ​exactly that ​tangled, and ​the tangle ‌is the ​point: every ​hoard is ​opened from ‌the inside, ​by someone ‌whose loyalty ​is a ‌price rather ​than a ‌fact. The ‌auger-stroke misses ​by the ​width of ​a snake ​— and ‌because it ‌misses, poems ​exist.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987), pp. 61–64.
