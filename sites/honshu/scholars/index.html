Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Honshū ​(honshu) — ‌The ‌Heart of ​the Islands ​— ​belongs to ​the ​Japanese tradition, ‌where it ​is ​catalogued under ‌the ​domain "Center, ‌Mainland, ​Heart". The ‌name means ​"'Main ​island' (本州)"[1].

​Honshū ​is the ​island ‌that became ​a civilization: ‌largest ​of the ‌Japanese ​archipelago, home ‌of every ​historic ‌capital from ​Nara ​to Kyōto ‌to ‌Edo-Tōkyō, and ‌the ground ​on ​which the ‌Jōmon, ​the Yayoi, ​and ‌the Yamato ​state built ​the ‌longest continuous ​imperial ‌tradition on ​earth.[2]

PuniCodex ​restores ​the name ​as ‌Honshū and ‌serves ​its temple ‌at honshū.com. ​The ​restoration preserves ‌the ‌long vowel ‌ū ‌— a ​distinctive feature ‌the ​ASCII form ​loses ​— which ‌places the ​name ​in Tier ​1. ‌The plain ​ASCII ​form honshu ​survives as ‌a ​modern convenience ‌imposed ​by the ‌early ​domain-name system; ‌the restoration, ‌not ​the fallback, ​is ​the form ​the project ‌defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗
  2. Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), Translated by W. G. Aston (excerpts), 720. ↗
  3. Shinto, Kojiki, Nihon Shoki, shrine rituals, and folk practice, various.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

No ​indigenous written ‌attestation survives ‌for ​this name; ​Honshū is ​a scholarly ​transliteration ​of the ‌reconstructed spoken ​form. ​Etymologically the ‌name means ​"'Main island' ‌(本州)"[1].

​The ASCII ‌form honshu ​survives ​only because ​the early ​domain-name ​system could ‌not carry ​diacritics; it ‌is ​a technological ‌compromise, not ​an ‌ancient spelling. ​The Unicode ‌restoration ​Honshū recovers ​the vowel ‌length of ‌the ‌original directly ​in the ​address ‌bar. The ​restoration preserves ​the long ‌vowel ​ū — ​a distinctive ‌feature ​the ASCII ‌form loses ​— ​which places ​the name ​in Tier ‌1.

‌The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

‌- ​h → ​H — ‌Same letter
‌- ‌o → ‌o — ​Same ‌letter

  • ​n → ​n ​— Same ‌letter
  • ​s → ​s ​— Same ‌letter
  • ​h ​→ h ​— Same ‌letter

​- u ‌→ ū ​— Macron ‌marks ​the long ‌vowel

The ‌project ​holds the ​domain honshū.com ​(xn--honsh-pfb.com) as ​the ‌canonical home ‌of this ​name[2].

Sources

  1. Nihon Shoki (Chronicles of Japan), Translated by W. G. Aston (excerpts), 720. ↗
  2. Kokugo dictionaries (Nihon Kokugo Daijiten / Kōjien / Daijisen), Shōgakukan / Sanseidō, various.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed ‌pronunciation ‌of the ​name ​is ​/hon.ɕuː/ ​— ​Japanese (Hepburn).[1]

‌For ​the ​modern ‌speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation ​is: ‌HON-shoo, ​with ​a long ​final ​ū.

​Kindred ‌and ​historical forms ‌of ​the ‌name:

​- ‌Japanese — ​本州
‌- ​Mythic ​name ‌— ‌Ōyashima

  • ‌Owned ​form ​— ‌Honshū

​Honshū 本州 ​— ‌"Main ​Province" ​— ‌closes with ​the ‌long ​ū ​of ​shū, preserved ​in ‌the ‌macron. ​The ‌name is ​descriptive ​geography ‌elevated ‌to ‌a ‌proper name: ​the ‌largest ​of ​Japan's ​islands, holding ‌Tokyo, ​Kyōto, ​Ōsaka, ​and ‌some three-quarters ​of ​the ​nation. ‌Both ​syllables carry ‌long ​vowels ‌in ​kana ‌(ほんしゅう); the ‌macron ​on ​the ​final ​ū marks ‌the ‌doubled ​u.

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​original ‌script ‌for ​this ​japanese ​name ​has ​not ‌yet ​been ​added ‌to ​PUNICODEX. ‌The ​‌form ​shown ​is ​a ​scholarly ​transliteration.[1]

‌The ​form ‌Honshū ​is ‌therefore ​a ‌scholarly ​transliteration ‌rather ​than ​an ‌attested ‌ancient ‌spelling; ​it ​encodes ‌the ​reconstructed ​sound ‌of ​the ​name ‌​for ‌modern ​use, ​and ​no ​mark ‌in ‌it ​is ‌decorative.

​Written ​本州: ‌hon ‌(root, ‌main, ‌origin) ​plus ‌shū ​(province). ​The ​Kojiki's ‌mythic ​name ​for ​the ‌island ​is ​Ōyashima, ​‌"the ​great ‌eight ​islands" ‌— ​the ‌archipelago's ‌whole ​body, ​of ​which ​Honshū ‌is ‌the ​spine.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Honshū's mythology ​is the national ‌charter itself: ‌the Kojiki's island-birth, ​the sun goddess's ​gift of the ​land, and the ​descent of ​her grandson to ‌rule it.[1]

​The Islands from ​the Spear (The ‌Birth)

The ​Kojiki opens on ‌the heavenly bridge: ​Izanagi and Izanami ‌stir the ​ocean with the ​jewelled spear, and ​from the brine ​dripping from its ​point the ‌first island coagulates. ​Descending, they wed ‌and give birth ​to the ‌islands of Japan ​— the "great ‌eight islands" culminating ​in Ōyashima — ‌and then ​to the kami ​of wind, sea, ‌mountain, and tree. ‌The archipelago is ‌not created ​but born: the ​land is kin.[2]

‌### Amaterasu's Realm ​(The Sun)

​When the sun ‌goddess Amaterasu withdrew ​into the Heavenly ​Rock-Cave — outraged ‌by her ​brother Susanoo's rampage ‌— the world ​went dark until ​the gods' comic ​dance lured ​her out. Her ‌mirror, hung outside ‌the cave, caught ​her own ‌light back to ​her; that mirror ​rests at Ise ‌on Honshū's Kii ‌coast, and ‌her shrine there ‌has been ritually ​rebuilt every twenty ‌years for thirteen ​centuries: the ​island's heartwood, renewed.

​### Ninigi and ‌the Rice Land ​(The Descent)

​Amaterasu sent her ​grandson Ninigi down ‌from heaven to ​rule the "Land ​of Fair ​Rice-ears of the ‌Fertile Reed-plain" — ​the Kojiki's name ‌for Honshū's ​heartland — bearing ‌the three regalia. ​His great-grandson Jimmu ‌conquered eastward to ‌Yamato and ​became the first ​emperor: the mythic ​bridge from island-birth ​to imperial genealogy, ‌anchored to ‌real ground in ​Nara's basin.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The ​island ‌is ‌the ​great ​syncretist ​of Asia: ​Buddhism ​arriving ‌from ​Korea ​in ‌the ​6th ‌century ​was ‌fused ​with ​kami-worship ​into shinbutsu-shūgō ​— ​the ‌dual-identity ​system ‌that ​ran ‌Japanese ​religion ‌for ​a ‌millennium; ​Zen and ​the ‌tea ‌culture ‌of ​Kyōto ​fused ‌with ​warrior ​aesthetics; ‌and ​the ​Meiji ‌restoration engineered ​State ‌Shinto ​on ​top ​of ​it ‌all. ‌Honshū's ​mountains ‌hold ​the ​shugendō ‌tradition — ‌Yamabushi ‌ascetics ‌practicing ​the ‌oldest ​fusion ​of ​all: ‌kami, ​buddha, ​and ​mountain as ‌one ​path.[1]

​Within ​the ‌Japanese ​tradition, ‌closely ​related ‌names ​in ‌the ‌corpus ​include Fūjin, ​Hokkaidō, ​Jizō, ​Kōbe, ‌Kyōto, ‌and ​Kyūshū.

Sources

  1. Kojiki (Records of Ancient Matters), Translated by Basil Hall Chamberlain, 712. ↗

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Honshū ​carries ‌‌roughly ​104 ​million ​people ​​— ‌the ​largest ​‌island ​population ‌on ​‌earth ​— ​and ​its ​​corridor ‌from ​Tōkyō ‌​to ‌Ōsaka ​is ‌the ​‌most ​studied ​urban ‌‌megalopolis ‌in ​geography. ​‌Its ​cultural ​exports ‌need ​​no ‌list: ​the ‌​world's ​first ​novel ​(The ‌‌Tale ​of ‌Genji) ​​was ‌written ‌here, ‌‌Zen ​gardens ‌and ​ukiyo-e ​​and ‌kabuki ​were ​​born ‌here, ​and ​the ​‌shinkansen ​that ‌shrank ​‌the ​island ‌in ‌​1964 ​taught ​the ​world ‌‌high-speed ​rail.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Sannai-Maruyama ​near ‌Aomori ‌preserves ​a ​Jōmon ​city ​of ​5,500 ‌years ​ago ​— ‌great ​cedar ‌pillars, ​longhouses, ‌and ​chestnut ​stores ​that ​rewrote ​the ‌era's ​reputation ‌from ​"primitive" ‌to ​prosperous. ‌The ​Yayoi's ‌moated ​villages ​and ‌the ‌keyhole-shaped ‌kofun ​tombs ​(Daisenryō ‌in ​Ōsaka ​is ‌among ​the ​largest ‌​tombs ‌on ​earth ​by ​area) ​trace ‌the ‌state's ​rise. ‌Nara's ​Tōdai-ji ​holds ‌the ‌world's ‌largest ‌bronze ​Buddha ‌in ​the ​world's ​largest ‌wooden ​building; ​Ise's ​shikinen ‌sengū ​— ​the ​twenty-year ‌rebuilding ​— ‌is ​living ‌archaeology, ​a ‌Bronze ‌Age ​rite ​performed ​on ​schedule ‌into ‌the ​present.[1]

Kojiki & Nihon Shoki

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​main ‌island's ‌names ​are ​the ​nation's ​names.

​- ‌Ōyashima ​(大八洲) ​— ‌'the ​Great ‌Eight ​Islands'; ‌the ​Kojiki's ​myth-name ​for ​the ​born ‌​archipelago.
‌- ​Akitsushima ‌— ​'the ‌Dragonfly ​Island'; ‌the ​classical ​poetry-name ‌for ‌Japan, ‌attributed ​to ​the ‌first ​emperor's ​image ‌of ​the ​land.
‌- ​Yamato ‌no ​moto ​— ​'the ​root ‌of ‌Yamato'; ​the ‌heartland-title ​of ​the ‌Nara ‌basin.
‌- ‌Honshū ​(本州) ‌— ​'the ​Main ​Province'; ‌​the ​descriptive ​name ‌that ​became ​the ​proper ‌one.
​- ‌Nakatsukuni ​— ‌'the ​Middle ‌Land'; ‌the ​cosmological ​title ​between ​heaven ‌and ‌the ​underworld.

Shinto Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​island ‌holds ‌Japan's ​holiest ​ground: ​Ise Jingū, ​the ​Grand ‌Shrines ​of ​Amaterasu ‌on ​the ‌Kii ​coast, ‌rebuilt ​on ​adjacent ​sites every ​twenty ​years ‌since ​the ‌7th ​century ‌— ​the ‌shikinen ​sengū, ‌a ​Bronze Age ​renewal ‌rite ‌performed ‌on ​schedule ​into ‌the ​present. ​The ‌Atsuta ​shrine ​of ‌the sacred ​sword, ‌Izumo ​Taisha ​(claimed ​by ​the ‌myth ‌as ​the ‌land-ceding ​god's ​seat, ‌one of ‌the ‌tallest ‌ancient ​halls), ‌Tōdai-ji ​with ​the ​world's ‌largest ​bronze ​Buddha, ​and the ‌ten ​thousand ​Inari ​shrines ‌of ​the ‌rice-fox ​god: ‌Honshū's ​sacred ‌map ‌is ​the archipelago's ​whole ​religion ​in ‌one ‌island's ​geography.

Sources

  1. The Ise Jingu records (shikinen sengu); Kojiki; the shrine gazetteers.

Japanese Buddhist Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Honshū is ​the island that ‌kept everything: the oldest ‌pottery beside the ​fastest trains, the ​goddess's mirror beside the ​parliament. Its lesson ​is continuity as ​craft — the shrine ‌rebuilt every twenty ​years so that ​it never ages and ‌never changes. It ​asks the heartland's ‌question: what in your ​tradition is worth ‌rebuilding, exactly as ​it was, forever?

The ​twenty-year rebuilding of ​Ise is the ​island's deepest answer to ​modernity: continuity is ‌not preservation but ​repetition — the ‌shrine is never old ​and never new, ‌the cypress always ​fresh, the craft always ‌taught. Honshū holds ​its megacities and ‌its mirror with the ​same hands, and ​its lesson to ‌every anxious age is ‌the carpenter's: what ‌you rebuild on ​schedule, you never have ​to save.

And ‌the island keeps ​answering in both registers ​at once: the ‌shinkansen bows to ​the mountain as it ​passes; the morning ‌news follows the ​shrine calendar; the ‌world's largest city ends, ​an hour out, ​in cedar valleys ​where the kami-names predate ​the state. Honshū's ‌genius was never ‌to choose between the ​shrine and the ‌station — it ​scheduled them. The heartland's ​final lesson is ‌that depth and ‌scale are not enemies: ‌an island can ‌hold a hundred ​million lives and still ‌keep, at Ise, ​an empty plot ​waiting for the next ​rebuilding, exactly as ‌it was.

Stand ​at Ise on ​a rebuilding year: two ​shrines, one old, ‌one new, standing ​side by side for ​a season — ​the island's whole ‌philosophy visible at a ​glance. The past ‌is not kept ​by freezing it but ‌by making it ​again, carefully, on ‌schedule, forever.

The main ‌island does not ​boast; it simply ​holds — the capitals, ​the shrines, the ​mountains, and the ‌memory — the spine ‌on which everything ​else hangs.[1]
