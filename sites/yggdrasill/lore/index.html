The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᚢᚴᚴᛏᚱᛅᛋᛁᛚᛚ

The name in its original Norse form. Yggdrasill (ᚢᚴᚴᛏᚱᛅᛋᛁᛚᛚ) is attested in the source tradition — “Ygg's horse (Odin's gallows)”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

yggdrasill

The plain yggdrasill form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Yggdrasill

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Yggdrasill. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Yggdrasill.com → yggdrasill.com

The non-ASCII characters in Yggdrasill are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Yggdrasill.

Pronunciation

How Yggdrasill was spoken

/ˈyɡː.drɑ.silː/ Old Norse Reconstruction
Y- Close front rounded [y], as in German ü — Old Norse y is always a vowel here, never the English consonant.
-gg- Geminate voiced velar stop [ɡː], held long across the syllable boundary, as in modern Icelandic and Italian.
-dr- The onset cluster [dr] that opens the second element — drasill, the poetic word for 'horse'.
-a- Short open back unrounded [ɑ] — Old Norse short a, not long á.
-sill Voiceless sibilant [s], short close front [i], and the geminate lateral [lː] closing the word, fully sounded.

Modern Approximation

'UEG-drah-sill' — the first vowel rounded like German ü, the double g and double l each held a fraction long, stress on the first syllable as in every Old Norse word.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Yggdrasill, and the fuller phrase askr Yggdrasills, 'the ash of Yggdrasill'; the tree is also called mjötviðr, 'measuring tree', in Völuspá 2.
  • Younger Futhark ᚢᚴᚴᛏᚱᛅᛋᛁᛚᛚ (ukktrasill), the normalized Viking-Age runic rendering.
  • Learned Latin-script Yggdrasil, with a single l — the standard modern scholarly simplification in English and German handbooks.
  • Modern Icelandic Yggdrasill, the unbroken living form of the name.

The Accent / Script Rule

Yggdrasill is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter for the ASCII alphabet to lose. A concept of this scale needs no rescued orthography; only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input.

05

World Tree

Yggr's Horse — the Gallows That Became a Cosmos

Yggdrasill is the great ash at the center of the Norse cosmos — the tree whose name means 'Yggr's horse', the steed of the Terrible One, which is to say the gallows on which [[odinn|Óðinn]] hung himself for nine nights to win the runes. Völuspá's seeress sees it first of all things: the towering ash, sprinkled with white clay, dews falling from its branches into the dales. Three roots run from it to three wells — to Urðr's well in heaven where the Æsir hold court, to [[mimir|Mímir]]'s well among the frost-giants where wisdom is hidden, to Hvergelmir in [[niflheimr|Niflheimr]] where the serpent gnaws. It is not a god and not a place but the frame that holds every god and every place: the axis the nine worlds hang on.

The Three Roots

One root among the Æsir at Urðr's well, one among the frost-giants at Mímir's well, one in Niflheimr over Hvergelmir — Snorri's map of a cosmos plumbed by a single tree.

The Hanged God

Hávamál 138: Óðinn hung nine nights on the windy tree, spear-wounded, given to himself, and took up the runes. The tree's name remembers the gallows — Yggr's horse is the steed a hanged man rides.

Ratatoskr the Messenger

The squirrel who runs up and down the ash carrying words of spite between the eagle at the crown and Níðhöggr at the root — malice as the cosmos's postal service.

The Wells of Fate and Wisdom

Urðarbrunnr, where the norns water the tree and the gods hold their þing; Mímisbrunnr, where [[mimir|Mímir]] keeps wisdom and Óðinn pledged an eye — the tree drinks from both.

The Ash at Ragnarök

Völuspá 47: when the end comes, 'the ash of Yggdrasill shudders as it stands, and the old tree groans' — shaken, not felled; the cosmos trembles on its axis before the renewal.

06

Mythology

Stories of Yggdrasill

Yggdrasill has no adventures — it is the stage on which all adventures happen. The sources do not tell stories about the tree so much as they take attendance at it: the seeress sees it first, Óðinn hangs on it, the gods ride to it daily, the creatures live in it, and at the end of things it shudders and stands. The mythology of Yggdrasill is a set of sightings, each one from a different height of the cosmos.

Völuspá 19–20

The Seeress's First Vision

The seeress of Völuspá, reciting the world's history to [[odinn|Óðinn]], comes to the tree before almost anything else: 'An ash I know standing, called Yggdrasill, a high tree, sprinkled with white clay; from it come the dews that fall in the dales; it stands ever green over Urðr's well.' Earlier still she remembered nine worlds and the mjötviðr, the measuring tree, beneath the earth — the frame was there before the filling.

Beneath the tree she sees the three maidens who come from the lake under it — Urðr, Verðandi, and Skuld, the norns who carve on slips of wood and lay down laws, allotting life to the children of men. The poem's first cosmology is not a geography but a picture: one tree, one well, three women, and everything else arranged around them.

Hávamál 138–141

Nine Nights on the Windy Tree

Hávamál never names Yggdrasill, but the tradition has always read its most terrible stanzas onto it: 'I know that I hung on the windy tree nine whole nights, wounded by the spear, given to Óðinn, myself to myself, on that tree of which no man knows from what roots it rises.' No bread fed him, no horn refreshed him; he peered downward, took up the runes screaming, and fell back.

The name itself is the commentary. Yggr, 'the Terrible', is an attested heiti of Óðinn, and drasill is the poets' word for a horse: the tree is 'Yggr's steed' — the gallows the hanged god rides, in the old kenning-logic by which the gallows is the hanged man's horse. The axis of the universe is named for a death, and specifically for the death a god inflicted on himself to buy knowledge.

Grímnismál 29–35

The Burden of the Ash

Grímnismál's [[odinn|Óðinn]], bound between the fires, recites the tree's census. Three roots run in three directions: one among the Æsir, one among the frost-giants where Ginnungagap once yawned, the third over Niflheimr, where Hvergelmir boils and Níðhöggr gnaws the root from below. In the branches sits an eagle, wise in much, with the hawk Veðrfölnir between its eyes; Ratatoskr the squirrel runs up and down bearing the eagle's words of spite to the serpent; four stags — Dáinn, Dvalinn, Duneyrr, Duraþrór — bite the highest shoots.

Then the poem delivers its verdict, the darkest thing said about any tree: 'The ash of Yggdrasill suffers more agony than men know — a stag bites it from above, its sides are rotting, Níðhöggr gnaws it from below.' The world-order is not a monument but a wound that keeps standing. Snorri, who knows these stanzas well, builds his own account on them.

Gylfaginning 15–16

Snorri's Map of the Worlds

Snorri systematizes what the poems scatter. The chief sanctuary of the gods is the ash Yggdrasill, greatest and best of all trees: its branches spread over the whole world and reach above the sky. Of its three roots, one is among the Æsir at Urðr's well, where the gods hold their court daily, riding over [[bifrost|Bifröst]]; the second is among the frost-giants at [[mimir|Mímir]]'s well, where wisdom and understanding are hidden and Óðinn pledged his eye for a drink; the third stands over Niflheimr and the spring Hvergelmir.

At Urðr's well stand the norns who shape men's fates; they take water and the clay that lies by the well and pour it over the tree so its limbs shall not wither, and the dew that falls from it men call honey-dew, the food of bees. Snorri keeps the eagle, the hawk, the squirrel, the serpents, and the stags from Grímnismál, and adds the detail that turns cosmology into hygiene: the tree must be watered, or the world dries out.

Völuspá 47; Vafþrúðnismál 44–45

The Tree at Ragnarök

When the end comes, the seeress gives the tree one line of pure dread: 'Yggdrasill's ash shudders as it stands, the old tree groans, and the giant slips free.' The cosmos announces its collapse through its axis — not by falling, but by trembling. Snorri's account of [[ragnarok|Ragnarök]] keeps the same note: heaven cloven, the sons of Muspell riding, and the ash shaking.

Vafþrúðnismál looks past the fire. Óðinn asks the giant what comes after, and learns that Líf and Lífþrasir will hide in Hodd-Mímir's wood through Fimbulvetr, living on the morning dews, and from them the new mankind will spring. The scholarly tradition has long connected that sheltering wood with the world tree itself — the tree that is named for a gallows ends as the refuge of the living.

Extended Lore

The Norse imagination did something no other mythology dared: it named the support of all existence after an instrument of execution. Yggdrasill is not 'the beautiful tree' or 'the tree of life' but 'Yggr's horse' — the gallows, remembered forever. The claim folded into the etymology is that the cosmos is held up by a price already paid: the god's nine nights, the pledged eye, the runes taken up screaming. And the tree answers the claim by suffering in its turn — bitten from above, rotting at the sides, gnawed from below, enduring more agony than men know, and still standing. Between the hanged god and the wounded tree runs the Edda's deepest intuition: that order is not the absence of damage but the refusal to fall. When Ragnarök comes, the ash shudders and groans — and the poems never say it breaks. Whatever burns, the frame holds long enough for Líf and Lífþrasir to wake in the morning dew.
