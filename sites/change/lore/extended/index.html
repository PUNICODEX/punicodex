Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script 嫦娥 Chángé — "The Moon goddess who drank the elixir of immortality"
Unicode Restoration Chángé Restored stress, length, and script
Modern ASCII change Plain-ASCII fallback

嫦娥 denotes the Chinese goddess of the moon. The Modern Standard Mandarin reading is Chángé — cháng (Tone 2) and é (Tone 2) — as recorded in the Unihan Database (Unicode Consortium, kMandarin) and standard Mandarin dictionaries. The goddess's earlier name was 姮娥 Héng'é; under the Han it was altered to Chángé to observe the naming taboo on the personal name of Emperor Wen (Liú Héng 劉恆), the graph 常 cháng ('constant') replacing the tabooed homophone 姮 héng. The tone-marked Pinyin restoration Chángé preserves the citation rising tone on both syllables; in connected speech the contour may be compressed, but the lexical tones remain Tone 2.

04

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Chángé is the woman who swallowed forever and paid for it with the earth. Wife of the archer Hòuyì, who shot nine suns out of the sky, she came into possession of the elixir of immortality — a gift of the Queen Mother of the West, Xīwángmǔ — and drank it. Whether she acted to keep the drug from a treacherous disciple or out of her own longing, the sources divide; what they agree on is the result: her body grew light, the ground let go of her, and she rose through the night until the moon received her. There she remains, the goddess of the cold luminary, in the Guǎnghán Palace — the Palace of Vast Cold.

She is not alone up there. The Jade Rabbit stands beside her, pounding the elixir with mortar and pestle, and the woodcutter Wú Gāng swings his axe at a cassia tree that heals each wound as it is struck. Once a year, at the full moon of the eighth month, all of China looks up at her: the Mid-Autumn Festival, when families reunite, mooncakes are cut, and the distance between heaven and earth is measured in shared light.

Chángé in Later Traditions

The rabbit in the moon is older than any single nation's telling of it. Buddhist tradition carried the śaśajātaka — the tale of the rabbit who offered itself as food and was set in the moon as a reward — from India into East Asia, and scholars commonly link the Chinese moon rabbit to this transmission, though the Jade Rabbit's elixir-pounding is China's own elaboration. Taoist religion absorbed Chángé into its celestial bureaucracy, where she is often identified with the sovereign of the yin luminary (太陰). Japan received the moon rabbit without the elixir: there he pounds mochi instead, and the autumn moon-viewing custom (tsukimi) echoes Mid-Autumn without keeping its goddess. Korea's harvest festival Chuseok and Vietnam's Tết Trung Thu share the same full-moon night of cakes, lanterns, and family gathering — the festival traveled further than the myth, and in each harbor took local form.

Modern Legacy

Chángé has been China's shorthand for beautiful distance since the poets claimed her. Sū Shì's Mid-Autumn lyric closes with the wish that, though a thousand miles apart, we may share chánjuān 嬋娟 — the lovely one, the moon, her. The Mid-Autumn Festival remains one of the great holidays of the Chinese world, and the mooncake its edible emblem. In 2020 the animated film Over the Moon carried her to global audiences as a pop-diva goddess still waiting for Hòuyì. Most remarkably, she has gone back: China's lunar exploration program is named for her, and the probes Chángé 1 through 6 (2007–2024) orbited the moon, landed on it, achieved the first landing on its far side (Chángé 4), and returned samples to earth (Chángé 5 and 6). No other deity in this lexicon has been so literally restored to her own heaven.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Chángé in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Chángé, Moon, Immortality, Mid-Autumn, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Chángé?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Chángé is /ʈʂʰaŋ˧˥ ɤ˧˥/ — approximately CHAHNG-UH — both syllables rise in tone, as in a questioning intonation; 'ch' is an aspirated retroflex with the tongue curled back, and 'é' is close to the vowel of 'her' with the r removed..

02What does Chángé mean?

Chángé means The Moon goddess who drank the elixir of immortality in the chinese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Chángé?

Chángé is associated with The full moon (Her dwelling and her face; completeness, distance, and the reunion it mirrors), The Jade Rabbit (Her companion (玉兔), who pounds the elixir of immortality with mortar and pestle — labor without end in her service), Mortar and pestle (The ceaseless preparation of the elixir she carried to heaven), The cassia tree (The self-healing tree Wú Gāng chops forever beside her palace — punishment and persistence in a single image), The mooncake (The round pastry of Mid-Autumn, cut and shared as an edible likeness of the moon she rules).

04Why restore Chángé in Unicode?

Plain ASCII change strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Chángé?

The core myth, fixed in the Huáinánzǐ (c. 2nd century BCE), begins with the archer Hòuyì, who had shot down nine of the ten suns and so received from Xīwángmǔ, Queen Mother of the West, the elixir of immortality. Chángé took it and floated up to the moon, where she became its goddess. The sources divide on her motive: in some versions she drinks to keep the elixir from Féngméng, Hòuyì's treacherous disciple; in others she drinks out of her own longing for deathlessness. Scholarship preserves both strands rather than adjudicating between them — the myth holds theft and sacrifice in suspension.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Chángé and related cults.
  • The flight to the moon is attested remarkably early. An early version of the story circulated in the Guǐcáng (歸藏), a divination text known from medieval quotations and from Qin-dynasty bamboo slips excavated at Wangjiatai in Hubei (tomb closed c. 3rd century BCE), in which Héng'é takes the elixir and ascends. The Huáinánzǐ (c. 139 BCE) gives the first full surviving account. Pictorial evidence is nearly as old: the painted silk funeral banner from Mawangdui Tomb 1 (sealed 168 BCE) depicts the moon disk with its toad and rabbit, and Han pictorial stones — notably from the Nanyang region — show Chángé in flight toward the moon, her robes streaming. Later dynasties rendered her on mirrors, ceramics, and festival prints, and moon-gazing scenes became a staple of Ming and Qing painting.
