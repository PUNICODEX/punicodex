01

Mythology

Stories of Brahman

Brahman has no myths — it is what myths are made of. Its ‘stories’ are the doctrine’s own ascent: the Vedic priest’s formula becoming the cosmic principle, the forest sages’ equation of the self with the all, and the grammarians’ discovery that the sacred syllable AUM is brahman compressed into sound.

Ṛgveda 10.129

The Creation Hymn

The Nāsadīya sūkta contemplates the state before creation — ‘there was neither existence nor non-existence’ — and names the One that breathed without breath. The hymn refuses to say what that One is; the later tradition named it brahman, and built the Upaniṣadic quest on the hymn’s silence.

Taittirīya Upaniṣad

Brahman as Food

The Taittirīya builds the cosmos from nourishment: food is brahman, the eater is brahman, the eater of the eater is brahman. The sequence — food, breath, mind, knowledge, bliss — is the Upaniṣadic ladder of the self, each rung a thicker description of the absolute.

Chāndogya Upaniṣad

Tat Tvam Asi

Nine times the teacher Uddālaka tells his son Śvetaketu ‘tat tvam asi’ — that thou art — reasoning from salt dissolved in water: invisible, present everywhere, the ground of taste. The sentence became the keystone of Vedānta, quoted by every commentator from Śaṅkara to the modern sages.

Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad

Neti Neti

The great dialogue has Yājñavalkya answer the question ‘what is brahman?’ only by negations — neti neti, not this, not this. The method is the tradition’s most honest: the absolute cannot be described, only approached by stripping away every description. The gods, the world, and the self remain — as its body.

02

The Absolute, the World-Ground

The Sacred Utterance That Became the All

Brahman is the load-bearing concept of Indian thought: the absolute reality beneath gods, world, and self. The word begins as the Vedic priest’s formula — the verse whose correct utterance moves the cosmos — and ends as the Upaniṣads’ world-ground, that which speech cannot reach but without which no speech sounds. In the lexicon it stands for the principle personified: not a god among gods, but what the gods are made of.

The Utterance

The Vedic brahman is the spoken formula — the verse whose pronunciation is ritual power.

The World-Ground

In the Upaniṣads, brahman is the all: “from which words turn back, together with the mind, unable to reach it”.

Tat Tvam Asi

“That thou art”: the Chandogya’s equation of brahman and the self — the tradition’s most quoted sentence.

Not This, Not This

Neti neti — the negative path: brahman defined by what it is not, since it is everything that is.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

ब्रह्मन्

The name in its original Sanskrit form. Brahman (ब्रह्मन्) is attested in the source tradition — “The sacred utterance that became the Absolute — the power of prayer expanded into the world-ground of the Upaniṣads”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

brahman

The plain brahman form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Brahman

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Brahman. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Brahman.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Brahman and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Brahman was spoken

/ˈbrɑːhmən/ Sanskrit (IAST: Brahman)
Brāh- The ā is long “ah”; the h following the m is aspirated breath — brah-, not bram-.
-ma Short final vowel; the neuter noun names the absolute itself.
-n Final n is lightly sounded; in the nominative the stem is brahma.

Modern Approximation

“BRAH-mun”

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

The word rises through three meanings: the spoken verse, the ritual power, and finally the all — the history of Indian metaphysics in one noun.
