Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the ​name is /ˈbrɑːhmən/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Brahman).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Brāh- — The ā is long “ah”; the h following the m is aspirated breath — brah-, not bram-.
  • -ma — Short final ​vowel; the neuter noun names the absolute itself.
  • -n — Final n is lightly sounded; in the nominative the stem is brahma.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: “BRAH-mun”

Kindred ​and historical forms of the name:

  • Vedic — brahman — the sacred utterance, the formula

The word rises through three meanings: the spoken ​verse, the ritual power, and finally the all — the history of Indian metaphysics in one noun.

Mythology

Brahman has no myths — it is what myths are made of. Its ‘stories’ are ​the doctrine’s own ascent: the Vedic priest’s formula becoming the cosmic principle, the forest sages’ equation of the self with the all, and the grammarians’ discovery that the sacred syllable AUM is brahman ‌compressed into sound.

The Creation Hymn (Ṛgveda 10.129)

The Nāsadīya sūkta contemplates the state before creation — ‘there was neither existence nor non-existence’ — and names the One that breathed without breath. The ​hymn refuses to say what that One is; the later tradition named it brahman, and built the Upaniṣadic quest on the hymn’s silence.

Brahman as Food (Taittirīya Upaniṣad)

The Taittirīya builds the ‌cosmos from nourishment: food is brahman, the eater is brahman, the eater of the eater is brahman. The sequence — food, breath, mind, knowledge, bliss — is the Upaniṣadic ladder of the self, ​each rung a thicker description of the absolute.

Tat Tvam Asi (Chāndogya Upaniṣad)

Nine times the teacher Uddālaka tells his son Śvetaketu ‘tat tvam asi’ — that thou art — reasoning from salt ‌dissolved in water: invisible, present everywhere, the ground of taste. The sentence became the keystone of Vedānta, quoted by every commentator from Śaṅkara to the modern sages.

Neti Neti (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad)

The great ​dialogue has Yājñavalkya answer the question ‘what is brahman?’ only by negations — neti neti, not this, not this. The method is the tradition’s most honest: the absolute cannot be described, only approached ‌by stripping away every description. The gods, the world, and the self remain — as its body.

Across Cultures

Brahman absorbed every rival ​absolute: the Vedic ṛta, the Upaniṣadic ātman, the grammarian’s śabda, and — through the Vedānta commentaries — the personal gods themselves, who turned out to be brahman’s named aspects. Buddhism defined its ​nirvāṇa partly against brahman, which kept the concept honest; and modern Indian philosophy still uses the word for the ultimate in any school.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus ‌include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.

Cultural Legacy

The word gave India its most quoted sentence (‘tat tvam asi’), its most quoted method (‘neti neti’), and its most quoted sound (AUM). ​‘Brahman’ passed into English as the learned word for the absolute; and the identification of brahman and ātman remains, after a century of translation, the central puzzle of comparative philosophy — the self ​and the world, one or two.
