Mythology

Stories of Diāna

Roman myth largely retells Artemis' stories under Diāna's name — but her cult institutions are Italy's own, older than the stories, and stranger: a grove ruled by an armed fugitive, a federal temple built as an act of constitutional politics, and a goddess whose most famous myth is a warning about looking.

The Bath

Actaeon

It is Ovid who gives the Roman telling its shape (Metamorphoses 3.138–252), and he gives it a moral that is pure Diāna: it is about seeing, not lust. Actaeon, out hunting at noon with his hounds and his horses, wanders off — whether by fate or by error, the poet hedges — into a valley where a natural arch of rock drips with spring water, and there the goddess is bathing, her nymphs crowding to shield her. Too late: he has seen. She catches up a handful of the spring and sprinkles him — 'now you may tell that you saw me, if you can' — and the antlers begin, the neck lengthens, the hands become feet, and the hunter becomes the stag. Worse follows: his own pack, which knows only the shape of prey, does not know its master's voice, and the hounds tear him down while he cries his own name. The myth is the goddess's charter: the wild is not a picture. To look at it as an object is already to have entered the hunt — on the wrong side.

The Grove

The King of the Wood

Strabo, a Greek geographer who knew Italy well, records the strangest priesthood in the Roman world (5.3.12): beside a lake in the Alban hills sacred to Diāna — Lake Nemi, which the Romans called Diana's Mirror — there stood a tree from which it was forbidden to break a bough, and the sanctuary's priest was always a runaway slave, armed with a sword, who had won his office by plucking the bough and killing his predecessor in single combat, and who held it thereafter against the next challenger, sword drawn. Ovid addresses the custom in the Fasti (3.263–272): when the priest who slew the slayer keeps watch in the lonely grove. The rite was already archaic in the Augustan age; two thousand years later it opened Frazer's Golden Bough, the book that made comparative religion a public science. The grove encodes a hard theology of the wild: possession of the sacred is never secure — it must be defended by the same violence by which it was taken, forever, at the edge of a lake that only reflects.

The Refuge

Hippolytus Reborn

Roman tradition gave Nemi a founding ghost. Virgil tells it in half a dozen lines (Aeneid 7.761–782): Virbius, the most beautiful of men, son of Hippolytus and Aricia, was raised in the goddess's grove, where horses are banned — for Hippolytus was the Greek hunter dragged to death by his own horses, and the chaste son of Theseus could not be trusted near them. Ovid gives the whole story (Metamorphoses 15.497–546): Hippolytus, dragged to pieces and brought back from the dead by Aesculapius's art at Diāna's pleading, was hidden by the goddess in her Italian grove and given a new name — Virbius, vir bis, 'twice a man.' The myth is the Roman answer to the Greek tragedy: where the Greek story kills the hunter who rejected love, the Italian sanctuary shelters and rebirths him. Nemi's grove is a hospital for the twice-living — the place where even the tragic refuse of Greek myth is given a second body and a new name, under Roman protection.

The Alliance

The Aventine Charter

Her greatest temple was a constitution. Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Antiquitates Romanae 4.25–26) describes the temple of Diāna that Servius Tullius built on the Aventine as the common sanctuary of the Latin peoples — a federal shrine where the cities of Latium met the Romans as equals, deliberately built in the Greek manner. Livy (1.45.2) fixes the foundation in Servius' reign. The dedication produced one of those characteristic Roman origin-stories about priority: at the joint festival, a Sabine declared his own ox the finest animal present and its entrails his by right; the cunning Roman priest sacrificed the beast first and carried off the liver, and the arbitration of the dispute confirmed that precedence belongs to whoever acts, not whoever talks. The sanctuary doubled as an asylum, sheltering fugitive slaves and debtors — fittingly, since its priest at Nemi was himself a fugitive. Diāna's Aventine was the one altar in Italy where Rome's future subjects practiced being partners, and the goddess of the wild presided over the city's oldest experiment in federalism.

The Maiden

Camilla, the Volscian

Virgil's last Italian heroine belongs to Diāna. Metabus, king of the Volscians and hated by his own people, fled with his infant daughter when his city burned; blockaded at the flooded river Amasenus, he lashed the baby to his spear, called on the goddess of the woods, and hurled her across the torrent — and she was caught, or the river-goddess caught her. Swearing her to virginity and to the hunt, he raised her in the wilderness, and she became the warrior-maiden of the Italian war in the Aeneid: fleet of foot, racing the wind itself, her spear-work compared to an Amazon's (7.803–817). She falls to the hidden spear of Arruns, and Diāna answers: her nymph Opis, posted on a hill for exactly this, plants an arrow in Arruns and speaks the poem's bitterest epitaph — her spoils will never be enjoyed (11.532–724). Camilla is the goddess's price and her boast: the wild breeds loyalty, and even the losing side of history gets a divine witness.

02

The Huntress of the Groves

Moon, Wild, and the King of the Wood

Diāna is Rome's own goddess before she is Artemis imported: mistress of the wildwood, the moon, and women in labour, whose strangest institution — the priest-king of Nemi who wins his office by murder — haunted anthropology for a century.

Trivia

Of the three ways — her title at the crossroads, where moon, hunt, and underworld meet.

Nemi

The lake called Diana's Mirror, sacred grove of the rex Nemorensis — the priest who slew the slayer (Strabo 5.3.12).

The Aventine

Her federal temple, founded by Servius Tullius as the common sanctuary of the Latin League (Livy 1.45.2; Dion. Hal. 4.25–26).

Actaeon's Hounds

The hunter who saw her bathing and was run down by his own pack — the wild's answer to the gaze (Ovid, Met. 3.138–252).

Sacred Symbols

The bow The hunt and the sudden death of women — her arrows strike gently, says Horace (Carm. 3.22), who asks her to spare the poet's beloved
The crescent The waxing moon she steers as Luna — and as Jana, the Old Latin feminine of Janus, the moon that opens the month
The deer Her sacred animal; the wild that cannot be owned, and the quarry Actaeon's hounds could not distinguish from their master
The oak of Nemi The grove whose bough — the Golden Bough — only a runaway slave might break, and only to begin his priesthood by murder
The shachihoko-less roofline Her Aventine temple, built in the Greek manner on Servius Tullius' foundations — the one Roman sanctuary the Latins entered as Rome's equals

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

DIANA

The name in its original Roman form. Diāna (DIANA) is attested in the source tradition — “'The bright one, goddess' (from the root *dyēu-, to shine)”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

diana

Reduced to plain diana, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Diāna

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Diāna restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Diāna.com → xn--dina-rsa.com

The non-ASCII characters in Diāna are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Diāna.

Pronunciation

How Diāna was spoken

/diˈaː.na/ Classical Latin

Modern Approximation

dee-AH-nah, with the long ā on the stressed second syllable.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Latin Dĭāna scans with a short i, a long penultimate ā, and a short final -a; the owned restoration Diāna marks the long ā exactly where the classical quantity puts it. Roman grammarians connected the name to the shining of the moon (from the same root as Iūpiter's Dyēus, 'the bright sky'), making her the feminine face of celestial light.

Extended Lore

Diāna is the wild that insists on a temple. Her priest wins office by violence in her most peaceful grove; her deer are protected by being hunted only by her. She asks the oldest question of stewardship: whether anything can be preserved without being owned — and answers with a lake that reflects the moon without holding it.
