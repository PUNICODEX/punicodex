Why Mārs belongs in your address bar

Every address bar is a choice. When you type Mārs, you are not typing ​a novelty; you are restoring a name that the early DNS, built for English typewriters, could not carry. The plain ASCII form mars is a leftover of that constraint, not the name itself. This post is the long version of the restoration: ‌where the name comes from, how the Original Script tradition wrote it, how it is pronounced, what the myths and the material record preserve, and why its Unicode form now lives as a working domain. The claim throughout is simple — the original ‌spelling is not decoration. It is the name.

Overview

Mārs (mars) — Father of Rome · The Red Planet — belongs to the Roman tradition, where ​it is catalogued under the domain "War, Agriculture, Sovereignty". The name means "The Roman god of war and guardian of agriculture, father of Rōmulus and Rēmus; namesake of the planet Mars and the month of March (epigraphic MARS)".

Mārs is the god ​the Greeks would not have recognized. Where Árēs is battle-lust and gets laughed off Olympus, Mārs is war as a Roman office — disciplined, auspicious, and owed to the state: the spear that stands in the Regia and moves when the res publica ​marches. But he is also the god of the field itself — of boundaries and crops and the spring lustration — and the father of Rōmulus and Rēmus, which makes him the only great god who is literally Rome's ancestor.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /maːrs/ — Classical Latin Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • M- — Bilabial nasal [m] — the lips close before ‌the name opens, a held breath before the vowel that carries everything.
  • -ārs — Long open ā [aː] — Lewis & Short print Mārs against the genitive Mārtis — sustained twice a short a's length, then the rolled r [r] and ​the dry final s: one syllable, hard and complete.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'MAHRS' — a single syllable; hold the open 'ah' longer than English wants to, let the r roll, and close it with a hiss.

Kindred and ​historical forms of the name:

  • Latin — Mārs, genitive Mārtis (Lewis & Short) — the stem Mārt- also fathers Mārtius 'of March' and martiālis, whence 'martial.'
  • Old Latin — Māvors, the archaic form the poets never dropped; Ovid plays Mavors ​against Mars within a single line.
  • Greek rendering — Ἄρης — Greek writers call him by their own war god's name, which is precisely the problem: the translation flattens what Rome insisted on keeping distinct.
  • Etymology — Disputed. A connection to ​the Vedic storm-deities the Maruts through a reconstructed *Māvort- is often proposed but unproven; antiquity offered derivations from Greek μάχη 'battle' and from mā 'male' with equal confidence and equal evidence.

Mārs is Tier 1 because the ā is long — the ​quantity is guaranteed by the genitive Mārtis and by Latin prosody — and that single macron is the one feature the ASCII 'mars' flattens. The epigraphy should be stated plainly: Roman inscriptions carve MARS with no length mark at all. The long vowel ‌is restored, not carved; the restoration is philology doing its honest work.

Mythology

Mārs enters myth almost entirely as a father — and what he fathers is the city. His stories are told by Rome's historians, not by its Homer, and ​they all serve one thesis: that Roman power is divine in origin and therefore answerable to ritual. The Vestal and the she-wolf, the shield from heaven, the old king fooled at a wedding: each myth is a charter for a piece of the ‌state cult.

Rhea Silvia at the River (The Vestal and the Twins)

Livy opens the Roman story with a vestal crime that is really a divine plan (1.3–4). Numitor, rightful king of Alba Longa, is deposed by his brother Amulius, who ​forces Numitor's daughter, the Vestal Rhea Silvia, into perpetual virginity so the line cannot challenge him. But the girl, sent to fetch water for a sacrifice, is overpowered in the sacred grove by the god — or so she swears, 'by the god ‌himself, she said that Mars was the author of her seized virginity' — and bears twin boys. Amulius orders them drowned in the Tiber; the river is in flood, the basket grounds at the fig-tree Ruminalis, and a she-wolf comes down from ​the hills to nurse them in the cave of the Lupercal, while a woodpecker flies food to the infants. The shepherd Faustulus finds them; his wife Acca Larentia raises them; and the twins grow up to restore Numitor and found a city of ‌their own on the Palatine. Livy reports the whole wonder-story and then, with the honesty that makes him Rome's first historian, adds that he gives it as it has come down, 'more adorned with poetry than burdened by proof.' The myth needs ​no proof: it explains why the war god's temple stands at the heart of the city his sons built.

Numa and the Ancile (The Shield from Heaven)

In Numa's peaceful reign — the king who talked Jupiter himself out of human sacrifice ‌with a handful of meal and onions — a shield fell from the sky, the ancile, with a promise that Rome's fate hung upon it. The problem was theft: one true shield, an empire to protect, and every enemy a potential thief. ​So the king set the cunning smith Mamurius Veturius a test he could not be told about — make eleven copies so exact that even Numa could not pick the divine original — and the twelve ancilia were entrusted to the Salii, the ​'leapers,' priests of Mars who danced through the city each March bearing the shields on their left arms and singing a carmen so ancient that even Augustus's scholars confessed they could barely construe it. The smith who made them was driven out, ‌or stoned, or honored — the versions disagree, and Ovid is visibly embarrassed by the tradition — but the ancilia stayed, and were still being carried when the empire was Christian. Mars's shield, like Rome itself, was a copy indistinguishable from the original; ‌that was the whole point.

The Vow at Philippi (The Avenger)

The greatest Mars myth is not archaic at all — it is Augustan, and paid for in blood. After Caesar's murder the nineteen-year-old Octavian marched with Antony against the Liberatores, and ‌at Philippi in 42 BC Brutus and Cassius fell, one by the sword, one by his own hand. Octavian had vowed a temple to Mars the Avenger — Mars Ultor — if he should win, and forty years later, in 2 BC, ​he dedicated it in his new Forum, flanked by colossal statues of the god in crested helmet, spear grounded, breastplate crowded with myth. The temple held the sword of the murdered Caesar and the standards recovered from the Parthians — Crassus's lost eagles ​finally retrieved by diplomacy and displayed as war won. Ovid, who was there, makes the dedication Rome's birthday party (Fasti 5.545–598): the new theater, the games, the imperial family on the podium, and Mars's godhead finally visible — for until the temple ‌rose, the Romans admitted, no image of Mars had any reliable claim to show what the god looked like. The myth is exactly as blunt as its name: the man who avenged the father became the son of a god, and Mars was ​the instrument.

Mars and Anna Perenna (The Old King Fooled)

Ovid is the only source, and he is delighted to be it (Fasti 3.523–710). Old Mars, he says, had fallen in love — with the warlike maiden Ana Perenna? No: with ​Minerva herself, who with a goddess's perfect tact referred him onward: 'you are old; she is young' — go woo Anna, 'the years' own goddess.' Anna knew what the old god was after and, on their wedding night, wrapped herself in a veil, ‌went to the bedroom in the dark, and — had placed the aged crone in the bed instead. When Mars at last lifted the veil by the lamp's light, the laugh was heard all over heaven, and Anna was raised to the ​skies as the river of years. The story is the Fasti's gift to Mars: the most ferocious of the gods made ridiculous by age and desire, and answered not with thunder but with a practical joke — Roman humor at the war god's ​expense, told in the month of his festival.

Mars for Turnus (The Enemy of Aeneas)

In Virgil's Aeneid Mars does for the losing side what he never does for Rome: he loves it. His favorite is Turnus, the Rutulian prince who ‌defends Italy against the Trojan landing, and the god moves through the last books like a pressure behind the war: he sends Allecto, the Fury, to kindle the conflict in Book 7; he strikes Turnus with a courage beyond his own when the ​prince pursues a phantom Aeneas onto a ship and is carried helpless from the field in Book 9; and in Book 12, when the treaty breaks, Mars arms him for the last duel. Yet Jupiter has already weighed the fates, and not ‌even the war god's advocacy moves the scale. Mars rages; fate is silent and exact. The scene is the Aeneid's quiet commentary on the god Rome trusted most: Mars can arm a hero, but he cannot assign the victory — and when Rome ​itself is at stake, the poet reminds us, the god of war fights on the other side too.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Mārs concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

​- Hasta Martis — The spear of Mars kept in the Regia at the hearth of the pontifex maximus. When Rome went to war the arms were 'moved' — the sign by which the whole city knew the campaign had begun; the spear ​stood at the exact center of the state.

  • Ancile — The shield that fell from heaven in Numa's reign and could not stay still while Rome's safety hung on it. The smith Mamurius Veturius forged eleven perfect copies so no thief ​could tell the true one; all twelve, the ancilia, were carried through the city each March by the dancing Salii.
  • The she-wolf — The lupa who found the exposed twins at the fig-tree of Rumina and suckled them in the cave of ‌the Lupercal. She is Mars's creature doing Mars's work — his sons fed by his own landscape — and she outlasted the republic on every coin.
  • The woodpecker (picus) — Mars's bird, sacred above others: in some accounts it was the ‌woodpecker that flew down to feed the crying twins when the she-wolf was away, and Ovid's Mavors swears his oath by 'the woodpecker and the wolf, my birds and my beasts.'
  • The October Horse — The right-hand trace-horse of the winning chariot ​at the October races on the Campus Martius, sacrificed to Mars on the Ides of October; its head was fought over by the Subura and the Via Sacra, and its blood was kept at the Regia.
  • The crested helmet of Gradivus ‌— Gradivus — 'he who marches forth,' from gradior — is Mars in the field: the god strides out before the army in crested helmet and cuirass, which is how the Forum of Augustus set him in marble, forty feet over the Avenger's ​temple.

Archaeology & Evidence

The Temple ‌of Mars Ultor in the Forum of Augustus, excavated and partially re-erected, held its colossal caryatid-flanked exedrae and fragments of the cult statues; its dedication inscription (CIL VI 872) survives. The temple of Mars in Circo, vowed after the Gallic capture and ‌dedicated in 388 BC, stood in the Campus Flaminius. The Regia in the Forum — rebuilt in imperial times over archaic foundations — housed the hasta Martis and the October Horse's blood. The Altar of Mars and Venus in the Forum Boarium, vowed ‌in 296 BC, celebrated the Julian claim of descent. Roman denarii from 137 BC onward stamp the she-wolf and twins on thousands of reverses, and imperial issues add Mars Ultor crowning a trophy — the god as Rome's most reliable mint-mark.

Disciplined War

Roman war under auspice and command — the legion, not the brawl. Mars receives victory owed, not rage indulged; Cicero allows war only ever to be waged to live in peace.

Guardian of Fields

​The field-god who keeps boundaries and crops: Cato's farmers purify the land with a prayer to Mars before the plough, and the Arval Brethren still dance for him at their grove.

Father of the Twins

By the Vestal Rhea Silvia he ​fathers Rōmulus and Rēmus; the she-wolf nurses them at the Lupercal, and from Romulus the city takes both its king and its name.

Mars Ultor

'Mars the Avenger' — vowed by the young Octavian before Philippi, paid with the greatest temple of ‌the age, dedicated 2 BC in the Forum of Augustus.

Across Cultures

The interpretatio graeca named Mars 'the Roman Ares,' and the identification never stopped misleading people. Greek writers rendered him Ἄρης without hesitation; Roman poets borrowed Ares's mythology wholesale; yet ​the cults were opposite in spirit. Árēs owned almost no temples and was despised; Mars owned some of Rome's greatest and was Rome's ancestor — the Salii danced for him, the Arval Brethren sang for him, and the old Roman year itself began ​in his month. Outside the Greek equation he traveled as a template: the Celts poured their own gods into his name, so that Roman Britain knows Mars Camulos, Gaul knows Mars Lenus and Mars Loucetius ('lightning'), and Tacitus reports the Suebi worshipping ​a Mars Thingsus, a 'Mars of the assembly.' The pattern is interpretatio in its purest form: a local deity kept entirely, a Roman label laid over him, and both names entered on the same altar.

Cultural Legacy

Mars is the most quietly successful name in the Western calendar. The ​month of March — Latin Martius, the first month of the old Roman year, when the spear moved and the campaigning season opened — still carries him; the weekday dies Martis became French mardi, Spanish martes, Italian martedì, and rode the loan into ​Japanese kasei and Korean hwasŏng as the name of the planet. 'Martial' preserves the stem in every English court martial and martial law; the Campus Martius, Rome's field of Mars, survives as the name of a district in the capital itself and ​as the common noun 'campus.' The planet, blood-red to every unaided eye, made him the sky's permanent soldier long after the last Salius had stopped dancing.

A Meditation

Mārs is the rare war god who is also a farmer's god, and the ‌combination is not a puzzle but a doctrine. Roman war and Roman agriculture were the same activity at different seasons: the boundary-stone guarded in peace is the frontier defended in war, and the men who hold the spear in summer are the ​men who hold the plough in spring. Mars stands at that hinge — Gradivus marching out in March, the October Horse thanked at the season's close — and the state that honored him knew exactly what it was saying: that its violence and ‌its husbandry were one continuous act of possession.

The restoration of the long ā is a small act of the same kind. The inscriptions say MARS; the prosody says Mārs; Lewis & Short print the macron because the genitive Mārtis leaves no ​choice. To flatten the name to 'mars' is to lose the one thing Latin actually tells us about how it sounded — a vowel held twice its length before the roll of the r, a syllable built like the god's own discipline: close, ‌sustained, and complete.

The Domain ‌Name

The restored name is live as a working domain: mārs.com, which the DNS carries in punycode form as xn--mrs-1oa.com — an ASCII-compatible encoding that lets a non-ASCII name travel the global network without breaking older infrastructure. The visitor sees Mārs; the machines ‌see the encoding. That duality is the engineering compromise on which the entire restoration rests, and it is why a name written the way its own tradition wrote it in Original Script can now be typed into any browser on earth.
