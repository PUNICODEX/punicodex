Mythology

Stories of Satī

Satī's story is told at length in the Śiva Purāṇa and the Skanda Purāṇa, with variant retellings across the Purāṇic corpus and Kālidāsa's account of her rebirth. It is a drama of honor, love, and the catastrophic consequences of refusing a daughter's chosen god — the founding tragedy of the Śaiva cosmos.

Marriage

The Marriage of Satī and Śiva

Satī was born to Dakṣa and his wife Prasūti as the incarnation of the supreme goddess Śakti, and from childhood she set her heart on Śiva — the ascetic who wore the ashes of cremation grounds, a garland of skulls, and a serpent for a cord, lord of yogins and dweller at the edge of society (Śiva Purāṇa, Rudrasaṃhitā, Satīkhaṇḍa). Dakṣa despised him: what father gives his daughter to a beggar smeared with funeral dust? Satī, rejected and mocked, withdrew to the mountain and performed austerities until Śiva, tested and satisfied, accepted her. Their marriage was celebrated by the gods against the father's silence, and she went to live with her husband on Kailāsa, the mountain that is both throne and hermitage. The union the myth celebrates is also the wound it will open: householder wed to renouncer, daughter to a father who could not forgive her choice. Every later catastrophe of the Śaiva cycle flows from that first exclusion.

The Sacrifice

Dakṣa's Yajña at Kankhal

Dakṣa arranged a great sacrifice at Kankhal, near the Ganges' descent, and invited every god, sage, and celestial being — omitting Śiva, his own son-in-law, and posting guards against his entry (Śiva Purāṇa 2.2; Skanda Purāṇa, Kāśīkhaṇḍa). Satī begged to attend her father's festival; Śiva warned her that a gift given in contempt is poison, but let her go with his gaṇas. At the rite she was received not with welcome but with a public tirade — Dakṣa reviled Śiva as a corpse-dweller, a skull-bearer, no true god at all. The assembled deities hung their heads; no one defended her. Then the myth takes its terrible turn: Satī does not curse the assembly and does not weep. She invokes her inner fire — the yogāgni of the goddess within — and abandons her body on the spot, before the eyes of the sacrifice she had come to honor. The altar raised to bind the cosmos together becomes the pyre that unmakes a daughter's place in her father's house.

Aftermath

Śiva's Grief and the Sacking of the Sacrifice

News reached Kailāsa, and the ascetic who had conquered desire by yoga discovered its full violence (Śiva Purāṇa 2.2; Skanda Purāṇa). From a lock of his matted hair he plucked two hairs and dashed them to the ground: from one rose Vīrabhadra, a warrior like a storm-cloud, and from the other Bhadrakālī, dark and laughing. They marched on Kankhal with the gaṇas, broke the sacrifice, scattered the gods, and beheaded Dakṣa — who was later restored, in some tellings with a goat's head, humbled and reconciled. Śiva himself came, took up Satī's body, and wandered the cosmos in the Tāṇḍava, the dance that shakes worlds, until the gods begged Viṣṇu to intervene; his discus cut the corpse apart, and the pieces fell across the subcontinent. The grief of the unmoved god is the myth's center: yoga had not removed love, only taught it where to break.

The Pīṭhas

The Body and the Map of India

Where each fragment of Satī's body fell, a Śakti-pīṭha arose — the 'seats of power' that map the goddess across India (Devībhāgavata Purāṇa 7; the lists vary between 51 and 52 sites). Her yoni fell at Kāmākhyā in Assam; her tongue at Jwalamukhi in the Punjab hills; her eyes at Nainital; each pīṭha sanctifying a different member of the dismembered wife. The geography is devotional, not surgical: the myth turns a catastrophe of patriarchal honor into a country-wide network of goddess-shrines, so that from Kashmir to Kanyakumari no pilgrim is far from Satī. The theological move is quietly radical — the father tried to erase the daughter from the sacrifice, and instead the daughter became the ground every sacrifice stands on. Satī worshipped at Kankhal itself is called Dākṣāyaṇī, 'Dakṣa's daughter', the very name the insult had thrown at her now an honorific of the shrine.

Rebirth

Born Again as Pārvatī

The Purāṇas carry the story past death. Satī was reborn to Himavat, the mountain-king, and his queen Menā, and named Pārvatī, 'daughter of the mountain' (Śiva Purāṇa, Umāsaṃhitā; Kālidāsa, Kumārasambhava 5–8). She grew into beauty and resolve, and when her father despaired of finding her a husband she answered that she would have Śiva or no one. Her austerities drew the ascetic's attention; he tested her in disguise — an old Brahmin mocking the god she loved — and her defense of Śiva convinced him. But the wooing nearly failed before it began: the love-god Kāma shot his flower-arrow at the meditating god and was burned to ash for it, until Pārvatī's penance itself won the marriage the gods needed, for only a child of Śiva and Śakti could kill the demon Tāraka. The rebirth is the tradition's answer to the first tragedy: Satī returns not as victim but as sovereign, the Śakti without whom the great god is a corpse.

02

Goddess of Marital Fidelity

First Wife of Śiva, Daughter of Dakṣa

Satī is the first consort of Śiva, the daughter of the proud Prajāpati Dakṣa. Her myth is the hinge on which the larger Śaiva narrative turns: an insult to her husband, an unbearable grief, and a self-immolation so total that it forces the god of destruction himself to destroy the sacrifice.

Divine Consort

She is Śiva's first wife, drawn to his ascetic power against her father's wishes.

Daughter of Dakṣa

Born to the patriarch Dakṣa, she embodies the conflict between household and hermitage.

Self-Immolation

She destroys her body by inner fire after Dakṣa insults Śiva.

Rebirth as Pārvatī

After death she is reborn as Pārvatī, who finally wins Śiva as her husband again.

Sacred Symbols

Sacred fire (agni) The yogic fire (yogāgni) from within, by which Satī abandons her body — Dakṣa's own sacrificial element turned against his rite.
Trident (triśūla) Śiva's weapon, symbol of the divine marriage she defends to the death.
Lotus Purity and unwavering devotion, especially her steadfast love for Śiva from childhood.
Wedding garland The marriage bond that Dakṣa's pride violates and Śiva's grief avenges.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

सती

The name in its original Sanskrit form. Satī (सती) is attested in the source tradition — “The True One — daughter of Dakṣa and first wife of Śiva, who immolated herself at her father's sacrifice”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

sati

Reduced to plain sati, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Satī

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Satī restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Satī.com → xn--sat-wta.com

The non-ASCII characters in Satī are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Satī.

Pronunciation

How Satī was spoken

/sɐ.tiː/ Sanskrit Reconstruction
S- Voiceless dental sibilant [s]; the initial of the participle sat.
-a- Short open central vowel [ɐ].
-t- Voiceless unaspirated dental stop [t̪]; Sanskrit t is pronounced with the tongue at the teeth, and the retroflex series is a separate set (ṭ, ṭh, ḍ, ḍh, ṇ).
-ī Long close front vowel [iː], marked by the macron/dīrgha sign.

Modern Approximation

'Suh-TEE' — keep the first vowel short and crisp, and lengthen the final 'ee'. In careful Sanskrit the t is dental, the tongue touching the teeth.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Satī is Tier 1: the macron on ī marks the long vowel of the Sanskrit feminine, a distinctive feature the ASCII form loses. The name is not merely a title but the proper name of Śiva's first wife and a goddess in her own right.

Extended Lore

Satī is the goddess who chooses her own end, and that choice has been read in opposite ways for centuries: as the ultimate devotion, or as the ultimate destruction of female agency. Both readings miss something. Satī's act is not about obedience; it is about the unbearability of a world in which her husband is publicly humiliated.
