The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Midir

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Midir is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Judge”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

midir

The plain midir form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Midir

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Midir. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Midir.com → midir.com

The non-ASCII characters in Midir are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Midir.

Pronunciation

How Midir was spoken

/ˈmʲiː.ðʲɪɾʲ/ Old Irish Reconstruction
M- Slender (palatalized) bilabial nasal [mʲ], the m pronounced with the tongue arched toward the hard palate — Old Irish distinguished slender from broad consonants throughout.
-í- Long close front vowel [iː], the long í that modern editors mark with a síneadh fada when they print Mídir.
-d- Lenited dental [ð], the soft voiced 'th' of English 'this'; Old Irish orthography writes the lenited consonant with a plain d.
-ir Short unstressed [ɪ] closing in a slender (palatalized) tap [ɾʲ], the light final r of the o-stem name.

Modern Approximation

'MEE-thir' — a long 'ee', the soft 'th' of 'this' for the d, and a whispered palatal r at the end. The modern Irish Mídír keeps essentially this sound.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Irish Midir of Brí Léith; the genealogical tradition makes him a son of the Dagda
  • Manuscript form Midir, the spelling printed by Bergin and Best from Lebor na hUidre
  • Modern Irish Mídír, with the fada marking the long vowel
  • Epithet Mídir the Proud, the handbooks' standing rendering of his style
  • Anglicized None — unlike Angus, the name never wore down into a living given name

The Accent / Script Rule

Midir is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the temple form is plain ASCII, with no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the restoration to rescue. The fada in Mídir belongs to modern editorial convention, not to the manuscript norm that the project follows, and the mechanical rule judges the restored form, not the editorial one. The capital alone distinguishes the temple form from the input; the god's standing in the tradition is beside the point.

05

Underworld, Fairy King

The proud lord of the síd of Brí Léith

Midir — Old Irish Mídir, styled the Proud — is the lord of the síd of Brí Léith, the Otherworld mound by Ardagh in Longford, and the central figure of Tochmarc Étaíne, the Wooing of Étaín. His dossier is the strangest kind of kingship in the Irish corpus: not the spear or the plunder, but the board game, the wager, and the patience that spans a thousand and twelve years and two births of the same woman. He fostered [[aengus|Óengus]], the son of the young; he won Étaín Echraide by her father's impossible labors; he lost her to jealousy, found her again in another lifetime, and took her from the high king of Ireland at a game of fidchell played in the middle of Tara. Where other gods take what they want, Midir stakes, loses, waits, and wins.

The Fidchell Board

The game of kings at which Midir sat across from Eochaid Airem in Tara, losing stake after stake until the board was set for the one prize he had come for.

The Two Swans

The payment taken in flight: Midir and Étaín rising through Tara's smoke-hole as two swans linked by a silver chain, while the king's guard watched from the walls.

Brí Léith

His síd by Ardagh in Longford — the mound Eochaid's host dug open in the war of the spades, and the hill the Dindsenchas names as his.

The Scarlet Quicken-Rod

Fuamnach's jealous stroke in his own hall, which made Étaín a pool of water, a worm, and a purple fly — the malice his kingship could not keep out.

06

Mythology

Stories of Midir

Midir's mythology is a single long courtship told in two lifetimes — the fullest Otherworld dossier in the Irish corpus, preserved entire in Tochmarc Étaíne. It begins with a sloe-thorn and a bride-price, passes through jealousy and transformation and a rebirth eleven hundred years later, and ends at a fidchell board in Tara and a war of spades against the hollow hills. Around that spine the tradition adds the fosterage: the lord of Brí Léith reared [[aengus|Óengus]], the Dagda's son, and the tie between the two mounds frames both gods' tales.

Tochmarc Étaíne

The Sloe and the Bride-Price

Midir of Brí Léith was the foster-father of Óengus, and he came to Brú na Bóinne to visit his fosterling. While the boys of the Brug were at their hurling, a sloe-thorn sprang from the play and put out Midir's eye; he stayed a year in the mound while Dían Cécht, the physician of the Túatha Dé Danann, made him whole. For his reward and his heart's desire he named Étaín Echraide, daughter of Ailill, the fairest woman in Ireland — and it was Óengus who went to win her.

Her father Ailill mac Máta, king of the Ulaid, set a bride-price no mortal could pay: twelve plains cleared of their stones and wood, and twelve rivers led out of the land to the sea. Óengus performed the labors with the Dagda's strength behind him, and Midir brought Étaín home to Brí Léith as his wife. The tale's arithmetic is exact: the god's first possession of Étaín was bought by service, the price of a wounded eye — and it was the one part of the story that ended peaceably.

Tochmarc Étaíne

The Fly in the Crystal Bower

Midir already had a wife: Fuamnach, foster-daughter of Bresal the druid, and versed in her foster-father's arts. When Étaín came home to Brí Léith, Fuamnach's jealousy found its instrument — a rod of scarlet quicken-tree — and one stroke made her rival a pool of water, then a worm, then a purple fly of wondrous size and sweetness. For seven years the fly that was Étaín went with Midir wherever he went, and he knew her in her shape; but Fuamnach's malice outlasted his keeping.

The fly came at last to Brú na Bóinne, where Óengus knew her and built her a grianán, a crystal sun-bower filled with healing herbs — until Fuamnach raised a magic wind that swept her across Ireland for seven years more, to fall into the cup of the wife of Étar, an Ulster warrior, at the Bay of Cíú. She was drunk, conceived, and born again a thousand and twelve years after her first birth: Étaín, daughter of Étar. Midir's house is the still center of the storm — the husband whose hall became the battlefield of two women's powers, and who waited out the centuries the tale demanded of him.

Tochmarc Étaíne

The Long Game at Tara

In her second life Étaín married Eochaid Airem, high king of Ireland at Tara, and Midir came for her as the tale had promised. First he came in the shape of Ailill Anglonnach, the king's brother, who was wasting with love of her — three nights he lay beside her in that likeness and did not touch her, and so saved both her honor and Ailill's life. Then he revealed himself and asked her to come away with him; she answered that she would not go without her husband's leave. So Midir took the leave the hard way.

He came to Tara and challenged Eochaid to fidchell. Game after game Midir lost, and paid the stakes without stint — horses and treasures in such plenty that the king's greed was schooled by the god's purse. Then the board was set once more, and the stake named was an embrace and a kiss from Étaín; Midir won. Eochaid deferred the payment for a month, and when the month was out Tara stood ringed with warriors and the house barred. Midir appeared all the same in the midst of the hall, sang to Étaín the invitation to the wondrous land, took her in his arms, and the two rose through the smoke-hole as two swans linked by a silver chain, while the host of Ireland watched from the ramparts and could do nothing.

Tochmarc Étaíne

The Fifty Women in One Likeness

Eochaid's answer was the spade: he set the men of Ireland to digging up the síd-mounds one by one, a war of delving against the hollow hills, until the host stood over Brí Léith itself. Then Midir came to the parley, and the bargain struck was surrender of a kind: on the morrow fifty women came out of the mound, every one of them in Étaín's exact likeness, and the king was told to choose his wife.

He chose — and chose his own daughter, born to Étaín in the síd and hidden in the likeness of her mother. By that unwitting union a daughter was conceived who was exposed and spared, reared as Mess Búachalla, the cowherd's fosterling, and through her the line ran down to Conaire Mór, the doomed king of the Bruiden Da Derga. When Midir revealed the deception he gave back the true Étaín as well; the tale lets the god keep the last word and the king keep his shame. It is the Otherworld's coldest jest: the wager was never about the game, and the digging was never going to win.

De Gabáil in t-Sída

The Foster-Father of the Young God

Behind the wooing stands the fosterage. When the Dagda divided the síd-mounds of Ireland among the Túatha Dé Danann, his son Óengus was away with Midir of Brí Léith, being fostered, and received nothing — the absence that led to the famous quibble of the day and the night, and to Brú na Bóinne passing to the son. The tradition thus ties the two mounds together from the beginning: the young god reared at Brí Léith, the proud god's eye healed at the Brug.

The foster-bond runs both ways through the dossier. It was for Midir that Óengus won Étaín Echraide and performed her father's labors; it was in Óengus's bower that Étaín the purple fly found her one refuge. Irish fosterage was no light tie — it stood beside blood, and the tales honor it. Midir's proudest title is not lord of Brí Léith but the one the stories keep proving: the man who raised the son of the young.

Extended Lore

Midir is the god of the long game, and the tradition tells you so in his name before it tells you in his story: the handbooks gloss it, cautiously, as judge — and a judge is exactly what a wager needs. Watch how he plays Eochaid. He loses, and loses, and pays without stint, because the losses are the lesson: the king is being taught that the man across the board can afford anything, that the stakes are a language, and that the only sentence being written is the last one. When the final stake is named — an embrace, a kiss — the game has already been won for a month. The fidchell board is a court, and the house always already holds the verdict.
