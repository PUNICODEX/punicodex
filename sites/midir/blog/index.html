The hidden history behind Midir

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Midir is the quieter case, and the more instructive one: the proudest host in Irish mythology, the god who won his wife back from the high king of Ireland at a board game, attached to a name so plain that the medieval manuscripts wrote it in unbroken ASCII. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, script, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the lord of Brí Léith has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII midir.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Midir (midir) — Underworld, Fairy King · Judge — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Underworld, Fairy King". The name's traditional gloss is "Judge", though its etymology is registered with caution rather than settled.

Midir — Old Irish Mídir, styled the Proud — is the lord of the síd of Brí Léith, the Otherworld mound by Ardagh in Longford: foster-father of Óengus, husband of Fuamnach and of Étaín, and the central figure of Tochmarc Étaíne, the Wooing of Étaín. His dossier is the strangest kind of kingship in the Irish corpus: not the spear or the plunder, but the board game, the wager, and the patience that spans a thousand and twelve years and two births of the same woman.

PuniCodex restores the name as Midir — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Irish writes no diacritic in this name; the fada of modern editorial Mídir is a convention, not the manuscript norm. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the god. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII midir.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old and Middle Irish as Midir, the spelling Bergin and Best print from the twelfth-century Lebor na hUidre; many modern editors add the fada — Mídir — to mark the long vowel, an editorial convention rather than the manuscript norm. The handbooks keep his standing epithet: Mídir the Proud, the proud host of Brí Léith.

The etymology is genuinely open, and this temple keeps it open. The reading the reference works register derives the name from the root med- — 'to judge, to measure, to rule' — with Old Irish midithir, 'judges', as the comparand: a name that would suit the god of the wager down to the ground. The derivation is hedged in the dictionaries themselves, and the hedge is part of the record.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in medieval Irish manuscripts in the Latin alphabet as Midir — Old Irish in Insular minuscule, attested in the great codices that preserve Tochmarc Étaíne, above all Lebor na hUidre, with the text completed from the fifteenth-century Yellow Book of Lecan.

The earlier monumental alphabet, ogham, predates the manuscript tradition and survives on some four hundred stones bearing personal names in formulaic genitives — but no attestation of this theonym has ever been read on any of them, and none would be expected: ogham stones mark graves and boundaries, not votive dedications. The manuscript form is the primary witness, and it is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈmʲiː.ðʲɪɾʲ/ — Old Irish Reconstruction.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: MEE-thir — a long 'ee', the soft 'th' of 'this' for the lenited d, and a whispered palatal r at the end. The modern Irish Mídír keeps essentially this sound.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Irish — Midir of Brí Léith; the genealogical tradition makes him a son of the Dagda
  • Manuscript form — Midir, as printed by Bergin and Best from Lebor na hUidre
  • Modern Irish — Mídír, with the fada marking the long vowel
  • Epithet — Mídir the Proud, the handbooks' standing rendering of his style
  • Anglicized — none: unlike Angus, the name never wore down into a living given name

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in all Old Irish. The m is slender, the tongue arched toward the palate; the í is long; and the final r is a light palatal tap.

Mythology

Midir's mythology is a single long courtship told in two lifetimes, preserved entire in Tochmarc Étaíne — the fullest Otherworld dossier in the Irish corpus.

The Sloe and the Bride-Price (Tochmarc Étaíne I)

Midir of Brí Léith, foster-father of Óengus, came to Brú na Bóinne to visit his fosterling. While the boys of the Brug were at their hurling, a sloe-thorn sprang from the play and put out Midir's eye; he stayed a year in the mound while Dían Cécht, the physician of the Túatha Dé Danann, made him whole. For his reward he named Étaín Echraide, daughter of Ailill, the fairest woman in Ireland — and Óengus won her from her father Ailill mac Máta, king of the Ulaid, by performing the demanded labors: twelve plains cleared of their stones, twelve rivers led to the sea. Midir brought Étaín home to Brí Léith as his wife.

The Fly in the Crystal Bower (Tochmarc Étaíne II)

Fuamnach, Midir's first wife and foster-daughter of Bresal the druid, struck her rival with a rod of scarlet quicken-tree: Étaín became a pool of water, a worm, and a purple fly. The fly went with Midir for seven years, and he knew her in her shape; she found refuge in Óengus's crystal bower at the Brug until Fuamnach's wind swept her across Ireland for seven years more, to fall into the cup of Étar's wife at the Bay of Cíú and be born again a thousand and twelve years after her first birth — Étaín, daughter of Étar. Midir's hall is the still center of the storm: the husband who waited out the centuries the tale demanded of him.

The Long Game at Tara (Tochmarc Étaíne III)

In her second life Étaín married Eochaid Airem, high king of Ireland at Tara. Midir came first in the likeness of the king's love-sick brother Ailill Anglonnach, lying beside her three nights without touching her; then he revealed himself, and she answered that she would not go without her husband's leave. He took the leave at the fidchell board: game after game he lost, paying stakes without stint — horses and treasures in such plenty that the king's greed was schooled by the god's purse — until the final stake was named, an embrace and a kiss from Étaín, and Midir won. When the month's deferral was out, Tara stood ringed with warriors and the house barred; Midir appeared all the same in the midst of the hall, sang to her the invitation to the wondrous land, took her in his arms, and the two rose through the smoke-hole as two swans linked by a silver chain.

The Fifty Women in One Likeness (Tochmarc Étaíne III)

Eochaid's answer was the spade: the men of Ireland dug up the síd-mounds one by one until the host stood over Brí Léith itself. Midir came to the parley, and the bargain was surrender of a kind: fifty women in Étaín's exact likeness came out of the mound, and the king was told to choose his wife. He chose his own daughter, born to Étaín in the síd and hidden in her mother's likeness — the unwitting union from which came Mess Búachalla, grandmother of Conaire Mór. When Midir revealed the deception he returned the true Étaín as well, and kept the last word. The wager was never about the game, and the digging was never going to win.

The Foster-Father of the Young God (De Gabáil in t-Sída)

Behind the wooing stands the fosterage. When the Dagda divided the síd-mounds of Ireland among the Túatha Dé Danann, Óengus was away with Midir at Brí Léith, being fostered, and received nothing — the absence that led to the famous quibble of the day and the night, and to Brú na Bóinne passing to the son. Irish fosterage stood beside blood, and the tales honor it: Midir's proudest title is not lord of Brí Léith but the man who raised the son of the young.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Midir is the iconography of his patience — a board, a chain, a thorn, a hill, and a riddle of faces:

  • The fidchell board — the game of kings played at Tara: patience staked as strategy, and a wager that was really a judgment
  • The silver chain — the link between the two swans that rose through Tara's smoke-hole
  • The sloe-thorn — the flying thorn that put out his eye at the Brug and set the whole wooing in motion
  • Brí Léith — his síd by Ardagh in Longford, the mound the host of Ireland dug open
  • The fifty women in one likeness — the riddle of resemblance at the mound's surrender

Archaeology & Evidence

Midir is a textual god — no ogham stone names him and no votive inscription invokes him — but both halves of his mythic geography are real and visitable. Brí Léith is a hill by Ardagh in County Longford, the mound the Dindsenchas names as his síd, and the surrounding bogland preserves one of the showpiece monuments of the Irish Iron Age: the Corlea Trackway, a massive oak togher across the bog a few miles south of Ardagh, excavated under Barry Raftery and fixed by dendrochronology to 148–147 BCE. The tale's other stage is Tara in County Meath, where the earthworks of Ráith na Rí, the Forrad, and the long banquet-hall trench called Tech Midchúarta still ridge the hill on which Eochaid Airem kept his court and lost his wager. The manuscript witnesses are exact: Tochmarc Étaíne is preserved, incompletely, in the twelfth-century Lebor na hUidre and completed in the Yellow Book of Lecan, the twin bases of Bergin and Best's edition; the companion Aislinge Óenguso survives in the fifteenth-century British Library MS Egerton 1782.

Realm & Domain

Midir's domain is Underworld, Fairy King — two terms that map onto the two halves of his dossier.

Underworld

The Irish Otherworld enters this dossier from below: Brí Léith is a hollow hill, a síd, and the tale's great set-piece is its excavation — Eochaid's host digging up the mounds of Ireland until the war of spades reached Midir's own roof. The underworld here is not a place of punishment but of plenty and music, the wondrous land of Midir's song to Étaín, hidden under a real Longford hill.

Fairy King

The síd-lord is the medieval original of the later fairy king: the proud host of the mound, master of glamour and shape-changing, whose bargains bind mortal kings. The continuity from Midir's court to the fairy-faith of the Irish countryside is the tradition's own.

The Wager as Judgment

The handbooks' cautious gloss of the name — judge — meets the tale at the fidchell board: the game at Tara is conducted like a court, the stakes schooling the king's greed until the final stake is named and the verdict lands.

Across Cultures

Midir has no cross-border witness of his own, and the honest comparison is typological rather than genetic. No Gaulish altar names him and no scholar has proposed one; where the Dagda can be set beside Sucellos on the strength of club and cauldron, Midir's dossier — the wager, the hunted bride, the mound that is a court — belongs to the Otherworld-king figure-type that Mac Cana and Sjoestedt trace through the Celtic materials without anchoring it to any single epigraphic god. Within Ireland his structural kin are the síd-lords of the neighboring tales: Bodb Derg of the Munster síde, and Manannán, the sea-going master of the Otherworld's glamour. The Welsh corpus offers only a functional parallel — Arawn, the lord of Annwn who likewise binds a mortal king by a bargain with a hidden edge — and the parallel is a pattern, not a pedigree.

Cultural Legacy

Midir's afterlife is the afterlife of Tochmarc Étaíne itself, the tale that never let him go. P. W. Joyce carried 'The Wooing of Etain' into modern English in Old Celtic Romances (1879), and Lady Gregory made the fidchell game and the swans part of the Celtic revival's furniture in Gods and Fighting Men (1904). James Stephens gave the story its strangest modern retelling in In the Land of Youth (1924), where Midir's arrival at Tara is staged as high comedy turning to awe. Bergin and Best's edition in Ériu (1938) fixed the text for scholarship, and Jeffrey Gantz's Penguin translation (1981) put the whole dossier on the general reader's shelf. The name itself took no second path — no Angus, no Brigit: Midir never became a given name, a place, or a pub, and remains what the manuscripts made him, a proper noun with a single bearer.

A Meditation

Midir is the god of the long game, and the tradition tells you so in his name before it tells you in his story: the handbooks gloss it, cautiously, as judge — and a judge is exactly what a wager needs. Watch how he plays Eochaid. He loses, and loses, and pays without stint, because the losses are the lesson: the king is being taught that the man across the board can afford anything, that the stakes are a language, and that the only sentence being written is the last one. When the final stake is named — an embrace, a kiss — the game has already been won for a month. A mortal wooing is measured in seasons; Midir's spans a thousand and twelve years, two births of the same woman, and a war of spades against his own roof — and he never hurries, because time is the one currency his kind does not spend. The hollow hills do not take what they want. They wait until the wanting itself concedes.

The Unicode Restoration

Midir is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Irish Midir carries none of these. The manuscripts write five plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them; the fada of modern editorial Mídir belongs to editorial convention, not to the manuscript norm the project follows. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the god; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines inherit whatever spelling the web normalizes — and for a plain-ASCII name, normalization is not the risk; confusion is. Midir the god, Mídir the editorial form, Mider the variant spelling, midir.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps them sorted.
