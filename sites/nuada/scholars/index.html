Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Nuada (nuada) ​— War, ‌King of ‌Tuatha · ​Cloud-maker, hunter ​— belongs ​to the ​Celtic tradition, ​where it ‌is catalogued ​under the ​domain "War, ‌King of ​Tuatha". The ‌name means ​"Cloud-maker, hunter"[1].

‌Nuada Airgetlám ​is the ​first king of ​the Tuatha ​Dé Danann ​in Ireland ‌— the ​ruler who ‌lost his ​arm at ‌the First ​Battle of ‌Mag Tuired, ​lost his ‌throne to ​the law ​that a ‌blemished man ‌cannot be ‌king, and won ​both back ​through the ‌physicians' craft: ​first the ​silver arm ‌that named ​him, then ​the living ‌arm that ​restored him. ‌His sword ​from Findias ​was one ​of the ​Four Treasures ‌of his ‌people, and ​he died at ‌the Second ​Battle of ​Mag Tuired ‌at the ‌hands of ‌Balor of ‌the Evil ​Eye.[2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the ​name as ​Nuada and ​serves its ‌temple at ​its temple. ​The restoration ​aSCII-equivalent form ‌— a description ​computed from ​the displayed ​form itself. ‌The plain ​ASCII form ‌nuada survives ​as a ‌modern convenience ​imposed by ‌the early ‌domain-name system; ​the restoration, ​not the ​fallback, is ​the form ‌the project ‌defends as ​philologically complete[3].

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

No ​indigenous ‌written attestation ‌survives ​for ​this ​name; ​Nuada is ​a ‌scholarly ​transliteration ​of the ‌reconstructed ​spoken ‌form. ​Etymologically the ‌name ​means ​"Cloud-maker, ​hunter"[1].

​The ASCII ​form ‌nuada ​survives ‌only because ​the ‌early ​domain-name ‌system ​could not ‌carry ​diacritics; ​it ‌is a ‌technological ‌compromise, ​not ​an ancient ‌spelling. ​The ​Unicode ‌restoration ​Nuada recovers ​the ‌full ​diacritic ‌detail of ​the ​scholarly ​transliteration ​directly ‌in the ‌address ​bar. ‌The ​restoration aSCII-equivalent ​form ‌— ‌a ‌description computed ‌from ​the ‌displayed ​form ​itself.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of the ‌name is /ˈnuː.að.u/ ‌— Old Irish ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • ​N- — Broad ​alveolar nasal [n̪ˠ], ​the plain 'n' ​of Old Irish ‌initial position, unlenited.
  • ​-uːa- — Long ​close back [uː] ‌gliding into open ​[a]; the two vowels ‌of Nuadu fall ​across a syllable ‌boundary.
  • -ð- — ​Lenited d, the ​voiced dental fricative ​[ð] — the ​soft 'th' of ​'this', which medieval ‌Irish scribes wrote simply ​as d.
  • -u ‌— Short back ​[u], the final ‌vowel of the ​Old Irish nominative ‌form Nuadu.

For ​the modern speaker, ‌the closest approximation ​is: 'NOO-ah-oo' — stress ​the first syllable, ‌hold the 'oo' ‌long, soften the ‌middle d toward ​the 'th' of ​'breathe', and finish ‌on a short ​'oo'. Modern English ​speakers usually say 'NOO-uh-duh'.

‌Kindred and historical ​forms of the ​name:

  • Old Irish ‌— Nuadu (later ​Nuada), byname Airgetlám, ‌'Silver-hand'
  • Middle Welsh ​— Nudd, and ​Lludd Llaw Eraint, 'Lludd ​of the Silver ​Hand', king of ‌Britain in Lludd ‌a Llefelys
  • Gaulish ​— Nodens (also ‌Nodons, Nudens), the ​god of the ​temple at Lydney ‌Park in Gloucestershire
  • Modern ‌Irish — Nuadha, ‌the living spelling ‌of the name

​Nuada is a ‌plain restoration by ​the mechanical rule: ​the attested Old ​Irish form carries ‌no diacritic and no ​distinctive letter, so ​plain ASCII loses ​nothing. The etymology ‌is honestly uncertain: ​one proposal reads ​the name as ​'cloud-maker', from a ‌root behind Welsh ​nudd 'mist'; another, argued ‌by Tolkien for ​the Gaulish Nodens, ‌connects it to ​a root meaning ‌'to catch, acquire' ‌— a fisher ​or hunter — ​which would suit ​the sea-and-hounds imagery of ​the Lydney temple. ‌Neither is settled, ‌and the temple ​says so.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

No ​indigenous writing ‌system is ‌securely ​attested for ​individual ​celtic names. ​The form ​shown ‌is a ​modern ​scholarly transliteration.[1]

‌The form ​Nuada ‌is therefore ​a ‌scholarly transliteration ​rather than ​an ​attested ancient ​spelling; ​it encodes ‌the reconstructed ​sound ‌of the ​name ‌for modern ​use, and ‌no ​mark in ‌it ​is decorative.

​The attested ‌Old ‌Irish form ‌is ​Nuadu (later ​Nuada), written ‌by ​medieval Irish ​scribes ‌in the ​Insular Latin ​bookhand ‌of the ​great ‌vellum codices; ​in the ​Ogham ​letter-row the ​name ‌would have ‌been notched ​ᚅᚒᚐᚇᚐ ‌(N-U-A-D-A), though ​no ​Ogham stone ‌yet found ‌carries ‌it. Because ‌the ​name is ‌already plain ​ASCII, ​its domain ​was ‌registered decades ​ago in ​the ​ordinary way: ‌nuada.com ​is held ​by a ​third ‌party outside ​the ‌PuniCodex collection ​and is ‌watched ​for release ‌— ‌no form ​of the ​name ​is currently ​owned, ‌and this ‌temple claims ​none.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Nuada's story is the ​Irish tradition's great parable of kingship and ‌the body: a ruler can be unmade by ‌a single wound and remade by healing, ​and the law watches both. The record ​runs from the Lebor Gabála Érenn through the ​two battles of Mag Tuired, and his ​Welsh and Gaulish cognates carry the same ​silver-handed name across the Celtic world.[1]

The First ‌Battle of Mag Tuired (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

​When the Tuatha Dé Danann came to ​Ireland, Nuada had been their king for seven ‌years. They demanded the country from the ​Fir Bolg, and the two peoples met ‌at Mag Tuired in Connacht. In the ​battle Nuada fought Sreng, champion of the Fir ‌Bolg, and Sreng struck off his right ​arm at the shoulder — yet the ​Tuatha Dé Danann won the field, and the ​Fir Bolg king Eochaid mac Eirc was ​driven to his death. The victors then ​offered the defeated a quarter of Ireland, and ‌the Fir Bolg chose Connacht, which their ​descendants held. The victory that made the ‌Tuatha Dé Danann masters of Ireland thus cost ​their king the wholeness the kingship required: ‌one champion's sword-stroke bought a kingdom and ​unmade its king in the same afternoon.[2]

‌The Silver Hand and the Hand of Flesh ​(Lebor Gabála Érenn)

Because a blemished man ‌could not hold the kingship, Nuada yielded ​the throne to Bres. The physician Dian Cécht, ​with Credne the brazier, made him a ‌full arm of silver that moved in ‌every joint — whence his byname Airgetlám, Silver-hand. ‌But Dian Cécht's own son Miach went ​further: he set the severed arm to ​the stump and healed it 'joint to joint ‌and vein to vein' in thrice nine ​days. Jealous that the son had outdone ​the father, Dian Cécht struck Miach dead; ‌from the young healer's grave grew three hundred ​and sixty-five herbs, which his sister Airmed ​sorted by their virtues — and which ‌Dian Cécht scattered, so that their full knowledge ​died with him. Healing, in this cycle, ‌is never free of the wound of ​envy.

Bres, the Satire, and the Restoration (Lebor ​Gabála · Cath Maige Tuired)

Bres mac ​Elatha, half Fomorian by blood, ruled badly: ​the warriors of Ireland were set to ‌menial labour, hospitality failed, and the poet Cairbre ‌mac Étaíne composed against him what the ​tradition calls the first satire ever made ‌in Ireland — and Bres's face broke out ​in blotches. Shamed and deposed, Bres fled ​to his Fomorian kin and came back ‌at the head of the host that forced ‌the Second Battle. The same law that ‌had unseated Nuada now unseated Bres, for ‌a blemished king could not stand; and with ​his arm made whole by Miach, Nuada ‌took back the kingship and held it ​until the war came. The symmetry is ​exact: one king falls by the body's wound, ​the other by the wound of the ‌word, and the same unforgiving law of ​wholeness judges them both.

The Second Battle and ​the King's Death (Cath Maige Tuired)

At ​Tara, on the eve of the war, ‌a stranger came to Nuada's hall claiming every ​skill at once — harpist, warrior, smith, ​poet, healer — and Nuada, testing Lugh ​and finding him unmatched, gave the young god ‌his own seat, the champion's seat, to ​lead the counsel of war. The Second ‌Battle of Mag Tuired was fought against ​the Fomorians, and there Nuada Airgetlám and Macha ‌his wife fell together at the hands ​of Balor of the Evil Eye. Balor's ‌own death followed within the hour, Lugh's sling-stone ‌through the destroying eye, and with the ​two kings' deaths the war ended and ​the tribute was lifted forever. The first king ​of the Tuatha Dé Danann in Ireland ​died in the victory that secured his ‌people's rule; the sword of Findias passed into ‌memory as the measure of a weapon ​that cannot be withstood.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Nuada ​belongs to ‌one of ‌the clearest ​pan-Celtic name ​families. In ​Welsh he ​is ​Nudd and, ‌in full ​form, Lludd ​Llaw Eraint ‌— 'Lludd ​of the ‌Silver Hand' ​— ‌the king ​of Britain ​in Lludd ​a Llefelys, ​whose byname ​renders Airgetlám ‌word ​for word ‌(llaw 'hand', ​eraint 'silver'). ‌In Gaulish ​he is ‌Nodens (also ​Nodons, ‌Nudens), worshipped ​at the ​great temple ‌at Lydney ‌Park in ‌Gloucestershire, where ​Roman dedicants ​equated ‌him with ​Mars and ​where the ‌imagery — ​hounds, fishermen, ​the sea ‌— ​suggests a ‌healer and ​hunter as ​much as ​a warrior. ​The Romano-British ‌curse ‌tablet of ​Silvianus, asking ‌'Lord Nodens' ​to deny ​health to ‌the thief ‌of his ‌ring, ‌shows the ​god working ‌as an ​enforcer of ​justice. Scholars ​(Mac Cana, ‌Koch) ​reconstruct a ​Common Celtic ​Noudont- or ‌Noudo- behind ​all three, ​though the ​root's ‌meaning — ​'cloud-maker' or ‌'catcher, fisher' ​— remains ‌genuinely debated.[1]

​Within the ‌Celtic tradition, ‌closely ​related names ​in the ​corpus include ​Aengus, Annwn, ‌Arawn, Badb, ‌Balor, and ​Bedwyr.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Nuada's ​afterlife runs ‌through ‌scholarship into ​fantasy. When ​R. ​E. M. ​and ​T. V. ‌Wheeler published ​their ​excavation of ‌the ​Nodens temple ‌at Lydney ​Park ‌in 1932, ​the ​young philologist ​J. R. ​R. ​Tolkien contributed ‌the ​appendix 'The ‌Name ​Nodens', tracing ‌the god's ​name ‌to a ​root ‌of catching ​and acquiring ​— ‌a fragment ‌of ‌Tolkien's scholarly ​life that ​fans ‌of his ​fiction ​still chase. ‌In modern ​Irish ​the name ‌survives ​as Nuadha, ‌and ​Maynooth University's ​old arms ​carried ​Nuada's sword. ‌In ‌popular culture ​the silver-handed ‌king ​rides again ​as ‌Prince Nuada ‌Silverlance in ‌Hellboy ‌II: The ​Golden ‌Army (2008), ​and the ​motif ​of the ‌magical ​prosthetic hand ​— ​from A ‌Song of ​Ice ​and Fire ​to ‌Japanese fantasy ​— descends, ‌knowingly ​or not, ‌from ​Airgetlám. Modern ‌Celtic pagans ‌honour ​Nuada as ​a ​god of ​rightful rule, ‌healing, ‌and sovereignty ​restored.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Nuada is ​textual in ‌Ireland — ‌no inscription ​names him ​there — ​but his ​Gaulish cognate ​has one ‌of the ​best-excavated cult ​sites in Roman ‌Britain. At ​Lydney Park, ‌Gloucestershire, overlooking ​the Severn ‌estuary, a ​temple complex ​of the ​late third ​and fourth ​centuries CE ‌was dedicated to ​Nodens: the ‌Wheelers' excavations ​(1928–29, published ‌1932 with ​Tolkien's philological ‌appendix) uncovered ​mosaic floors, ‌inscribed slabs ​reading DEVO ​NODENTI and ‌D M NODONTI ‌('to the ‌god Mars ​Nodens'), bronze ​dog figurines, ‌a fisherman ​plaque, and ​pins and ‌bracelets left ​as votives ​— the ‌material of a ​healing-and-hunting cult. ‌The famous ​curse tablet ​of Silvianus, ​asking Nodens ​to withhold ‌health from ‌whoever stole ​his ring ‌until it ​was returned 'to ​the temple ‌of Nodens', ‌was found ‌on the ‌site; a ​gold ring ‌inscribed SENICIANE ​VIVAS IN ​DEO, plausibly ​the very ‌ring, surfaced at ​Silchester. On ​the Irish ​side, the ‌texts are ​the evidence: ​the Second ​Battle of ‌Mag Tuired ​survives in ‌the sixteenth-century ​British Library Harley ‌MS 5280, ​the First ‌Battle in ‌a single ​late vellum ​copy edited ​by Fraser, ​and the ‌Lebor Gabála ‌in its ​medieval recensions.[1]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Nuada ​is the ‌king of ‌the necessary ​surrender. The ​Irish law ​of the ​unblemished king ​looks cruel ‌— a ​man maimed ​in his ‌people's victory ​loses his ‌throne — ​but the ‌myth refuses ​both easy ​readings. ​It does ​not mock ​the law: ‌Bres, who ​takes the ‌throne, is ​judged by ‌the same ​standard and ‌falls to ​a blemish ‌of the ​face raised ​by satire. ‌And it ‌does not ‌abandon ​the wounded: ​the whole ‌craft of ​the physician-gods ​bends itself ‌to making ​Nuada whole, ​first in ‌silver, then ​in flesh. ‌What the ​cycle teaches ​is that ​sovereignty is ​not possession ‌but fitness, ‌and fitness ​can ‌be lost ​and restored. ​The silver ‌hand is ‌the emblem ‌of every ‌function that ​has to ‌be rebuilt ​after catastrophe: ​it moves ​in every ‌joint, it ​does the ​work, and ​it is ‌not ​the thing ​it replaces ​— until ‌Miach's deeper ​healing shows ‌that even ​that distinction ‌can be ​overcome. The ‌king who ‌rules again ​is not ​the king ​who fell. ​He is ‌the king ‌who was ​remade.[1]

Sources
