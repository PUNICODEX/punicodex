Overview

Nuada (nuada) — War, King of Tuatha · ​Cloud-maker, hunter — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "War, King of Tuatha". The name means "Cloud-maker, hunter".

Nuada Airgetlám is the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann ​in Ireland — the ruler who lost his arm at the First Battle of Mag Tuired, lost his throne to the law that a blemished man cannot be king, and won both back through ​the physicians' craft: first the silver arm that named him, then the living arm that restored him. His sword from Findias was one of the Four Treasures of his people, and he died at the ​Second Battle of Mag Tuired at the hands of Balor of the Evil Eye.

The Original Script

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly ​transliteration.

The form Nuada is therefore a scholarly transliteration rather than an attested ancient spelling; it encodes the reconstructed sound of the name for modern use, and no mark in it is decorative.

The attested ‌Old Irish form is Nuadu (later Nuada), written by medieval Irish scribes in the Insular Latin bookhand of the great vellum codices; in the Ogham letter-row the name would have been notched ᚅᚒᚐᚇᚐ (N-U-A-D-A), though ​no Ogham stone yet found carries it. Because the name is already plain ASCII, its domain was registered decades ago in the ordinary way: nuada.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex ‌collection and is watched for release — no form of the name is currently owned, and this temple claims none.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈnuː.að.u/ — Old Irish Reconstruction.

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • N- — Broad alveolar nasal [n̪ˠ], the plain 'n' of Old Irish initial position, unlenited.
  • -uːa- — Long close back [uː] gliding into open [a]; the two vowels of Nuadu fall across a syllable ​boundary.
  • -ð- — Lenited d, the voiced dental fricative [ð] — the soft 'th' of 'this', which medieval Irish scribes wrote simply as d.
  • -u — Short back [u], the final vowel of the Old ​Irish nominative form Nuadu.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'NOO-ah-oo' — stress the first syllable, hold the 'oo' long, soften the middle d toward the 'th' of 'breathe', and finish on ​a short 'oo'. Modern English speakers usually say 'NOO-uh-duh'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Irish — Nuadu (later Nuada), byname Airgetlám, 'Silver-hand'
  • Middle Welsh — Nudd, and Lludd Llaw Eraint, 'Lludd of ​the Silver Hand', king of Britain in Lludd a Llefelys
  • Gaulish — Nodens (also Nodons, Nudens), the god of the temple at Lydney Park in Gloucestershire
  • Modern Irish — Nuadha, the living spelling of the ‌name

Nuada is a plain restoration by the mechanical rule: the attested Old Irish form carries no diacritic and no distinctive letter, so plain ASCII loses nothing. The etymology is honestly uncertain: one proposal ​reads the name as 'cloud-maker', from a root behind Welsh nudd 'mist'; another, argued by Tolkien for the Gaulish Nodens, connects it to a root meaning 'to catch, acquire' — a fisher or hunter — ‌which would suit the sea-and-hounds imagery of the Lydney temple. Neither is settled, and the temple says so.

Mythology

Nuada's story is the Irish tradition's great parable of kingship and the body: a ruler can ​be unmade by a single wound and remade by healing, and the law watches both. The record runs from the Lebor Gabála Érenn through the two battles of Mag Tuired, and his Welsh and ‌Gaulish cognates carry the same silver-handed name across the Celtic world.

The First Battle of Mag Tuired (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

When the Tuatha Dé Danann came to Ireland, Nuada had been their king for seven ​years. They demanded the country from the Fir Bolg, and the two peoples met at Mag Tuired in Connacht. In the battle Nuada fought Sreng, champion of the Fir Bolg, and Sreng struck off his ‌right arm at the shoulder — yet the Tuatha Dé Danann won the field, and the Fir Bolg king Eochaid mac Eirc was driven to his death. The victors then offered the defeated a quarter ​of Ireland, and the Fir Bolg chose Connacht, which their descendants held. The victory that made the Tuatha Dé Danann masters of Ireland thus cost their king the wholeness the kingship required: one champion's ‌sword-stroke bought a kingdom and unmade its king in the same afternoon.

The Silver Hand and the Hand of Flesh (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

Because a blemished man could not hold the kingship, Nuada yielded the ​throne to Bres. The physician Dian Cécht, with Credne the brazier, made him a full arm of silver that moved in every joint — whence his byname Airgetlám, Silver-hand. But Dian Cécht's own son Miach ​went further: he set the severed arm to the stump and healed it 'joint to joint and vein to vein' in thrice nine days. Jealous that the son had outdone the father, Dian Cécht ‌struck Miach dead; from the young healer's grave grew three hundred and sixty-five herbs, which his sister Airmed sorted by their virtues — and which Dian Cécht scattered, so that their full knowledge died with ‌him. Healing, in this cycle, is never free of the wound of envy.

Bres, the Satire, and the Restoration (Lebor Gabála · Cath Maige Tuired)

Bres mac Elatha, half Fomorian by blood, ruled badly: the ‌warriors of Ireland were set to menial labour, hospitality failed, and the poet Cairbre mac Étaíne composed against him what the tradition calls the first satire ever made in Ireland — and Bres's face broke ​out in blotches. Shamed and deposed, Bres fled to his Fomorian kin and came back at the head of the host that forced the Second Battle. The same law that had unseated Nuada now ​unseated Bres, for a blemished king could not stand; and with his arm made whole by Miach, Nuada took back the kingship and held it until the war came. The symmetry is exact: one king ‌falls by the body's wound, the other by the wound of the word, and the same unforgiving law of wholeness judges them both.

The Second Battle and the King's Death (Cath Maige Tuired)

At Tara, ​on the eve of the war, a stranger came to Nuada's hall claiming every skill at once — harpist, warrior, smith, poet, healer — and Nuada, testing Lugh and finding him unmatched, gave the ​young god his own seat, the champion's seat, to lead the counsel of war. The Second Battle of Mag Tuired was fought against the Fomorians, and there Nuada Airgetlám and Macha his wife fell together ‌at the hands of Balor of the Evil Eye. Balor's own death followed within the hour, Lugh's sling-stone through the destroying eye, and with the two kings' deaths the war ended and the tribute was ​lifted forever. The first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann in Ireland died in the victory that secured his people's rule; the sword of Findias passed into memory as the measure of a weapon ​that cannot be withstood.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Nuada concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The silver arm — Wholeness made by craft ‌when nature failed — the prosthesis that kept a king king in all but law
  • The sword of Nuada — The irresistible weapon from the city Findias, one of the Four Treasures brought to Ireland
  • ​Tara (Temair) — The seat of the kingship of Ireland, which a blemished man could not lawfully hold
  • The physician's craft — Dian Cécht's silver-work and Miach's flesh-work — healing as the instrument of sovereignty
  • ‌Dog and fisherman (as Nodens) — The hound figurines and sea imagery of the Lydney Park temple, where his Gaulish cognate was worshipped

Archaeology & Evidence

Nuada is textual in Ireland — no inscription ​names him there — but his Gaulish cognate has one of the best-excavated cult sites in Roman Britain. At Lydney Park, Gloucestershire, overlooking the Severn estuary, a temple complex of the late third and fourth ​centuries CE was dedicated to Nodens: the Wheelers' excavations (1928–29, published 1932 with Tolkien's philological appendix) uncovered mosaic floors, inscribed slabs reading DEVO NODENTI and D M NODONTI ('to the god Mars Nodens'), bronze dog ​figurines, a fisherman plaque, and pins and bracelets left as votives — the material of a healing-and-hunting cult. The famous curse tablet of Silvianus, asking Nodens to withhold health from whoever stole his ring ​until it was returned 'to the temple of Nodens', was found on the site; a gold ring inscribed SENICIANE VIVAS IN DEO, plausibly the very ring, surfaced at Silchester. On the Irish side, the texts ‌are the evidence: the Second Battle of Mag Tuired survives in the sixteenth-century British Library Harley MS 5280, the First Battle in a single late vellum copy edited by Fraser, and the Lebor Gabála in ‌its medieval recensions.

Across Cultures

Nuada belongs to one of the clearest pan-Celtic ‌name families. In Welsh he is Nudd and, in full form, Lludd Llaw Eraint — 'Lludd of the Silver Hand' — the king of Britain in Lludd a Llefelys, whose byname renders Airgetlám word for ‌word (llaw 'hand', eraint 'silver'). In Gaulish he is Nodens (also Nodons, Nudens), worshipped at the great temple at Lydney Park in Gloucestershire, where Roman dedicants equated him with Mars and where the imagery ‌— hounds, fishermen, the sea — suggests a healer and hunter as much as a warrior. The Romano-British curse tablet of Silvianus, asking 'Lord Nodens' to deny health to the thief of his ring, shows ​the god working as an enforcer of justice. Scholars (Mac Cana, Koch) reconstruct a Common Celtic Noudont- or Noudo- behind all three, though the root's meaning — 'cloud-maker' or 'catcher, fisher' — remains genuinely debated.

‌Within the Celtic tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aengus, Annwn, Arawn, Badb, Balor, and Bedwyr.

Cultural Legacy

Nuada's afterlife runs through scholarship into fantasy. When R. E. M. and T. V. Wheeler ​published their excavation of the Nodens temple at Lydney Park in 1932, the young philologist J. R. R. Tolkien contributed the appendix 'The Name Nodens', tracing the god's name to a root of catching ​and acquiring — a fragment of Tolkien's scholarly life that fans of his fiction still chase. In modern Irish the name survives as Nuadha, and Maynooth University's old arms carried Nuada's sword. In popular culture ​the silver-handed king rides again as Prince Nuada Silverlance in Hellboy II: The Golden Army (2008), and the motif of the magical prosthetic hand — from A Song of Ice and Fire to Japanese fantasy ‌— descends, knowingly or not, from Airgetlám. Modern Celtic pagans honour Nuada as a god of rightful rule, healing, and sovereignty restored.

A Meditation

Nuada is the king of the necessary surrender. The Irish law of the unblemished king looks cruel — a man maimed in ‌his people's victory loses his throne — but the myth refuses both easy readings. It does not mock the law: Bres, who takes the throne, is judged by the same standard and falls to a ​blemish of the face raised by satire. And it does not abandon the wounded: the whole craft of the physician-gods bends itself to making Nuada whole, first in silver, then in flesh. What the cycle ‌teaches is that sovereignty is not possession but fitness, and fitness can be lost and restored. The silver hand is the emblem of every function that has to be rebuilt after catastrophe: it moves ​in every joint, it does the work, and it is not the thing it replaces — until Miach's deeper healing shows that even that distinction can be overcome. The king who rules again is not ‌the king who fell. He is the king who was remade.
