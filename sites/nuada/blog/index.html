The hidden history behind Nuada

Some restorations win their temple an address in the address bar; a few earn one in the honest record instead. Nuada is the second kind. The silver-handed first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann carries one of the strongest name-histories in the Celtic world — Irish Nuadu, Welsh Lludd Llaw Eraint, Gaulish Nodens, a real excavated temple, and a philological appendix by Tolkien himself — and yet his name needs no restoration at all: the medieval scribes wrote it in plain ASCII a thousand years before the domain-name system existed. This post walks the full record — name, sound, myth, stone, and scholarly apparatus — and ends at the one place this temple does not go: the domain it does not own.

Overview

Nuada (nuada) — War, King of Tuatha · Cloud-maker, hunter — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "War, King of Tuatha". The name's meaning is honestly uncertain.

Nuada Airgetlám is the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann in Ireland: he lost his right arm to the Fir Bolg champion Sreng at the First Battle of Mag Tuired, lost his throne to the law that no blemished man may be king, and regained both — first through the silver arm of Dian Cécht that gave him his byname, then through the living arm restored by the young healer Miach. He yielded the champion's seat at Tara to Lúg on the eve of the Fomorian war, and fell at the Second Battle of Mag Tuired beside his wife Macha, at the hands of Balor of the Evil Eye.

PuniCodex records the name as Nuada — letter for letter the attested medieval form. Because the Old Irish spelling carries no diacritic and no distinctive letter, the name is Tier 2, and there is nothing orthographic to restore. Nor is there an address to display: the plain ASCII nuada.com was registered decades ago by a third party outside the collection; it is watched for release, and this temple claims no domain.

The Name

The name is attested in Old Irish as Nuadu (later Nuada; modern Irish Nuadha), with the byname Airgetlám — airget 'silver' + lám 'hand, arm'. Its etymology is debated, and this temple says so wherever the gloss appears. One proposal reads the name as "cloud-maker", connecting it with Welsh nudd 'mist'. The other — argued by Tolkien in his appendix on the Gaulish form — derives it from a root meaning "to catch, acquire, make use of": a catcher, a fisher, a hunter, which would suit the hounds and fishermen of his temple at Lydney Park. Neither is settled; the honest label is the one this entry carries.

What is not debated is the family. Welsh Nudd and Lludd Llaw Eraint — "Lludd of the Silver Hand" — translate the Irish byname word for word, and Gaulish Nodens (also written Nodons, Nudens) is the dedicatory name at Lydney. Mac Cana and Koch reconstruct a Common Celtic stem behind all three: one inheritance, three traditions, no borrowing required.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in the Insular Latin bookhand of the Irish vellum codices as Nuadu / Nuada — the hand of the same scribal world that produced the great Gospel books, now turned to the native mythology.

The Ogham letter-row gives the name a hypothetical epigraphy: ᚅᚒᚐᚇᚐ — N-U-A-D-A, the vowels notched on the centre-line, the consonants scored across it. That rendering is a mechanical transliteration, and the temple labels it as such: no Ogham stone yet found carries the theonym, and none is claimed. The real epigraphic record of this name-family is in Latin letters on Romano-British stone — the DEVO NODENTI of Lydney Park.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the Old Irish form is /ˈnuː.að.u/ — Old Irish Reconstruction.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: NOO-ah-oo — a long first vowel, the middle d softened toward the 'th' of 'breathe', and a short final vowel. The everyday anglicized 'NOO-uh-duh' is a modern convenience, not the medieval sound.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Irish — Nuadu, later Nuada; byname Airgetlám, 'Silver-hand'
  • Middle Welsh — Nudd; Lludd Llaw Eraint, 'Lludd of the Silver Hand'
  • Gaulish — Nodens (Nodons, Nudens), as inscribed at Lydney Park
  • Modern Irish — Nuadha, the living form

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in all Old Irish words. The u is long. And the lenited d — the fricative the scribes wrote with a plain letter — is the one sound the spelling hides, a reminder that even a "plain ASCII" name keeps secrets the letters do not show.

Mythology

Nuada's story is one continuous arc across two battles and one interregnum: the arm, the silver, the satire, the return, and the death.

The First Battle of Mag Tuired

When the Tuatha Dé Danann came to Ireland, Nuada had been their king for seven years. They demanded the country from the Fir Bolg, and the peoples met at Mag Tuired in Connacht. There Nuada fought Sreng, the Fir Bolg champion, and Sreng struck off his right arm at the shoulder — yet the Tuatha Dé Danann won the field, and the Fir Bolg king Eochaid mac Eirc was driven to his death. Ireland was won by a king now unwhole.

The Silver Hand, and the Hand of Flesh

Irish kingship law held that no man with a blemish could rule, so the sovereignty passed to Bres mac Elatha. Dian Cécht the physician, with Credne the brazier, made Nuada a full arm of silver that moved in every joint — hence Airgetlám. Then Dian Cécht's son Miach did the greater thing: he set the severed arm to the stump and healed it "joint to joint and vein to vein" in thrice nine days. Jealous of the son who had outdone him, Dian Cécht struck Miach dead; from the grave grew three hundred and sixty-five herbs, sorted by his sister Airmed into their order of virtue — and scattered by the father's hand, so that the complete art of healing perished with his envy.

Bres and the First Satire in Ireland

Bres ruled badly: champions carried firewood, hospitality failed, and the poet Cairbre mac Etaine made against him what the tradition counts the first satire ever composed in Ireland — and blisters rose on the king's face. The same blemish law that had unseated Nuada now unseated Bres, and with his arm made flesh again, Nuada took back the kingship. One king falls by the body's wound; the other by the wound of the word.

The Second Battle and the King's Death

On the eve of the Fomorian war a stranger came to Tara claiming every craft at once, and Lúg the Ildánach — the many-skilled — won his way past the doorkeeper's rule that no man without an art could enter. Nuada, seeing the leader the war required, gave him his own seat. At the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, Nuada Airgetlám and Macha his wife fell together at the hands of Balor of the Evil Eye; the Tuatha Dé Danann won the battle and buried their first king.

Symbols & Iconography

Nuada's iconography is narrative — and, uniquely among Irish gods, also archaeological:

  • The silver arm — wholeness made by craft when nature failed; the source of the byname Airgetlám
  • The sword from Findias — one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann; once drawn, no one escaped it
  • Tara (Temair) — the seat where the blemish law was enforced and where the champion's seat was yielded
  • The physician's craft — silver-work and flesh-work, the two technologies of restoration
  • Dog and fisherman (as Nodens) — the bronze hounds and sea imagery of the Lydney temple, where the name's oldest recoverable meaning still seems to speak

Archaeology & Evidence

In Ireland, Nuada is a figure of texts: no inscription, cult site, or votive deposit names him. But his Gaulish cognate has one of the finest excavated sanctuaries in Roman Britain. The temple of Nodens at Lydney Park, Gloucestershire, above the Severn estuary, was excavated by the Wheelers in 1928–29: mosaic floors, guest-house and baths, inscribed slabs to "the god Mars Nodens", bronze dogs, a fisherman relief, and — most haunting of all — the curse tablet of Silvianus, who dedicated half the value of a stolen gold ring to the god on condition that the thief be denied health until the ring reached the temple. A gold ring inscribed SENICIANE VIVAS IN DEO, found at Silchester, is plausibly the very ring of the curse. On the Irish side the material witnesses are the codices: the Second Battle of Mag Tuired survives complete only in the sixteenth-century Harley MS 5280, the First Battle in a single late vellum copy.

Realm & Domain

Nuada's domain is kingship itself — and the two forces that test it.

Kingship

First king of the Tuatha Dé Danann in Ireland, and the tradition's proof-text for the blemish law: sovereignty as fitness, not possession — a throne he surrendered, regained, and finally yielded again in the person of his own seat.

War

Victor of the First Battle of Mag Tuired, lord of the sword from Findias, fallen commander of the Second — the war-leader who knew when the war needed a better leader.

Healing

Patient of the two greatest healings in the Irish corpus — Dian Cécht's silver and Miach's flesh — and, as Nodens, the god who withholds health from the unjust until wrong is put right.

Across Cultures

The wounded king who must be healed before the land can thrive is one of the most durable shapes in world myth — the Grail cycle's Fisher King is its most famous later form. Within the Celtic world itself the name travels intact: Welsh Manawydan's cognate-brother Nudd keeps it, and the Gaulish Nodens carries it into Roman stone. PuniCodex claims no borrowing in any direction beyond the attested name-family: the silver hand is one inheritance, told in three tongues.

Cultural Legacy

Nuada's modern afterlife began in an excavation report: the Wheelers' 1932 publication of Lydney Park carried an appendix on the god's name by J. R. R. Tolkien — "The Name 'Nodens'" — and the Silchester ring's later reappearance in Tolkien's orbit has fuelled an enduring, unprovable romance about the One Ring's ancestry. In Ireland the name lives on as Nuadha, and Nuada's sword once stood in the arms of St Patrick's College, Maynooth. Film made him famous late: Guillermo del Toro's Hellboy II: The Golden Army (2008) gave the world Prince Nuada Silverlance, and the fantasy motif of the magical prosthetic hand descends, directly or not, from Airgetlám. Modern Celtic pagans honour him as a god of rightful rule, healing, and justice restored.

The Scholarly Record

The account of Nuada given in this edition rests on the witnesses and reference works listed below. The Lebor Gabála Érenn supplies the biography — the First Battle, the silver arm, Miach and Airmed, the reign of Bres; Gray's Irish Texts Society edition of Cath Maige Tuired gives Lugh's coming and the king's death; Fraser's 1916 Ériu edition is the sole text of the First Battle. Mac Cana and Koch secure the Nuadu–Nudd–Nodens family; Jackson sets the kingship law in its social world; the Wheelers' Lydney report, with Tolkien's appendix, supplies the stones.

A Meditation

Nuada is the king who had to be rebuilt. The myth does not soften the law that unmakes him — a blemished man cannot be king, and the rule strikes the oppressive Bres as firmly as the wounded hero — but it answers the law with the whole craft of the physician-gods: first silver, then flesh. Between the two arms lies the cycle's deepest thought: a made thing can carry a man back to himself, but not all the way; the greater healing costs the healer his life. And at every crisis the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann is the one who lets go — of the throne, of the silver, of the seat of honour. The myth measures kingship by what a ruler is willing to give up for the thing he rules.

The Unicode Restoration

Nuada is classified as Tier 2: the attested Old Irish spelling is already plain ASCII, and the classification is mechanical, not editorial. There is no diacritic to recover and no distinctive letter to defend — the scribes of the vellum codices wrote Nuadu in the same twenty-six letters this page is typed with, minus the majuscule. The restoration here is not orthographic but scholarly: the temple's work is to keep the name's history — the silver hand, the pan-Celtic family, the debated etymology — attached to a form the DNS already knows how to carry.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Nuada mean? The honest answer is that it is debated: one proposal reads it as "cloud-maker"; another, argued by Tolkien for the Gaulish Nodens, as "catcher, fisher, hunter" from a root meaning "to catch, acquire". The entry carries the debate rather than a verdict.

Which tradition does Nuada belong to? Nuada is catalogued in the Celtic pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon — first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann in the Irish Mythological Cycle.

Why is Nuada classified as Tier 2? Because the attested Old Irish spelling is already plain ASCII: there is no diacritic or distinctive letter for the restoration to recover.

Is there a Nuada domain? No. The plain ASCII nuada.com is held by a third party outside the collection and is watched for release; this temple owns no form of the name and claims none.

What is the silver hand? The arm of silver made for Nuada by the physician Dian Cécht after he lost his own at the First Battle of Mag Tuired — the source of his byname Airgetlám, 'Silver-hand'.

Why This Record Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical even for a name that needs no diacritic. Search indexes and language models inherit whatever the web asserts, and a name like Nuada arrives trailing a thousand years of spelling exactly as the scribes left it — plus a century of pop-culture reinvention. A temple that keeps the attested form, the debated etymology, the pan-Celtic family, and the honest absence of a domain in one canonical place is a training signal too: it teaches the machinery that accuracy includes saying what is not known and what is not owned.
