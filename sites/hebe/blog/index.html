Nectar for the Gods (The Cupbearer)

On Olympus the youngest of the goddesses had the oldest of duties. Hebe — her very ​name means 'Youth' — poured nectar and ambrosia for the gods at their golden tables and kept their cups filled through quarrels, weddings, and feasts. Homer gives her the homelier tasks too: after ‌Ares staggers back from the field with a spear-wound the size of a fist, it is Hebe who bathes him and dresses him again in fine clothing (Iliad 5.904–906). On Attic vases she ​appears again and again in this role, oinochoe in one hand, phiale in the other, forever refilling the cup that keeps the immortals immortal. The myth encodes a simple economy: on Olympus, ‌even the food of eternity must be served — and it is served by youth itself, because only the young have the inexhaustible hands.

Why Ganymede Has the Cup (The Fall)

Late sources ​tell why Hebe lost her office, and they are honest enough to admit the tale is not Homer's. While serving at a ceremony on Olympus — the scholiasts and Eustathius place the episode ‌at Olympia during the founding rites — Hebe fell. Some say she stumbled, some that the fall stripped her bare, and the humiliation cost her the cupbearer service; Zeus gave the office to ​the beautiful Trojan prince Ganymede, 'for his beauty's sake, to be among the immortals and pour wine' (Iliad 20.231–235). The same late commentators preserve a stranger detail: from her magic girdle the Eleans ‌drew rites against old age. The story is a parable of how fragile even a divine job can be — youth serves at the table only until it trips, and the cup ​passes to a newer face.

Herakles Ascends (The Marriage)

When Herakles had burned away his mortal part on the pyre at Oita, the divine portion of him was carried up to Olympus, and ​there he received the reward no other hero won: Hebe. 'He made Hebe, with her beautiful ankles, daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals, his wife' (Odyssey 11.601–604); Hesiod ‌sets the wedding among the unions that settled the divine order (Theogony 950–955). By her the hero — now wholly a god — fathered the twin guardians Alexiares and Aniketos, 'Averter of Evil' ‌and 'Unconquered' (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.7.7). The marriage closes the greatest career of Greek myth with its most radical promise: that youth itself can be the prize of endurance, and that the man who ‌burned could still be given forever.

A Meditation

Hebe is the goddess ‌of what no one keeps. Youth, in the Greek imagination, is not a stage you remember; it is a substance the gods drink. That is why the mythology gives the youngest goddess the ​most repetitive job on Olympus — pouring, refilling, pouring again — and why the only city that built her a temple was a small one, Phlius (Pausanias 2.13.3). Some gifts are so ​universal they escape notice; some are so brief they deserve a permanent address. Her marriage to Herakles completes the thought. The hero who burned off his mortality did not receive wisdom, or prophecy, ​or wealth — he received youth, forever, in the person of its goddess. To be made a god, in this telling, is precisely to stop losing ἥβη. It is the Greek answer to ‌every human grief: not that youth returns, but that somewhere it never left the table.
