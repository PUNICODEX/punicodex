A ‌Son Between Sky and Earth (The Separation)

In the Māori creation told by Wiremu Maihi Te Rangikāheke and printed by George Grey, the Sky Father Ranginui and the Earth Mother Papatūānuku lay locked ​in an embrace, and their children — among them Rongo, Tāne, Tangaroa, Haumia-tiketike, Tāwhirimātea, and Tūmatauenga — lived crushed in darkness between them. Rongo's fiercest brother Tū urged the final solution: kill the ‌parents and have done. The others recoiled at the thought of slaying their own father and mother, and the gentler plan of Tāne prevailed — the parents would be forced apart, not ​slain. Rongo stood with Tāne's party. Tāne braced his head against the earth and his feet against the sky and thrust, and light came pouring in where darkness had been (Grey, Polynesian Mythology, ‌the legend of the children of Rangi and Papa). In this telling the gods of food are born of suffocation: Rongo's whole mythology begins in a space too tight to live in, and ​every field he blesses is a little of that darkness opened to the sun.

Hidden in the Bosom of the Earth (The Storm)

Tāwhirimātea, god of winds, had opposed the separation of his ‌parents, and when it was done he took war to his brothers. Tangaroa fled to the sea; Tāne withdrew into the forests; but Rongo and Haumia-tiketike thrust themselves into the open bosom of ​their mother the Earth, and there they hid (Grey, Polynesian Mythology). When the storm at last spent itself, it was Tūmatauenga, the one brother who had stood his ground, who turned on the ‌hidden gods: he dug with a sharp stake into the earth and dragged them out — Rongo's kūmara and Haumia's aruhe, the fern-root — and so those foods were given to humankind. The ​myth is exact about its meaning: agriculture is not a theft from nature but a rescue of hidden gods. The digging-stick wounds the mother gently, and what it brings up is a brother.

​The First Fruits of Rongomatāne (The Planting)

In ritual rather than epic, Rongo's story was re-enacted every planting season. The fuller name Rongomatāne — Rongo-of-the-abundant-soil — was invoked in the karakia that opened ‌the kūmara garden; the seed-tubers went into the ground with the digging-stick, the first fruits of the old crop were returned to the god before anyone ate, and the storehouse stood under his ‌tapu until the new year (Tregear, The Maori-Polynesian Comparative Dictionary, s.v. Rongo; Best, Māori Religion and Mythology). To eat kūmara without acknowledging Rongo was to break the treaty between the digger and ‌the hidden brother; Best records planting prayers that address the god by name and lineage, asking the earth's permission as one asks a chief's. The myth's ending was thus rewritten annually in every ​garden of the North Island: the god hides in the ground, the people dig him up with thanks, and the harvest is the two keeping faith.
