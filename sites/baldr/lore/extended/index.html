Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse — Baldr, son of Óðinn and Frigg; also a rare poetic common noun, baldr 'lord, prince'.
  • Old High German — Phol, the figure paired with Wodan in the Second Merseburg Charm, widely — though not universally — read as Baldr.
  • Old English — Bældæg, son of Woden in the Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies, whose first element is the same word.
  • Modern Icelandic — Baldur, the name's unbroken living form.

Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

No cult of Baldr is attested, on any of the usual evidence: no runestone invokes him, no place-name can be securely his, no festival or priesthood is recorded, and the Edda gives him rites but no worshipers. His one possible appearance outside the poems is liturgical in character — the Second Merseburg Charm, in which 'Balder's foal' is lamed and Wodan heals it, would set his name inside an actual healing formula spoken in the tenth century, though the identification of the charm's Phol with Baldr remains argued rather than proven. What survives in place of a cult is a footprint in the language: baldrs brá, 'Baldr's eyelash', the Old Norse name for the daisy, and the poetic common noun baldr, 'lord, prince' — the name living on as plant-lore and diction long after any temple that might have housed it was gone. Modern Heathen midsummer and funerary observances honour him today; that is revival, warmly meant and historically new.

In the Earth

Baldr is an almost entirely textual god: no runestone invokes him, no votive deposit names him, and the place-name evidence sometimes claimed for him is insecure. The nearest material witnesses are indirect. Úlfr Uggason's Húsdrápa (c. 983), quoted in Skáldskaparmál, describes the funeral procession — Óðinn, Freyr, Heimdallr, the valkyries, and Hyrrokkin launching the ship — as carved on the panels of Óláfr pái's hall at Hjarðarholt in Iceland, proving the myth's currency in pre-Christian art a generation before conversion. On the continent, the Second Merseburg Charm survives in a tenth-century theological manuscript from Fulda (now Merseburg, Cathedral Library), the only Old High German text that may carry the god's name. The Codex Regius preserves Völuspá and Grímnismál; Baldrs draumar is found only in the fourteenth-century manuscript AM 748 I 4to.

Iconography

How Baldr was imaged across the ages

The Image

Baldr's one attested Viking Age portrait existed, and is lost. About 983 Úlfr Uggason composed Húsdrápa on the carved panels of Óláfr pái's new hall at Hjarðarholt in Iceland — the funeral described stanza by stanza, Óðinn riding Sleipnir, Heimdallr with the horn, and Hyrrokkin wrenching Hringhorni down to the water — and the panel itself burned with the hall or rotted centuries ago. The attributes the tradition fixed are few and exact: the mistletoe shaft, the ring Draupnir laid on the pyre, the great ship, and the daisy that took its name from his eyelash. No picture stone or surviving carving has been securely read as him; the corpus gives words, not faces. The first surviving images are paper ones — the ink drawings painted into post-medieval Icelandic Edda manuscripts, where the death scene becomes a small northern crucifixion. Every later painter inherited that manuscript pose: the bright god falling, the blind brother's arm guided.

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

Baldr rode out of the Middle Ages in three codices. Völuspá and Grímnismál descend in the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda (c. 1270); Baldrs draumar survives uniquely in the fourteenth-century AM 748 I 4to; Snorri's telling lives in the Codex Regius of the Prose Edda. His rival version traveled faster: Saxo's euhemerized Balderus reached print with the 1514 editio princeps of the Gesta Danorum, more than a century before the Edda itself was printed — the 1665 Copenhagen Edda Islandorum with its Latin translation — and the Arnamagnæan collecting of the early eighteenth century then secured the Icelandic manuscripts for scholarship. The continental whisper arrived in 1841, when Georg Waitz found the Merseburg charms in the cathedral library and published them the following year, putting Baldr's possible second name, Phol, into the only pagan German charm-text on record. Nineteenth-century philology — Rask, the Grimms, Bugge's dissections of Völuspá — made him a fixture of the new Germanic studies, and the twentieth made him their most argued-about corpse, the pivot of every dying-god debate the discipline has had.

Identifications Across Traditions

The strongest cross-border witness is the Second Merseburg Charm, preserved in a tenth-century Fulda manuscript and the only pagan theogonic text in Old High German: 'Phol and Wodan rode to the wood; then Balder's foal wrenched its foot' — and Wodan charms it whole, bone to bone and blood to blood. Since Jacob Grimm, Phol has widely been read as Baldr himself, which would make the charm the one continental attestation of the god — though the identification is argued, not proven, and Simek counsels caution. The deeper syncretic debate is modern: nineteenth-century scholars, struck by the innocent god who dies and returns after the world's end, read Baldr as a northern Christ or a Frazerian dying-and-rising vegetation spirit. Current scholarship is cooler — Lindow and Orchard note that Baldr's return is scheduled strictly after Ragnarǫk, and that the myth's true company is not the Gospels but the northern complex of fratricide, failed ransom, and prophesied loss.

Modern Legacy

Baldr's afterlife is outsized precisely because his dossier is thin: the beautiful dead god became a vessel every age refills. Matthew Arnold's narrative poem 'Balder Dead' (1855) made him the Victorian emblem of vanished perfection; Frazer's 'Balder the Beautiful' volumes of The Golden Bough built a whole theory of dying gods on the mistletoe; and the Norse revival cast him as the North's own god of light. Modern Heathenry keeps him at the centre of its midsummer and funerary rites as the god of the good death and the return. The name never died in the North itself: Icelandic Baldur and Danish-Norwegian Balder remain living given names, and the daisy — baldrs brá — still carries his eyelash in the Old Norse lexicon.

The Original Script Note

What the written record preserves — and what it does not

Original Script

The normalized Old Norse form is Baldr — already plain ASCII, with no diacritic or distinctive letter for the restoration to recover; in the Younger Futhark the name would have been carved ᛒᛅᛚᛏᛦ (b-a-l-t-R, the t-rune writing d and ᛦ the final nominative -r). Because the name is pure ASCII, its domain was registered decades ago in the ordinary way: baldr.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection and is watched for release — no form of the name is currently owned, and this temple claims none.

The Meditation

What Baldr means — beyond the dictionary

Baldr is the god the Edda built out of an absence. He is given no adventure, no trial, no weapon of his own — only perfection, prophecy, and the one overlooked thing. The myth's engineering is merciless: Frigg's oath is nearly complete, and nearly is the whole story; the gods' game is nearly harmless, and the mistletoe is nearly a plant; the world's weeping is nearly unanimous. Each near-victory is a door closing. What remains is the strangest consolation in the northern corpus — not rescue, but return: after the fire and the flood of Ragnarǫk, Völuspá says, Baldr and Höðr come back together from Hel, the slain and the slayer reconciled, to a green earth. The Edda does not promise that loss can be prevented. It promises that what was best is not lost from the world for good.
