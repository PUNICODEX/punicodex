01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Baldr (baldr) ​— Light, Beauty, ‌Purity · The ‌Best and Fairest ​of the Æsir ​— is the ​shining son of ​Óðinn and Frigg ​whose death by ‌the mistletoe shaft ​is the first ​grief of the ‌gods and the ​certain herald of ‌Ragnarǫk.[1] Snorri introduces ​him with the ‌formula the whole ​tradition repeats: he ​is the best, ​and all praise ​him — so ​fair and bright ‌that light streams from ​him, wisest and ‌most gracious of ​the Æsir, and ‌yet shadowed by ​one flaw, that ‌none of his ​judgments can stand.[2]

‌His attestation is ​thin but pivotal. ​The Poetic Edda ‌knows him through ‌prophecy rather than ‌deed: Völuspá's seeress ​sees his doom, ​and the whole ‌of Baldrs draumar ​is Óðinn's ride ​to wring that ‌doom from a ​dead völva.[3] The ​Prose Edda gives ‌the narrative — ​Frigg's oath, the ‌gods' game, Loki's ​hand guiding blind ​Höðr's throw, the ​burning of Hringhorni ​with Nanna dead ‌of grief beside ‌him, Hermóðr's nine-night ​ride, and the ‌giantess Þökk's one ​refusal.[1] Saxo's euhemerized ​Balderus and the ‌Second Merseburg Charm's ‌enigmatic Phol are ‌the only substantial ‌witnesses outside the ​Icelandic corpus.[4]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Baldr — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, because ‌the normalized Old Norse ​form is itself ​plain ASCII: no ​diacritic, no distinctive ‌letter, nothing for ​the ASCII alphabet ​to lose. The ​name is therefore ‌Tier 2 by ​the project's mechanical ‌rule — a ​property of its ‌orthography, not of ​the god's standing. ‌The plain-ASCII domain ‌baldr.com is registered ​to a third ​party outside the ​collection and is ​watched for release; ‌the project claims ‌no domain for ​this temple.[5]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ‌Baldr (nominative; genitive ​Balders). Its etymology ​is genuinely disputed, ​and honesty requires ​both main lines. ​The stronger philological ‌line derives it ​from Proto-Germanic balþraz, ​'bold, lordly': Old ‌Norse preserves baldr ​as a rare ‌poetic common noun ​meaning 'lord, prince', ‌and Old English ​bealdor 'prince, hero' ​is its exact ​cognate — the ​god's name would ​then simply be ‌the title, 'the Lord'.[1] ​The alternative, favored ‌by scholars impressed ​by the god's ‌shining character, connects ​the name with ‌Baltic baltas 'white' ​(Lithuanian baltas), yielding ‌'the shining, the ​white one' — ​attractive to the ‌myth, but philologically ‌weaker, and Simek ‌and Orchard both ​register the dispute ​rather than settle ‌it.[2]

The ASCII ​form is not ​a compromise here: ‌normalized Old Norse ​Baldr is already ​written in plain ‌ASCII letters. There ​is no diacritic, ‌no thorn or ​eth, no vowel-length ​mark for the ​early domain-name system ​to have stripped. ‌The name is ‌classified Tier 2 ​by the project's ‌mechanical rule — ​the restoration preserves ​nothing the ASCII ‌form loses — ‌which is a ‌statement about orthography, ‌never about the ​god. The final ‌-r is the ​Old Norse nominative ​masculine ending, borne ​by the plain ‌letter itself.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • ​b → B ​— Same, capitalized

‌- a → ​a — Same
​- l → ​l — Same
‌- d → ​d — Same
‌- r → ​r — Same

‌The project holds ​no domain for ‌this name: baldr.com ‌is registered to ​a third party ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈbɑl.dr̩/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- B- — ​Voiced bilabial ‌stop [b], ​fully voiced in ​initial position, ‌as in ​English 'bald'

  • ‌-a- — ​Short open ‌back unrounded [ɑ], ​held brief; ​Old Norse ​short a, ​not the long ​á
  • ‌-ldr — ​Lateral [l], voiced ‌stop [d], ​and the ‌syllabic trill [r̩] ​of the ‌nominative ending, ​which carries its ‌own beat

​For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest approximation ‌is: 'BAHL-dur' ‌— a ​short open 'ah', ​the l ‌and d ​run together, and ​a light ‌syllabic r ​at the end, ​as in ‌modern Icelandic ​Baldur.

Kindred ‌and historical forms ​of the ​name:

  • ​Old Norse — ​Baldr, son ‌of Óðinn ‌and Frigg; also ​a rare ‌poetic noun, ​'lord, prince'
  • ​Old High ‌German — ‌Phol, paired ‌with Wodan in ‌the Second ​Merseburg Charm, ‌widely read as ​Baldr[2]
  • ​Old English ​— Bældæg, son ‌of Woden ​in the ​Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies[3]

​- Latinized ‌— Balderus, ​the form ​Saxo Grammaticus writes
​- Modern ‌Icelandic — ​Baldur, the unbroken ‌living form

​Baldr is ‌Tier 2 because ​the Old ‌Norse form ‌is already plain ​ASCII: nothing ​distinctive is ​lost in ​transit. The god's ‌fame vastly ‌outruns his ​name's orthography.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet as ​Baldr — Old ​Norse (North ​Germanic), attested 13th ​c. CE in ‌the Codex Regius ​and the ​codices of the ‌Prose Edda, recording ​material centuries ‌older.[1] The manuscript ​form is itself ‌plain ASCII: Old ​Norse writes ​no diacritic in ​this name, and ​the restoration changes ​nothing but ‌the capital. In ​the Younger Futhark ‌— the sixteen-rune ​Viking-Age row ‌— the name ​would have been ‌carved ᛒᛅᛚᛏᛦ (b-a-l-t-R); ​the runic ‌row underspecifies what ​the manuscripts fix, ​with ᛏ writing ‌both t ‌and d and ‌ᛦ the final ​nominative -r.[2]

​The scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step by ​step:

  • The ​name is ‌a single stem, ​bald- + the ​nominative masculine ending ‌-r; no ​compound, no prefix.

‌- Old Norse ​orthography requires no ​accent: the ​a is short, ​and short vowels ‌carry no mark.
‌- The ​etymology is disputed: ‌Proto-Germanic balþraz 'bold, ​lordly' (with ​the poetic noun ‌baldr 'lord, prince' ‌and Old English ‌bealdor as ‌cognates) against the ​'white, shining' line ‌that would connect ​the name ​to the god's ​radiance; the handbooks ‌register the dispute ​rather than ​settle it.[3]

  • ​The Unicode restoration ‌Baldr is therefore ​identical to ​the ASCII form ​— a Tier ‌2 name by ​the mechanical ‌rule, whose dignity ​comes from the ‌god, not ​from rescued diacritics.

‌PuniCodex holds no ‌domain for this ​name: the ​plain-ASCII baldr.com is ​registered to a ​third party outside ‌the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, runic appendix (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  3. Cleasby & Vigfússon, s.v. baldr; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, s.v. Baldr (etymology).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Baldr is ​catalogued under ‌Light, Beauty, ‌Purity — ​and the ​sources earn ​every term ​of it: ​light streams ‌from his ​face, his ​eyelash names the ‌whitest plant ​in the ‌world, and ​his hall ‌admits nothing ​impure.[1]

​Light

Snorri's ​portrait makes ​brightness a ​physical property: ‌Baldr is so ​fair that ‌a splendour ​radiates from ‌him, and ​the daisy, ‌whitest of ​all plants, ‌is baldrs ​brá, 'Baldr's ​eyelash' — ‌his brightness the ‌standard by ‌which whiteness ​itself is ​measured.

‌Beauty

'He ​is the ​best, and ‌all praise ​him': the ​formula is ‌repeated across the ​corpus as ‌the fixed ​epithet of ​the fairest ​of the ​Æsir, most ‌beautifully spoken ‌and most ​merciful.

‌Purity

His ​dwelling is Breiðablik, ​the broad-gleaming, ‌'in the ‌land where ‌the fewest ‌bale-runes lie' ​— Grímnismál's ‌way of ​saying that ​nothing unclean ​has footing ‌there.[2]

Peace ​and Judgment

​He is ​the mildest ‌of judges, ​and Snorri ​adds the ​haunting rider ‌that none ​of his ‌judgments can ​stand — as ‌if the ​tradition already ‌knew his ‌goodness was ​not for ​this world. ​His son ​Forseti inherits ‌the office ‌of reconciliation ​in full.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 22, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Grímnismál 12 (Breiðablik), trans. Larrington (2014).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Baldr's iconography ​is the ‌iconography of ‌his death ​— the corpus ​gives him ​no regalia ​in life, but ​fixed around ‌his pyre ​a set ​of objects no ‌retelling can ​omit:[1]

  • ‌The mistletoe ​— The one ‌shoot Frigg ​judged too ​young to swear ​the oath: ​slender, fair, ​and fatal; ‌Völuspá calls it ​high-grown and ‌very beautiful, ​which is ‌the myth's whole ​irony in ‌a plant

​- Hringhorni — ‌His ship, ​'greatest of ​all ships', ‌which the Æsir ‌could not ‌launch until ​the giantess ​Hyrrokkin came riding ‌a wolf ​with vipers ​for reins and ‌shoved it ​to the ​sea

  • ‌Draupnir — The ​drip-gold ring ‌Óðinn laid ​on the ​pyre; Baldr sent ​it back ​from Hel ‌with Hermóðr as ‌the token ​that the ‌dead god ​had received the ​errand
  • ‌Breiðablik — ‌The broad-gleaming ‌hall, Grímnismál's seventh ‌station of ​heaven, where ‌the fewest evils ​lie
  • ​Baldrs brá ​(the daisy) ‌— The whitest ​of all ​plants, named ​for his ‌eyelash: the only ​attribute Baldr ​carries in ​living botany
  • ‌The ring ​of the ‌fire — ​The pyre itself, ‌hallowed by ​Þórr's hammer ‌Mjölnir, into ‌which the dwarf ​Litr was ​kicked — ​the funeral as ​the gods' ‌last ceremony ‌in an ​unbroken world

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Völuspá 31–33; Grímnismál 12, trans. Larrington (2014).

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Baldr's mythology is a ​single arc — foreseen, enfolded, executed, ‌mourned, and answered only after the end ‌of the world — told across both ​Eddas with unusual agreement.[1]

The ​Dreams and the Prophecy (Baldrs draumar · ​Völuspá)

It begins with dreams: Baldr dreams ​balefully for his life, and the ​Æsir take counsel. In Baldrs draumar, Óðinn ‌himself saddles Sleipnir, rides down to Niflhel, ​and raises a dead völva from ​her grave east of Hel's gate. She ‌answers what she would not: the benches ​of Hel are strewn and the ‌mead brewed for Baldr; Höðr will be ​the slayer; Váli, born to Óðinn, will ‌avenge him one night old, neither ​washing his hands nor combing his hair ​until Baldr's foe burns; and Þökk will ​weep dry tears — let Hel ​hold what she has.[2] Völuspá gives the ​same doom to its seeress: she saw ‌the mistletoe, slim and very fair, ​and from that shoot the sorrow-dart, and ‌Höðr shooting.[3]

The Oath and the ​Throw (Gylfaginning 49)

Frigg fights the ‌prophecy with an oath, exacted from all ​things — fire, water, metals, stones, earth, ‌trees, sicknesses, beasts, birds, serpents — ​that they will not harm Baldr. Only ‌the mistletoe, growing west of Valhǫll, seemed ​too young to ask. The gods' ​pastime becomes shooting and hurling at Baldr, ‌whom nothing can hurt, until Loki, in ‌a woman's shape, learns the omission ‌from Frigg, puts the mistletoe in blind ​Höðr's hand, and guides the throw. The ​shaft goes through Baldr and he ‌falls — Snorri calls it the most ​ill-fated deed done among gods and men. ​The Æsir cannot even weep at ‌first; and they cannot take vengeance, for ​the place is a sanctuary.[1]

The ​Burning of Hringhorni

The Æsir bear ‌Baldr to the sea and bring out ​Hringhorni, greatest of all ships, which ‌they cannot move; the giantess Hyrrokkin launches ​it with one shove, fire flying from ​the rollers. On the pyre they ​lay Baldr, and Nanna, his wife, daughter ​of Nepr, dies of grief at the ‌sight and is laid beside him. ‌Óðinn lays the ring Draupnir on the ​fire; Þórr hallows the pyre with Mjölnir ‌and kicks the dwarf Litr into ​the flames.[1]

The Ransom and the ​Refusal

Hermóðr the Bold rides nine nights ‌to Hel and begs for Baldr's ‌release; Hel answers that all things, living ‌and lifeless, must weep him out. Messengers ‌go through the world and all ​things weep — men and beasts, earth ‌and stones, trees and metals — save ​the giantess Þökk in her cave: ​'Let Hel hold what she has.' The ​Æsir believed Þökk was Loki. The condition ‌fails by one refusal, and Baldr ​remains with the dead.[1]

The Vengeance ​and the Return

Váli, born to Óðinn ​for the purpose, kills Höðr when ‌one night old, and Loki is bound ​until Ragnarǫk. Then comes the corpus's strangest ​consolation: after the fire and flood, ​Völuspá says, Baldr and Höðr come back ‌together from Hel — slain and slayer ​reconciled — to the green earth, ‌where the fields grow unsown.[3]

Saxo's ​Counter-Tradition

Saxo Grammaticus tells the death as ‌heroic history: Balderus, a demigod son ​of Odin whom no ordinary steel can ‌wound, and the human prince Høtherus contend ‌for Nanna's hand; Høtherus wins the ​sword Mimming from the satyr Mimingus, wounds ​Balderus, and the demigod dies after three ​days; Bous — Saxo's Váli — ​is begotten to avenge him. The euhemerized ‌skeleton keeps the myth's bones: the invulnerable ‌man, the one weapon, the rival's ​hand, the begotten avenger.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The single ​continental witness ‌is the ‌Second Merseburg Charm, ​preserved in ​a tenth-century Fulda ​manuscript: 'Phol ​and Wodan ​rode to the ‌wood; then ​Balder's foal wrenched ​its foot,' ‌and Wodan ​charms the limb ‌whole, bone ​to bone, ‌blood to blood. ​Since Jacob ​Grimm, Phol has ​widely been ​read as ​Baldr — which ‌would make ​this the god's ‌only Old ​High German ‌attestation — but ​the identification ‌is argued, not ​proven, and ‌modern handbooks ​print it with ​a question ‌mark.[1] Old ‌English offers a ‌genealogical shadow ​rather than a ​myth: Bældæg, ‌'son of ​Woden', stands in ​the West ‌Saxon royal genealogies, ​his name ​compounding the ‌same word with ​'day'.[2]

The ‌larger syncretic ​debate is the ​scholarship's own. ​Nineteenth- and early ​twentieth-century readers, ‌struck by ‌the innocent god ​who dies ‌and returns, aligned ​Baldr with ​the Christ ‌of the Gospels ‌or with ‌Frazer's dying-and-rising ‌vegetation spirits — ​the 'Balder ‌the Beautiful' volumes ​of The ​Golden Bough ​made him the ‌type of ​the slain god. ​Current scholarship ​is cooler: ‌Lindow and Orchard ​observe that ​Baldr's return is ​scheduled strictly ‌after Ragnarǫk, ​that his death ‌functions as ​the tragedy ‌of the gods ​rather than ‌as a fertility ‌rite, and ​that the ​myth's true company ​is the ​northern complex of ‌fratricide, failed ‌ransom, and ​prophesied loss.[3]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Baldr's ​afterlife is ‌outsized precisely ‌because his ​dossier is ​thin: the ​beautiful dead ​god became ​a vessel ‌every age ​refills. Matthew ​Arnold's 'Balder ‌Dead' (1855) ​made him ‌the Victorian ​emblem of ‌vanished perfection, ​and the ​nineteenth-century Norse ​revival cast ​him as ​the North's ‌own ​god of ‌light and ​innocence.[1] Frazer's ‌The Golden ​Bough built ‌whole volumes ​— 'Balder ‌the Beautiful' ​— on ​the mistletoe, ‌and though ‌the theory ‌has faded, ​the phrase ​fixed Baldr ‌in the ​English imagination ​as the ‌slain god ​par excellence.[2]

​In the ‌North ​itself the ‌name never ​died: Icelandic ​Baldur and ​Danish-Norwegian Balder ​remain living ‌given names, ‌and the ​daisy still ‌carries his ​eyelash in ​the Old ‌Norse word ‌baldrs brá. ‌Modern Heathen ‌and Ásatrú ​communities keep ‌him at ​the centre ​of their ​rites of ‌mourning ​and midsummer, ​reading his ​death and ‌promised return ​as the ​tradition's theology ​of loss. ‌In modern ​fiction, games, ‌and comics ​he appears ‌whenever a ​retelling needs ‌the gods' ‌innocence to ​break — ​his death ​is the ​standard first ‌movement of ‌any Ragnarǫk ​narrative.[3]

Sources

  1. Arnold, Matthew, 'Balder Dead', in Poems (London: Longman, 1855).
  2. Frazer, James George, The Golden Bough: Balder the Beautiful, 2 vols., 3rd ed. (London: Macmillan, 1913).
  3. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Baldr (reception).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Baldr ​is an ‌almost entirely ‌textual god: ​no runestone ​invokes him, ​no votive ​deposit ​names him, ‌and the ​place-name evidence ​sometimes claimed ‌on his ​behalf is ‌insecure.[1] ​The material ‌witnesses are ​indirect but ​real. Úlfr ​Uggason's Húsdrápa, ​composed ​about 983 ‌and preserved ​in Skáldskaparmál, ‌describes the ​funeral procession ‌— Óðinn, ​Freyr, ‌Heimdallr, the ​valkyries, and ‌Hyrrokkin launching ​the ship ​— as ‌carved on ‌the ‌panels of ​Óláfr pái's ​hall at ‌Hjarðarholt in ​Iceland, proving ​that the ‌Baldr ​myth was ​monumental art ‌a generation ​before the ‌conversion.[2]

On ​the ​continent, the ​Second Merseburg ​Charm survives ‌in a ‌tenth-century theological ​manuscript from ‌Fulda ​— the ​only Old ‌High German ‌text that ‌may carry ‌the god's ​name, ‌whatever one ​makes of ​Phol. The ​poetic witnesses ‌are likewise ​codicological: Völuspá ​and ​Grímnismál in ‌the Codex ​Regius (c. ​1270), while ​Baldrs draumar ‌is ​found only ‌in the ​fourteenth-century manuscript ‌AM 748 ​I 4to ‌— a ‌reminder ​that the ​poem on ​which so ​much rests ‌hangs by ‌a single ​thread.[3]

  2. Úlfr Uggason, Húsdrápa, preserved in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987); Lindow, Norse Mythology: A Guide (2001), s.v. Húsdrápa.
  3. The Poetic Edda, trans. Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2014), manuscript introduction.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda knows ‌Baldr almost ‌entirely through prophecy ​— he ​is the ​one god ​whose story is ​told before ‌it happens. ​Völuspá's seeress sees ​it: the ‌mistletoe 'stood ​slim and ‌very fair, high-grown'; ​from that ‌shoot came ​a dangerous dart ​of sorrow, ​and Höðr ​shot it; and ​Óðinn's son ‌Váli, one ​night old, ‌will neither wash ​his hands ‌nor comb ​his hair until ‌Baldr's slayer ​burns.[1] The ‌same poem closes ​the arc: ​after Ragnarǫk, ‌Baldr and ‌Höðr return from ‌Hel together, ​and the ​fields grow unsown.[2]

‌Baldrs draumar ​is the ​corpus's most ‌concentrated Baldr text ​— the ​whole poem ‌is the foreknowledge ​of his ‌death. Óðinn ​rides to Niflhel, ​wakes a ​long-dead völva ​east of ‌Hel's gate, and ‌forces the ​doom from ‌her stanza by ​stanza: Hel's ​hall is ‌decked for ‌Baldr; Höðr will ‌strike; Váli ‌will avenge; ​and the poem ‌ends with ​the völva ​recognizing her questioner ​— 'you ‌are not ​Végtam, as ​I thought; you ​are Óðinn, ‌the old ​enchanter' — and ​her final ​word that ‌none will wake ​her again ‌until Loki ​breaks free ‌and the destroyers ​come.[3] Grímnismál ‌contributes the ‌one bright note: ​Breiðablik, the ​seventh land, ​where Baldr ​built his hall ‌and where ‌the fewest ​bale-runes lie.[4]

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Baldr's great narrative ‌witness. Gylfaginning ​22 paints ​the portrait — ​the best ​and fairest, light ​streaming from ‌his face, ​baldrs brá the ​whitest plant, ‌wisest and most ​merciful, and ‌the strange ​rider that none ‌of his ​judgments can stand ​— so ​that Gylfaginning ​49 can spend ​it. That ‌chapter is the ​longest continuous ‌narrative in the ​Edda: the ‌dreams, Frigg's ​oath from all ‌things, the ​mistletoe's exemption, the ‌gods' game, ​Loki's disguised ​questions, Höðr's guided ‌throw, the ‌gods struck speechless ‌in the ​sanctuary, the ​funeral they cannot ‌begin until ​Hyrrokkin launches Hringhorni, ​Nanna's death, ‌Óðinn's ring, ​Þórr's hammer and ​the dwarf ‌Litr, Hermóðr's nine ​nights, Hel's ‌condition, the world's ​weeping, and ​Þökk's refusal ​— 'the most ​ill-fated deed ‌done among gods ‌and men'.[1]

​Skáldskaparmál keeps ‌the myth inside ​the poetic ​system itself: poets ‌are taught ‌to call ‌Baldr 'son of ‌Óðinn and ​Frigg, husband of ‌Nanna, father ​of Forseti, ​owner of Hringhorni ​and Draupnir, ‌foe of Höðr, ​companion of ​Hel' — the ​god's whole ‌biography compressed ​into a kenning ​list. And ​it is Skáldskaparmál ‌that preserves ​Úlfr Uggason's ‌Húsdrápa, the tenth-century ​poem describing ‌the funeral as ​carved on ‌an Icelandic ‌hall — proof ​that Snorri ​was codifying a ​myth his ​audience had ‌seen on walls ‌for two ​hundred years.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 22 and 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for Baldr; Húsdrápa), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no invocation ​of the ​god ​Baldr. No ​runestone ​asks his ‌favour, ​no amulet ​bears his ‌name, ​and no ‌inscription ​links a ‌dedicant ​to his ​cult — ​a ​silence consistent ​with ‌a god ​whose ‌prominence in ​the ‌surviving myth ​far outruns ‌his ​footprint in ‌lived ​religion.[1]

What ​the ‌epigraphic and ‌onomastic record ‌does ​preserve is ​the ‌name's raw ​material. ​Old Norse ‌baldr survives ​as ​a rare ‌poetic ​common noun, ‌'lord, ​prince', and ​its cognate ​Old ​English bealdor ‌'prince, ‌hero' is ​ordinary ‌heroic vocabulary ​in Beowulf ​— ‌the theonym ‌is ‌an elevated ‌title, ​whatever its ‌ultimate etymology.[2] ​In ​the Younger ​Futhark ‌the name ​would ​have been ​carved ‌ᛒᛅᛚᛏᛦ (b-a-l-t-R): ​the t-rune ​ᛏ ​writes both ‌t ​and d, ‌and ​ᛦ carries ‌the final ​nominative ‌-r — ‌a ​normalized reconstruction, ​since ​no Viking-Age ​carving of ‌the ‌name is ​known.[3]

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, the great ‌counter-witness is ‌Saxo Grammaticus, whose ​Gesta Danorum (Book ​3) tells ​the Baldr story ​as Danish history ​a generation ‌before Snorri's text ​reached its final ​form. Saxo's ‌Balderus is a ​demigod son of ‌Odin whose ​body no ordinary ‌steel can wound; ​his rival ​is the human ​prince Høtherus, and ​the prize ​is Nanna. The ‌myth's machinery survives ​in heroic ‌dress: Høtherus wins ​the sword Mimming ‌from the ​satyr Mimingus, receives ‌strength-stealing food from ​strange forest ‌maidens, wounds Balderus ​at last, and ​the demigod ‌dies on the ‌third day and ‌is buried ​in a barrow; ​Bous — Saxo's ‌form of ​Váli — is ​begotten of Odin ‌and Rinda ​to avenge him, ​and kills Høtherus ‌before dying ​of his own ‌wounds.[1] The two ​accounts are ​the tradition's own ​proof that the ​story was ‌old, northern, and ‌told in more ​than one ‌key.

In the ​skaldic record the ​myth is ‌older than any ‌prose telling: Úlfr ‌Uggason's Húsdrápa ‌(c. 983) describes ​the funeral on ‌Óláfr pái's ​carved hall panels, ​and the skalds ​use Baldr ‌in kennings for ​men — a ​warrior is ​a 'Baldr' of ‌the sword or ​the shield, ​the god's name ​having become a ‌common noun ​for a fighting ‌man.[2] Saxo knows ​no Höðr ‌as blind brother ​and no mistletoe; ‌Snorri knows ‌no Mimming. Between ​them stands the ​whole problem ​of Baldr: one ​death, two traditions, ‌and no ‌version that can ​be dismissed.

Sources

  1. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 3, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).
  2. Úlfr Uggason, Húsdrápa, in Skáldskaparmál, trans. Faulkes (1987); Cleasby & Vigfússon, s.v. baldr (the noun in kennings).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Baldr ​is the ‌god the ‌Edda built ​out of ​an ​absence. He ​is given ​no adventure, ‌no trial, ​no ​weapon of ‌his own ​— only ‌perfection, prophecy, ​and the ‌one ​overlooked thing. ​The myth's ​engineering is ​merciless: Frigg's ​oath ‌is nearly ​complete, and ‌nearly is ​the whole ‌story; ​the gods' ‌game is ​nearly harmless, ‌and the ​mistletoe ​is nearly ‌a plant; ‌the world's ‌weeping is ​nearly ​unanimous. Each ‌near-victory is ​a door ​closing.[1]

What ‌remains ​is the ​strangest consolation ‌in the ​northern corpus ‌— not ​rescue, ​but return. ​After the ​fire and ‌the flood ‌of ​Ragnarǫk, Völuspá ‌says, Baldr ​and Höðr ​come back ‌together ‌from Hel, ‌the slain ‌and the ​slayer reconciled, ‌to ​a green ​earth.[2] The ​Edda does ‌not promise ​that ​loss can ​be prevented. ‌It promises ​that what ​was ​best is ‌not lost ​from the ‌world for ​good — ‌and ​asks, in ‌the meantime, ‌what a ​life is ​worth ​that is ​lived inside ‌a prophecy ‌it cannot ​escape.

  2. Völuspá 62 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
