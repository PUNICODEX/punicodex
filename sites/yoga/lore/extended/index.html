Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

No priesthood — the practitioner is the officiant. The daily āsana and prāṇāyāma are the liturgy; the guru is the tradition’s single human institution. International Day of Yoga (June 21) is its largest annual congregation.

In the Earth

The discipline leaves postures, not temples: the Mohenjo-daro seal of a figure in yogic seat (its identity debated), the Mathura images of śramaṇa ascetics, and the modern globe’s studios. The oldest ‘monument’ is the human body arranged to be still.

05

Iconography

How Yoga was imaged across the ages

The Image

The seated ascetic — Śiva as Yogīśvara, lord of yogis, on the Elephanta reliefs — is the discipline’s master-image: stillness arranged. The cakra-diagrams of the subtle body and the haṭha-yoga’s diagrams of the inner sun and moon are its anatomy.

06

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

The modern world received yoga twice: first as esoteric philosophy through theosophy, then as physical culture through the global āsana movement. Scholars debate what was lost and what survived; the practice itself — two hundred million daily acts of stillness — is the largest living answer the lexicon contains.

Identifications Across Traditions

Buddhism and Jainism adopted yoga wholesale — the Buddha’s dhyāna is Patañjali’s dhāraṇā by another name — while Sufi India found its own correspondences. The modern export reversed the flow: haṭha-yoga, stripped of metaphysics, returned to India from the West as physical culture before being re-spiritualized. The word is now claimed by three civilizations at once.

Modern Legacy

Some two hundred million people practise asana today — the largest following of any discipline in the lexicon. ‘Yoga’ entered English as a loan-word a century ago and now needs no italics. The discipline’s texts stand, with the Upaniṣads and the Gītā, among the world’s most translated and most printed books.

The Meditation

What Yoga means — beyond the dictionary

Yoga: the yoke that joins — and the word itself is joined, across four millennia and two language families, to ‘yoke’. Restored plainly, it demonstrates what the discipline teaches: the simplest form can carry the heaviest joining. Two syllables, one root, half the planet practising.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Yoga, Union, Discipline, and canonical restoration

01How do you pronounce Yoga?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Yoga is /ˈjoʊɡə/ — approximately “YOH-guh”.

02What does Yoga mean?

Yoga means Union, yoking — spiritual and ascetic discipline by which the individual self is joined to the divine; also the name of one of the six orthodox schools of Hindu philosophy. in the sanskrit tradition.

03What are the symbols of Yoga?

Yoga is associated with The Eight Limbs (Patañjali’s aṣṭāṅga: restraint, observance, posture, breath, withdrawal, focus, meditation, absorption.), Union of Rivers (The yogin joins mind to breath to object as rivers join the sea — the Upaniṣadic image.), Yoga in the Gītā (Skill in action: the yogin works without craving the fruit — karma-yoga as the path for the householder.), The Cessation of the Waves (Patañjali’s definition: yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ — the stilling of thought’s waves, so the seer abides in itself.).

04Why use the canonical spelling of Yoga?

Yoga is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Yoga?

“The Chariot Simile” — the Kaṭha Upaniṣad story that anchors the mythology of Yoga, told in full on the lore page.
