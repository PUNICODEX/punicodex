Mythology

Yoga has no myths ‌— it has sūtras, and the sūtras are their own literature of precision. Its ‘stories’ are the doctrine’s own history: the ascetics in the forests of the Upaniṣads, the ​Nātha-lineage’s wonder-working adepts, and the modern export that made a metaphysical discipline into the world’s most practised physical art.

The Chariot Simile (Kaṭha Upaniṣad)

The body the chariot, the ‌senses the horses, the mind the reins, the intellect the driver, the rider the self: the Kaṭha Upaniṣad’s simile is the oldest complete map of the psychophysical discipline. Who holds ​the reins attains the end of the road — ‘the still place from which the wise do not return’.

The Yoga of Three Paths (Bhagavadgītā)

The Gītā democratizes the ‌discipline: jñāna-yoga of knowledge for the philosopher, karma-yoga of selfless work for the householder, bhakti-yoga of devotion for the lover of god. ‘Yogasthaḥ kuru karmāṇi’ — established in yoga, ​act — is the verse on which Gandhi claimed to have fed his whole life.

The One Hundred Ninety-Five Sūtras (Patañjali)

Patañjali’s Yoga-sūtra — c. 195 aphorisms — systematized ‌what had been experiment: the eight limbs, the afflictions, the meditative absorptions, the supernormal powers that are signs on the path and traps at its end. Commentators from Vyāsa to ​the present have kept it the living constitution of the discipline.

The Immortal Teachers (Nātha Lineage)

The medieval Nātha order gave yoga its adepts-legend: Matsyendra caught among the fishes, ‌Gorakṣa the miracle-working founder, and later figures claimed to walk the centuries — the siddhas, perfected ones, whose bodies outlast death. Hatha-yoga’s physical canon comes from this line of ​embodied experimenters.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Yoga concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The Eight Limbs — Patañjali’s ​aṣṭāṅga: restraint, observance, posture, breath, withdrawal, focus, meditation, absorption.
  • Union of Rivers — The yogin joins mind to breath to object as rivers join the sea — the Upaniṣadic ‌image.
  • Yoga in the Gītā — Skill in action: the yogin works without craving the fruit — karma-yoga as the path for the householder.
  • The Cessation of the Waves ‌— Patañjali’s definition: yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ — the stilling of thought’s waves, so the seer abides in itself.

Across Cultures

Buddhism and Jainism adopted yoga wholesale — the Buddha’s dhyāna is Patañjali’s dhāraṇā by another name — while Sufi India found ​its own correspondences. The modern export reversed the flow: haṭha-yoga, stripped of metaphysics, returned to India from the West as physical culture before being re-spiritualized. The word is now ‌claimed by three civilizations at once.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.

Cultural Legacy

Some two hundred ​million people practise asana today — the largest following of any discipline in the lexicon. ‘Yoga’ entered English as a loan-word a century ago and now needs no italics. ‌The discipline’s texts stand, with the Upaniṣads and the Gītā, among the world’s most translated and most printed books.
