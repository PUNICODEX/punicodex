The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

Ὄνειρος

The name in its original Greek form. Óneiros (Ὄνειρος) is attested in the source tradition — “Dream”. Its diphthongs and acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

oneiros

Reduced to plain oneiros, the name loses everything that made it specific: diphthongs and acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Óneiros

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Óneiros restores diphthongs and acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Óneiros.com → xn--neiros-9wa.com

The non-ASCII characters in Óneiros are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Óneiros.

04

Pronunciation

How Óneiros was spoken

/ˈo.nei̯.ros/ Attic Greek Reconstruction
O- Long omega [ɔː], the stressed first syllable that opens the name of dreams.
-nei- Diphthong [ei̯], the middle syllable that gives the name its rising sound.
-ros Short [r] plus [o] plus [s], the agent-noun ending: "the dreamer" or "the dream itself".

Modern Approximation

'oh-NEH-ros' — stress on the first syllable, the middle vowel gliding like the 'ay' in 'say,' and a clean final '-ros'.

Etymological Kin

  • Greek Ὄνειρος (Oneiros), singular; Οἰνείρων or Ὄνειροι in the plural
  • Etymology Uncertain; possibly from a root meaning "to say" or "to see" in sleep
  • Latin Somnus for sleep itself; the Oneiroi appear in Latin as the sons of Somnus in Ovid

The Accent / Script Rule

Óneiros is Tier 1 because the Greek Ὄνειρος carries length (omega) and the acute accent on the first syllable, features the ASCII form oneiros flattens.

05

The Dream

Personification of Dreams and Dream-Visions

Óneiros is the Greek personification of the dream: the winged figure or figures who pass through the gates of horn and ivory, carrying true messages or false delusions to sleeping mortals. He is one of the many children of Nýx, Night, and in Homer he serves the gods as a messenger who takes human shape in sleep.

Son of Night

Hesiod makes Óneiros one of the children of Nýx, born without a father in the dark before the gods.

Messenger Through Sleep

In the Odyssey, a dream takes the form of a woman to stand beside Penelope's bed and speak to her.

The Gate of Dreams

In the Odyssey, dreams pass through gates of horn (true) and ivory (false), an image of sleep's ambiguity.

False and True Dreams

Some dreams are winged with truth, others with deception; Óneiros carries both kinds.

Sacred Symbols

Wings The speed with which dreams cross from the realm of sleep to the sleeper
Mist or cloud The insubstantial, shifting nature of dream-visions
The gates of horn and ivory The two paths by which true and false dreams reach mortals
Stars or night sky The darkness from which dreams descend, children of Nýx
06

Mythology

Stories of Óneiros

Óneiros moves through Greek literature as a servant of the gods, a child of Night, and the ambiguous voice that speaks to sleepers.

Hesiod, Theogony

Children of Night

Hesiod lists Óneiros among the children of Nýx, born without a father: "And Nýx bore hateful Moîra and black Kēr, and Thanatos and Hypnos, and the tribe of Oneiroi." The Oneiroi are therefore siblings of death and sleep, related to fate and the dark. They are not gods of cult but powers of the night.

Homer, Odyssey 19

The Gates of Horn and Ivory

Penelope, troubled by dreams, asks a dream-visitor about their truth. The dream explains that there are two gates of dreams: one of horn, through which true dreams pass, and one of ivory, through which deceptive ones come. The image gives Greek literature one of its most enduring metaphors for the ambiguity of sleep.

Homer, Iliad 2

Dream Messenger of Zeús

At the opening of the Iliad's second book, Zeús sends a dream to Agamemnon to lure him into battle. The dream takes the form of Nestor and stands at the king's head, speaking with a voice like the old counselor's. The scene establishes the Oneiroi as divine messengers who can wear the shape of the living.

Ovid, Metamorphoses

The Cave of Sleep

Ovid gives the Oneiroi a Latin home in the cave of Somnus, where a thousand dreams lie waiting to be sent. Among them is Morpheus, the shape-shifter, who becomes the most famous of the dream-figures in later Western art. The Roman tradition separates and renames the dream-powers, but the Greek Óneiros remains their source.

Extended Lore

Óneiros is the voice that speaks when the will is asleep. He is not a god of temples but a power of the night, a child of Nýx who carries messages we cannot receive by daylight. Some of those messages are true and some are false, and the dreamer must learn to tell them apart.
