Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Old Irish — badb, 'scald-crow' — the common noun for the hooded crow and the theonym are the same word; badb catha, 'crow of battle', is her standing title
  • Proto-Celtic — *bodwā, 'battle, fighting' — the root the name shares with the war-names of Gaul (Matasović, EDPC)
  • Gaulish — Cathubodua, 'battle-crow' — the goddess of the Mieussy altar, her name the exact reverse compound of badb catha
  • Modern Irish — badhbh, 'scald-crow, hooded crow' — the unbroken living word

04

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Badb is the Irish war-goddess whose name is her shape: badb, the scald-crow — the grey-and-black hooded crow that walks the battlefield when the spears have done their work. She is one of the three of war whom the Lebor Gabála calls the daughters of Ernmas — Badb, Macha, and Anand, the Morrígan — and the Táin gives her its most terrible sound: the shriek over the marching hosts that kills men with fear before a blow is struck. She never fights; she foretells, she panics, and she gathers what the battle leaves.

Badb in Later Traditions

Badb's clearest cross-border witness is Gaulish, and it is exact. An altar from Mieussy in Haute-Savoie dedicates to Cathubodua — *catu-*bodwā, 'battle-crow' — the precise reverse compound of Irish badb catha. Delamarre and Koch treat the two names as one formation across the Celtic languages: the crow of battle named in stone on the continent and in saga in Ireland. The Welsh corpus, by contrast, is silent on her — no figure of the Mabinogi continues the name, and the Brythonic silence is recorded rather than repaired. Typological comparison has set her beside the Norse valkyries, the raven-feeding choosers of the slain; the handbooks note the parallel without claiming descent. Within the lexicon her closest kin are her own sisters, [[morrigan|the Mórrígan]] and [[macha|Macha]], and the war she prophesied binds her to [[dagda|the Dagda]], [[lugh|Lugh]], and [[nuada|Nuadu]].

Modern Legacy

The crow of battle rode the Celtic revival in English dress. W. M. Hennessy's 'The Ancient Irish Goddess of War' (Revue Celtique, 1870) had already gathered the Morrígan material for scholars, and Lady Gregory's Gods and Fighting Men (1904) carried Cath Maige Tuired and its war-goddesses to a wide reading public, crow-form and prophecy complete. Modern fantasy and games have made the war-goddess's crow-shape one of the stock images of the genre, and contemporary pagan practice honors Badb among the three of the Morrígan. In the Irish language the word never needed reviving: badhbh has meant the hooded crow all along, and the grey bird on the battlefield needed no gloss for any Irish hearer. Across the water, the Scottish bean nighe kept her folk shape washing at the ford deep into the era of the great folklore collections.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Badb in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Badb, Battle, Death, Crow, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Badb?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Badb is /ˈbaðβ/ — approximately 'BAH-dhuv' — one short syllable; let the d soften into the 'th' of 'this' and the final b melt into a light v. Modern Irish badhbh keeps the softening, and English ears often hear the word as something like 'bave'..

02What does Badb mean?

Badb means Crow, scald-crow in the celtic tradition.

03What are the symbols of Badb?

Badb is associated with The scald-crow (The hooded crow, grey and black — her name, her shape, and her retinue on every field), The shriek (Panic as a weapon: the sound that kills men with fear before the spear is thrown), The ford (The boundary between bank and bank, living and dead — the water where the doomed are read), The triad (Badb, Macha, Némain: the three of war, one terror in three voices), The washing of spoils (The harness and garments of the soon-dead, rinsed in the ford before the battle that will bloody them).

04Why restore Badb in Unicode?

Plain ASCII badb strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Badb?

The Lebor Gabála Érenn, Ireland's medieval book of invasions, gives the war-goddesses their pedigree: 'Badb and Macha and Anand, of whom are the Paps of Anu in Luachair, were the three daughters of Ernmas the she-farmer.' The verse recension sings the same family in another key — 'Badb and Macha, greatness of wealth, Morrígu, springs of craftiness, sources of bitter fighting, were the three daughters of Ernmas' — so that the tradition itself is of two minds: sometimes the Morrígan is the third sister, sometimes the single name for all three.The same book marries her into the house of the war-god. Neit son of Indui, the god of battle, had two wives — Badb and Nemain — and the pedigree records the slaying of the three of them at Ailech by the Fomorians. Cormac's Glossary had already fixed the marriage in the language of record: Neith, the god of battle among the pagan Gael, and Nemon his wife. Badb is never an isolated figure; she is a sister and a wife in a family whose whole business is war.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Badb and related cults.
  • Badb is a textual goddess: no ogham stone names her, no votive inscription invokes her, and Irish paganism generally left literature rather than dedications. Her manuscript witnesses are exact. The Táin Bó Cúailnge survives in its oldest recension in Lebor na hUidre, the Book of the Dun Cow (Royal Irish Academy, c. 1100), and in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster; Lebor Gabála Érenn likewise rests on the Book of Leinster and its sister codices; and Cath Maige Tuired survives uniquely in the sixteenth-century British Library MS Harley 5280, the basis of Gray's edition. The nearest epigraphic witness is continental: the altar from Mieussy in Haute-Savoie dedicating to Cathubodua, 'battle-crow', is the standing proof that the crow-of-battle compound is old Celtic and not an Irish literary invention — comparative evidence across the water, never an identification.
