01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Badb (badb) ​— Battle, Death, ‌Crow · Crow, ‌scald-crow — is the ​Irish war-goddess whose ​name is her ​shape: Old Irish ​badb, the scald-crow, ​is the common ‌noun for the hooded ​crow (Corvus cornix) ​and the theonym ‌in a single ​word, and the ‌medieval writers never ​felt the need to ‌choose between them.[1] ​Her standing title ​in the texts ​is badb catha, ​'crow of battle' ​— the bird that ‌haunts the field ​and the goddess ‌who owns it, ​named in one ‌breath.[1]

She belongs to ​the war-goddess complex ‌the Lebor Gabála ​Érenn calls the ‌daughters of Ernmas ​— Badb, Macha, ​and Anand, of whom ‌are the Paps ‌of Anu in ‌Luachair — a ​genealogy the verse ​recension gives as ‌Badb, Macha, and the ​Morrígu, so that ​the tradition itself ‌is of two ​minds as to ​whether the Morrígan ‌is the third sister ​or the single ‌name for all ​three.[2] The same ​book marries her ​to the war-god: ​Neit son of Indui ‌had two wives, ‌Badb and Nemain.[2] ​Her dossier is ‌voice and omen ​rather than adventure: ​the shriek over the ‌hosts in the ‌Táin that kills ‌a hundred warriors ‌with fright,[3] the ​war-goddess washing at ‌the ford of the ​Unius before Mag ​Tuired,[4] and the ​scald-crows settling on ‌the dying Cú ​Chulainn's pillar-stone.[5]

PuniCodex restores ​the name as ​Badb — identical ‌to the ASCII ​input, because normalized ​Old Irish writes ​no diacritic in this ‌name. The mechanical ​rule therefore places ‌it in Tier ​2: nothing distinctive ‌is lost in ​transit, a statement about ‌orthography and never ‌about the goddess. ​No domain is ​claimed: the plain-ASCII ​badb.com is registered ​to a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[1]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The theonym ​is attested in Old ‌and Middle Irish as ‌badb (later badhbh), a ​feminine noun whose first ​and never-abandoned sense is ​zoological: the scald-crow, ​the grey-and-black hooded crow ​of the Irish countryside.[1] ‌The Dictionary of the ​Irish Language records both ​registers — the bird ‌and the supernatural battle-being ​— and the ‌texts exploit the identity ​deliberately: when the war-goddess ‌is named, the hearer ​already knows her shape.[1] ​Her standing phrase is ​badb catha, 'the ​crow of battle', used ​of the battlefield terror ‌itself as readily as ​of the goddess.[1]

The ‌etymology is old Celtic, ​not Irish alone. ‌Vendryes' LEIA and Matasović's ​Proto-Celtic dictionary trace the ‌word to Proto-Celtic bodwā, ​'battle, fighting' — a ‌root the continental languages ​kept in war-names ​— and the Gaulish ‌altar from Mieussy in ‌Haute-Savoie dedicates to Cathubodua, ‌catu-bodwā, 'battle-crow': the precise ​reverse compound of the ​Irish phrase.[2] The crow ‌of battle is ​therefore not an Irish ​literary conceit but an ‌inherited Celtic formation, attested ​in stone on the ​continent and in saga ‌in Ireland.[2]

Within ​the Irish tradition the ‌name shades into her ​sisters': the Táin couples ​her with Bé Néit, ​'woman of battle', and ​Némain,[3] and the ‌Lebor Gabála's two recensions ‌of the daughters of ​Ernmas make the Morrígan ‌now the third sister, ​now the collective name.[4] ​The honest verdict of ‌the handbooks is ‌fluidity, not error: Badb, ‌Macha, Némain, and the ‌Morrígan behave as one ​complex with several voices.[5]

‌The ASCII form badb ​loses nothing, because ​the normalized Old Irish ​spelling is already plain ‌Latin letters; the restoration ​is therefore a matter ​of canonical reference, not ​rescued diacritics, and ‌the name is classified ​Tier 2 by the ​project's mechanical rule.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation runs:

  • ‌b → B — ​Same, capitalized
  • ‌a → a — ​Same
  • d → ‌d — Same
  • ​b → b — ‌Same

The project holds ‌no domain for this ​name: badb.com is ​registered to a third ​party outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is watched ‌for release. The temple ‌lives at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈbaðβ/ — ​Old Irish ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Ba- — ​Broad bilabial stop ‌[b] and ​short open [a], ​stressed on ‌the first ​— here the ‌only — ​syllable, as ‌in all Old ​Irish
  • ​-db — Lenited ​[ð], the ​voiced dental ​fricative of English ‌this, running ​into lenited final ‌[β], a ​soft bilabial ‌v; both consonants ​melt in ‌speech though the ​spelling keeps ‌them whole

​For the modern ​speaker, the ‌closest approximation ‌is: 'BAH-dhuv' — ‌one short ​syllable, the d ​softened to ‌the 'th' ​of this, the ​final b ‌to a light ​v. Modern ​Irish badhbh ‌keeps the softening, ​and English ‌ears often ​hear the word ​as something ​like 'bave'.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of ‌the name:

  • ​Old Irish ‌— badb, 'scald-crow', ​the common ​noun and ‌the theonym in ‌one word; ‌badb catha, ‌'crow of battle'

​- Proto-Celtic ‌— *bodwā, 'battle, ​fighting', the ​root beneath ​the name (Matasović, ‌EDPC)

  • ​Gaulish — Cathubodua, ​'battle-crow', the ​goddess of ‌the Mieussy altar

​- Modern ​Irish — badhbh, ​'scald-crow, hooded ‌crow', the ​unbroken living word[2]

‌Badb is ​Tier 2 ‌because the restoration ​preserves nothing ‌ASCII loses: the ‌letters of ​the manuscript ​form are the ​letters of ​the keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Sources

  1. Thurneysen, Rudolf, A Grammar of Old Irish, trans. D. A. Binchy and Osborn Bergin (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1946), on lenition and initial stress.
  2. Matasović, Ranko, Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Celtic (Leiden: Brill, 2009), s.v. *bodwo-; Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise (Paris: Errance, 2003), s.v. boduā.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌medieval Irish ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet as ​badb — ​Old Irish / ​Middle Irish in ​Insular script, ‌attested Old Irish, ​c. 7th–10th c. ​CE, in ‌the glossaries and ​in the great ‌codices that ​preserve the Táin ‌and the Lebor ​Gabála.[1] The ​earlier monumental alphabet, ​ogham, predates the ​manuscript tradition, ​but no attestation ‌of this theonym ​has ever ‌been read on ​an ogham stone; ‌the manuscript ​form is the ‌primary witness.[2]

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step by ​step:

  • Old ​Irish was ‌written in the ‌Latin alphabet using ‌Insular minuscule ​from the early ​medieval period; the ‌spelling badb ​requires no diacritic.

​- The word ‌is a ​common noun — ​the scald-crow, the ‌hooded crow ​— and the ‌theonym is the ​same word; ​the Dictionary of ​the Irish Language ​records both ‌registers.[1]

  • The ‌medial d and ​final b ‌are lenited in ​pronunciation — [ð] ​and [β] ‌— but the ‌orthography writes plain ‌d and ‌b; nothing distinctive ​is lost in ‌ASCII.[3]
  • ​The Unicode restoration ​Badb is therefore ​identical to ‌the ASCII form ​apart from the ​capital; the ​temple form is ‌a canonical reference, ​not a ​rescued spelling.

One ​caution attaches to ‌the record: ​the texts keep ‌her name fluid ​with her ‌sisters' — Bé ​Néit, Némain, Macha, ‌the Mórrígan ‌— and the ​handbooks treat the ​complex as ​one figure with ​several voices rather ‌than a ‌scribal confusion to ​be solved.[4]

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Badb is ​catalogued under ‌the domain 'Battle, ‌Death, Crow' — ​three words ​that map exactly ​onto her ​dossier: the field ​she haunts, ‌the knowledge she ​carries, and the ​shape she ‌wears.[1]

Battle

​She never ‌fights, and the ​texts are ‌exact about it: ​her weapon is ​the voice. ​In the Táin, ​Badb and ​Bé Néit and ‌Némain shriek above ​the marching ‌hosts at Gáirech ​and Irgáirech ‌until a hundred ​warriors fall ‌dead of fright ​— the battle's ‌true opening, ​fought and won ​before a ‌spear is cast.[2]

‌### Death

‌She is the ​reader of the ​doomed: the ‌war-goddess washing at ​the ford ​of the Unius ‌on the eve ​of Mag ​Tuired,[3] the scald-crows ‌settling on ​Cú Chulainn's pillar-stone ‌to tell ​his enemies he ​is gone — ​'that pillar ​is not wont ‌to be ‌under birds'.[4] Death ​is not her ‌doing; it ​is her knowledge.

​### Crow

‌Her name is ‌the animal: ‌badb, the scald-crow, ‌the grey-and-black hooded ​crow that ‌comes down to ​the field ​when the field ​is finished. ‌The theonym refuses ​metaphor — the ​goddess has ​a species.[1]

‌The Moment ​Before

The connective ​tissue of all ​three domains ‌is foreknowledge: shriek, ​washing, and ‌settling are the ​same act ‌in three scenes ​— the end ‌of the ‌day, seen at ​its beginning. ​The later Gaelic ​washer at ​the ford, the ‌Scottish bean nighe, ‌keeps exactly ​this brief.[5]

Badb's ​attributes are ‌a ‌species, a ​sound, ​a ​place, a ​number, ​and a ‌chore ​— the ​sparest ‌regalia in ​the ‌Irish corpus, ​and ‌the ​most exact:[1]

​- ​The scald-crow ​— ​The hooded ‌crow, ​grey and ‌black: ​her name, ‌her ​shape, ‌and her ​retinue ‌on every ​field
​- The ‌shriek ‌— Panic ‌as ​a weapon: ​the ‌sound ​over the ​hosts ‌that kills ​men ​with fear ‌before ​the spear ‌is ​thrown[2]

  • ​The ​ford ​— The ‌boundary ‌between bank ​and ‌bank, living ​and ​dead: the ‌water ‌where the ‌war-goddess ‌stands ​washing[3]
  • ‌The ​triad — ​Badb, ​Macha, Némain: ‌the ​three of ​war, ​one terror ‌in ​three ​voices[4]
  • ​The ‌washing of ​spoils ‌— The ​harness ‌and garments ​of ‌the soon-dead, ‌rinsed ​in ​the ford ​before ​the battle ‌that ‌will bloody ​them[5]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Badb's mythology is genealogy, ​sound, and omen rather than adventure: she ‌is the sister in the list, the ‌voice over the army, the bird on ​the pillar-stone. Four texts carry her dossier, ​and the commentators have spent a ​century and a half working out where ​she ends and her sisters begin.[1]

​The Daughters of Ernmas (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

‌The Lebor Gabála Érenn gives the war-goddesses ​their pedigree: 'Badb and Macha and Anand, ​of whom are the Paps of Anu ‌in Luachair, were the three daughters of ​Ernmas the she-farmer.' The verse recension sings ‌the family in another key — 'Badb ​and Macha, greatness of wealth, Morrígu, springs ‌of craftiness, sources of bitter fighting, ​were the three daughters of Ernmas' — ​so the tradition itself is of two ​minds about the third sister.[2] The same ​book marries her into the war-god's house: ​Neit son of Indui, god of battle, ‌had two wives, Badb and Nemain, and ​the pedigree records their slaying at Ailech ‌by the Fomorians.[2] Cormac's Glossary had already ​fixed the marriage in the record: Neith, ‌the god of battle among the pagan ​Gael, and Nemon his wife.[3]

‌The Shriek over the Hosts (Táin Bó ​Cúailnge)

When the men of Ireland march ‌under Medb for the brown bull of ​Cooley, the Táin marks the nights of ​the hosting with the sound of the ‌war-goddesses: 'Badb and Bé Néit and Némain ‌shrieked above them that night in Gáirech ‌and Irgáirech, so that a hundred of ​their warriors fell dead of fright.'[4] She ​does not wound; she announces. The shriek ‌is prophecy delivered as noise — ​and the Irish phrase for battlefield terror, ​badb catha, is simply her name at ‌work as a common noun.

The ​War-Goddess at Mag Tuired (Cath Maige Tuired)

​On Samain night before the Second Battle ‌of Mag Tuired, the Dagda met the ​Morrígan at the ford of the Unius ‌in Connacht, where she stood washing with ​one foot on either bank; she pledged ​herself to the Túatha Dé Danann's cause, ​promising the hearts of the Fomorian ​host and the blood of their king ‌Indech.[5] After Lugh's sling-stone felled Balor, it ‌was the Morrígan who proclaimed the rout ​to the heights of Ireland and uttered ‌the prophecy — 'Peace up to heaven, ​heaven down to earth, earth beneath heaven' ​— and then its darker twin, the ‌vision of the world's ending.[5] The texts ‌keep the sisters' voices interchangeable; what the ‌manuscript gives to the Morrígan, the tradition ‌hears also in Badb's register.

​The Crow on the Pillar-Stone (Aided Con ‌Culainn)

Cú Chulainn's death-tale, Brislech Mór Maige ​Muirthemni, gives the crow-goddesses the last word. ​Mortally wounded, Cú Chulainn lashed himself to ​a pillar-stone to die on his feet, ‌and for three days his enemies would ​not close in — until the battle-goddess ​Morrígu came with her sisters in the ​form of scald-crows and settled on his ‌shoulder. 'That pillar is not wont to ​be under birds,' said Erc, and ​only then did the host dare take ​the hero's head.[6] The living man keeps ‌her off; the dead man is her ​perch.

The Washer at the Ford ‌(Gaelic tradition)

The medieval texts give the ​war-goddess her washing-place at the ford of ‌the Unius;[5] the later Gaelic tradition gives ​the figure her full shape — the ‌washer at the ford, rinsing the blood-stained ‌garments of those about to die in ​battle. In Scottish Gaelic folklore she ​is the bean nighe, and the commentators, ​Mac Cana among them, treat her as ​the folk continuation of the Badb's own ‌figure.[7] The washing is prophecy, not threat: ‌the doom is already written, and she ​merely launders the evidence.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Badb's clearest ​cross-border witness ‌is Gaulish, ‌and it ​is exact. ​An altar ​from Mieussy ​in Haute-Savoie ​dedicates to ‌Cathubodua — catu-bodwā, ​'battle-crow' — ​the precise ‌reverse compound ​of Irish ‌badb catha. ​Delamarre and ‌Koch treat ​the two ​names as one ​formation across ​the Celtic ​languages: the ‌crow of ​battle named ‌in stone ​on the ‌continent and in ​saga in ‌Ireland.[1] The ​Welsh corpus, ‌by contrast, ​is silent ​on her ‌— no ‌figure of ‌the Mabinogi continues ​the name, ​and the ‌Brythonic silence ​is recorded ​rather than ‌repaired.[2] Typological ​comparison has ​set her ‌beside the Norse ​valkyries, the ‌raven-feeding choosers ​of the ​slain; the ​handbooks note ​the parallel ‌without claiming ‌descent.[3]

Within ​the Irish tradition ‌her closest ​kin are ​her own ‌sisters, the ‌Mórrígan and ‌Macha — ‌one complex ​with several ‌voices — and ​the war ​she prophesied ​binds her ‌to the ​Dagda, Lugh, ​and Nuadu. ​The Christian ‌scribes who preserved ​her did ​not euhemerize ​her into ‌a queen ​or a ‌druid, as ​they did ‌the great ​gods; they left ‌her what ‌she was, ​a bird ​and a ​voice, which ​may be ‌why the ‌name needed ​no rehabilitation.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

The ​crow of ‌battle rode ‌the Celtic ​revival ​in English ​dress. W. ​M. Hennessy's ​'The ‌Ancient Irish ​Goddess of ​War' (Revue ‌Celtique, ​1870) had ‌already gathered ​the Morrígan ‌material ​for scholars, ​and Lady ​Gregory's Gods ​and ​Fighting Men ‌(1904) carried ​Cath Maige ‌Tuired ​and its ‌war-goddesses to ​a wide ‌reading ​public, crow-form ‌and prophecy ​complete.[1] Modern ​fantasy ‌and games ‌have made ‌the war-goddess's ​crow-shape ​one of ‌the stock ​images of ​the ‌genre, and ​contemporary pagan ​practice honors ‌Badb ​among the ‌three of ​the Morrígan.[2]

​In ​the Irish ​language the ‌word never ‌needed ​reviving: badhbh ‌has meant ​the hooded ​crow ‌all along, ‌and the ‌grey bird ‌on ​the battlefield ‌needed no ​gloss for ​any ​Irish hearer.[3] ‌Across the ​water, the ​Scottish ​bean nighe ‌kept her ​folk shape ​washing ​at the ‌ford deep ​into the ‌era ​of the ‌great folklore ​collections — ‌the ‌longest-lived of ​all her ​forms, and ​the ​one that ‌never had ‌to be ​reconstructed.[2]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Badb ​is a ‌textual ‌goddess: no ​ogham ​stone names ​her, ​no votive ​inscription invokes ‌her, ​and Irish ​paganism ‌generally left ​literature ‌rather than ​dedications.[1] ‌Her manuscript ​witnesses, ​however, are ​exact. ​The Táin ​Bó ‌Cúailnge survives ​in its ‌oldest ​recension in ‌Lebor ​na hUidre, ‌the ​Book of ‌the ​Dun Cow ​(Royal ‌Irish Academy, ‌c. ‌1100), and ​in ​the twelfth-century ‌Book of ​Leinster; ​Lebor Gabála ‌Érenn ​likewise rests ​on ‌the Book ​of ‌Leinster and ​its ​sister codices; ​and ​Cath Maige ‌Tuired survives ‌uniquely ​in the ‌sixteenth-century ​British Library ​MS ‌Harley 5280, ‌the ‌basis of ‌Gray's ​edition.[2]

The ‌nearest ​epigraphic witness ​is ​continental: the ‌altar from ​Mieussy ​in Haute-Savoie ​dedicating ‌to Cathubodua, ​'battle-crow', ​is the ​standing ‌proof that ​the ‌crow-of-battle compound ​is ‌old Celtic ​and ‌not an ‌Irish literary ​invention ​— comparative ​evidence ​across the ‌water, ‌never an ​identification.[3]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Badb walks ​two cycles. ‌To the ‌Mythological Cycle ​she belongs ​by blood and ​marriage: the ​Lebor Gabála ​Érenn makes ‌her a daughter ​of Ernmas ​beside Macha ‌and the ​Morrígan, and ‌a wife of ​the war-god ‌Neit son ​of Indui;[1] ​Cath Maige ​Tuired gives the ​war-goddess her ​two great ‌scenes — ​the washing at ‌the ford ​of the ‌Unius with ​the Dagda ‌on Samain night, ​and the ‌proclamation of ​victory with ​its prophecy of ‌peace after ‌Lugh's sling ‌felled Balor.[2]

​To the ​Ulster Cycle she ‌belongs by ​voice. The ​Táin Bó ‌Cúailnge opens ​its killing with ​her shriek ‌over the ​hosts of ‌Medb at Gáirech ​and Irgáirech,[3] ​and the ​death-tale of ​Cú Chulainn, ‌Brislech Mór Maige ‌Muirthemni, closes ​the hero's ‌life with ​the scald-crows ​on his pillar-stone ‌— 'that ‌pillar is ‌not wont ‌to be under ​birds'.[4] The ‌glossaries keep ​her household ​in the ​record: Cormac's Glossary ‌glosses Neith ​as the ​god of ​battle among the ‌pagan Gael, ​with Nemon ​his wife.[5] ​Medieval scribes ‌euhemerized the great ​gods into ‌kings and ​druids; her ‌they left ​as she was ‌— a ‌bird and ​a voice ​— and she ​is perhaps ​the least ‌rehabilitated figure ‌in the ​whole corpus.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Welsh corpus ‌is silent ‌on ​Badb, and ​the silence ​deserves ​to be ​recorded rather ‌than ​repaired. Neither ​the ‌Mabinogi nor ​the wider ‌Brythonic ​tradition preserves ‌a figure ​derived ​from the ​same name, ​and ​no convincing ‌Welsh counterpart ​has ‌been established ​by the ‌modern ​handbooks.[1]

The ‌instructive ​comparison therefore ‌runs not ​west ​to Wales ‌but east ‌to ‌Gaul. The ​altar from ​Mieussy ‌in Haute-Savoie ​dedicates to ​Cathubodua ‌— catu-bodwā, ​'battle-crow' — ​the ‌exact reverse ​compound ‌of the ​Irish badb ​catha: ​the crow ​of battle ‌turns ‌out to ​be old ‌Celtic ​common property, ​named in ‌stone ‌on the ‌continent and ‌in ​saga in ‌Ireland, ​with Wales ​simply absent ​from ‌the attestation ​map.[2] Where ​the ​Irish war-goddess ‌complex is ​rich ​and tripled, ​the Welsh ‌tradition ​distributes its ‌war and ​sovereignty ‌figures otherwise; ​the ‌honest note ‌is that ​the ​two insular ​traditions did ​not ‌divide the ‌same inheritance ​evenly.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Badb. ‌Ogham, the ​early ​Irish monumental ​script of ​roughly ​the fourth ‌to ​seventh centuries, ​survives ‌on some ​four ‌hundred stones ​bearing ‌mostly personal ​names in ​formulaic ​genitives; no ​reading ​of this ‌theonym ​has been ‌proposed ​for any ‌of ​them, and ‌none would ​be ‌expected — ​ogham ​stones mark ‌graves ‌and boundaries, ‌not ​votive dedications.[1]

​The ‌nearest epigraphic ​witness to ​her ‌name is ​continental ​and exact: ‌the ​altar from ‌Mieussy ​in Haute-Savoie ​dedicated ​to Cathubodua, ​catu-bodwā, 'battle-crow' ‌— ‌the precise ​reverse ‌compound of ​the ​Irish badb ‌catha. ‌The inscription ‌proves ‌that the ​crow-of-battle formation ‌predates ​and outreaches ​the ​Irish literary ‌tradition; ​it remains ​comparative ​evidence, never ‌an ​identification of ​the Irish ​goddess ‌with the ​Gaulish ‌one.[2] The ​epigraphic ‌silence at ​home ‌is characteristic ‌of ​Irish paganism, ​whose cult ​left ​place-names and ‌literature ‌rather than ​stone.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Badb is ​the rare ‌theonym that ‌refuses to ​be a ​metaphor: the ​word for ​the bird ​and the ‌name of the ​goddess are ​one, and ‌the medieval ​writers never ‌felt the ​need to ‌choose between ​them. To ​name her is ​already to ​say what ​she does. ‌Other gods ​have attributes; ‌she has ​a species ‌— the grey ​scald-crow that ‌comes down ​to the ‌field when ​the field ​is finished, ‌and the ‌texts simply ‌ask us to ​understand that ​the bird ‌knew all ​along.[1]

Her ​power is ‌the moment ​before. In ​no surviving ‌text does she ​strike a ‌blow: she ​shrieks over ​the marching ​column, washes ​at the ‌boundary water, ‌settles on ​the pillar-stone — ‌the goddess ​of foreknowledge ​and of ‌aftermath, of ‌everything in ‌a battle ‌except the ​fighting. That ‌is not a ​small dossier ​but a ​strange and ‌exact one: ​she is ​what war ​feels like ‌to those who ​already know ​its end. ​The name ‌keeps the ​question open ‌in every ​grey bird ‌on a ​fence-post: if you ‌could see ‌the end ​of the ​day at ​its beginning, ​what would ‌you do ‌with the ​hours between?
