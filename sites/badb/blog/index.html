The hidden history behind Badb

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Badb is the opposite case, and the more instructive one: a war-goddess so feared that her shriek kills a hundred men in the Táin before a spear is cast, attached to a name so plain that Old Irish itself wrote it in unbroken ASCII — because the name is not a metaphor but a species, the common word for the hooded crow. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, script, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the crow of battle has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII badb.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Badb (badb) — Battle, Death, Crow · Crow, scald-crow — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Battle, Death, Crow". The name's traditional gloss is "Crow, scald-crow", and it is literal: the theonym and the common noun are one word.

Badb is the Irish war-goddess of the crow: one of the daughters of Ernmas beside Macha and the Morrígan, a wife of the war-god Neit, and the voice of the battlefield in the Táin, where her shriek over the marching hosts kills a hundred warriors with fright. She never strikes a blow in any surviving text — she foretells, she panics, and she gathers what the battle leaves.

PuniCodex restores the name as Badb — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Irish writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII badb.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old and Middle Irish as badb (later badhbh), and its first sense is zoological: the scald-crow, the grey-and-black hooded crow of the Irish countryside. The Dictionary of the Irish Language records both registers — the bird and the supernatural battle-being — and the medieval texts exploit the identity deliberately: when the war-goddess is named, the hearer already knows her shape. Her standing phrase is badb catha, 'the crow of battle', used of battlefield terror itself as readily as of the goddess.

The etymology is old Celtic, not Irish alone. Vendryes' LEIA and Matasović's Proto-Celtic dictionary trace the word to bodwā, 'battle, fighting', and the Gaulish altar from Mieussy in Haute-Savoie dedicates to Cathubodua — catu-bodwā, 'battle-crow', the precise reverse compound of the Irish phrase. The crow of battle is inherited Celtic common property: named in stone on the continent, in saga in Ireland.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in the medieval Irish manuscripts as badb, written in the Insular Latin script of the great codices — the same hand that copied the Táin into Lebor na hUidre and the Book of Leinster. The earlier monumental alphabet, ogham, predates the manuscript tradition by centuries, but no ogham stone has ever been read as carrying this theonym — and none would be expected: ogham stones mark graves and boundaries with personal names, not votive dedications to gods.

That detail matters in the usual way: the manuscript spelling is the primary witness, and it is itself plain ASCII. There is no lost letter to rescue, no diacritic to reinstate — the Insular scribes wrote the name exactly as a modern keyboard does, capital aside, and the restoration is a matter of canonical reference rather than recovered orthography.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈbaðβ/ — Old Irish Reconstruction.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: BAH-dhuv — one short syllable, the d softened into the 'th' of 'this', the final b melting into a light v. Modern Irish badhbh keeps the softening, and English ears often hear the word as something like 'bave'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Irish — badb, 'scald-crow', the common noun and the theonym in one word; badb catha, 'crow of battle'
  • Proto-Celtic — *bodwā, 'battle, fighting', the root beneath the name
  • Gaulish — Cathubodua, 'battle-crow', the goddess of the Mieussy altar
  • Modern Irish — badhbh, 'scald-crow, hooded crow', the unbroken living word

The stress falls on the single syllable, as initial stress is the rule in Old Irish. The a is short and open. And the two written stops are lenited in speech — d to the soft 'th' of 'this', b to a bilabial v — though the orthography keeps them whole.

Mythology

Badb's mythology is genealogy, sound, and omen rather than adventure: she is the sister in the list, the voice over the army, the bird on the pillar-stone.

The Daughters of Ernmas (Lebor Gabála Érenn)

The Lebor Gabála Érenn gives the war-goddesses their pedigree: 'Badb and Macha and Anand, of whom are the Paps of Anu in Luachair, were the three daughters of Ernmas the she-farmer.' The verse recension sings the family in another key — 'Badb and Macha, greatness of wealth, Morrígu, springs of craftiness, sources of bitter fighting' — so the tradition itself is of two minds about the third sister. The same book marries her into the war-god's house: Neit son of Indui had two wives, Badb and Nemain, and Cormac's Glossary keeps the marriage on record — Neith, the god of battle among the pagan Gael, and Nemon his wife.

The Shriek over the Hosts (Táin Bó Cúailnge)

When the men of Ireland march under Medb for the brown bull of Cooley, the Táin marks the nights of the hosting with the sound of the war-goddesses: 'Badb and Bé Néit and Némain shrieked above them that night in Gáirech and Irgáirech, so that a hundred of their warriors fell dead of fright.' She does not wound; she announces. The shriek is prophecy delivered as noise — and the Irish phrase for battlefield terror, badb catha, is simply her name at work as a common noun.

The War-Goddess at Mag Tuired (Cath Maige Tuired)

On Samain night before the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the Dagda met the Morrígan at the ford of the Unius, where she stood washing with one foot on either bank; she pledged herself to the Túatha Dé Danann's cause, promising the hearts of the Fomorian host and the blood of their king Indech. After Lugh's sling-stone felled Balor, it was the Morrígan who proclaimed the rout and uttered the prophecy — 'Peace up to heaven, heaven down to earth, earth beneath heaven' — and then its darker twin, the vision of the world's ending. The texts keep the sisters' voices interchangeable; what the manuscript gives to the Morrígan, the tradition hears also in Badb's register.

The Crow on the Pillar-Stone (Aided Con Culainn)

Cú Chulainn's death-tale, Brislech Mór Maige Muirthemni, gives the crow-goddesses the last word. Mortally wounded, Cú Chulainn lashed himself to a pillar-stone to die on his feet, and for three days his enemies would not close in — until the battle-goddess Morrígu came with her sisters in the form of scald-crows and settled on his shoulder. 'That pillar is not wont to be under birds,' said Erc, and only then did the host dare take the hero's head. The living man keeps her off; the dead man is her perch.

The Washer at the Ford (Gaelic tradition)

The medieval texts give the war-goddess her washing-place at the ford of the Unius; the later Gaelic tradition gives the figure her full shape — the washer at the ford, rinsing the blood-stained garments of those about to die in battle. In Scottish Gaelic folklore she is the bean nighe, and the commentators, Mac Cana among them, treat her as the folk continuation of the Badb's own figure. The washing is prophecy, not threat: the doom is already written, and she merely launders the evidence.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Badb is the sparest in the Irish corpus, and the most exact — a species, a sound, a place, a number, and a chore:

  • The scald-crow — the hooded crow, grey and black: her name, her shape, and her retinue on every field
  • The shriek — panic as a weapon: the sound over the hosts that kills men with fear before the spear is thrown
  • The ford — the boundary between bank and bank, living and dead: the water where the war-goddess stands washing
  • The triad — Badb, Macha, Némain: the three of war, one terror in three voices
  • The washing of spoils — the harness and garments of the soon-dead, rinsed in the ford before the battle that will bloody them

Archaeology & Evidence

Badb is a textual goddess: no ogham stone names her, no votive inscription invokes her, and Irish paganism generally left literature rather than dedications. Her manuscript witnesses are exact. The Táin Bó Cúailnge survives in its oldest recension in Lebor na hUidre, the Book of the Dun Cow (c. 1100), and in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster; the Lebor Gabála rests on the Book of Leinster and its sister codices; Cath Maige Tuired survives uniquely in the sixteenth-century British Library MS Harley 5280, the basis of Gray's edition. The nearest epigraphic witness is continental: the altar from Mieussy in Haute-Savoie dedicating to Cathubodua, 'battle-crow', is the standing proof that the crow-of-battle compound is old Celtic and not an Irish literary invention.

Realm & Domain

Badb's domain is Battle, Death, Crow — and the sources earn every term of it.

Battle

She never fights, and the texts are exact about it: her weapon is the voice. At Gáirech and Irgáirech her shriek over the hosts kills a hundred warriors with fright — the battle's true opening, fought before a spear is cast.

Death

She is the reader of the doomed: the war-goddess washing at the ford on the eve of Mag Tuired, the scald-crows settling on Cú Chulainn's pillar-stone. Death is not her doing; it is her knowledge.

Crow

Her name is the animal. The theonym refuses metaphor — the goddess has a species, and the grey bird on the battlefield needed no gloss for any Irish hearer.

The Moment Before

The connective tissue of all three is foreknowledge: shriek, washing, and settling are the same act in three scenes — the end of the day, seen at its beginning.

Across Cultures

The clearest cross-border witness is Gaulish, and it is exact: the Mieussy altar's Cathubodua is catu-bodwā, 'battle-crow' — the precise reverse compound of Irish badb catha. The Welsh corpus, by contrast, is silent on her: no figure of the Mabinogi continues the name, and the Brythonic silence is recorded rather than repaired. Typological comparison has set her beside the Norse valkyries, the raven-feeding choosers of the slain; the handbooks note the parallel without claiming descent. Within the corpus her closest kin are her own sisters, the Mórrígan and Macha, and the war she prophesied binds her to the Dagda, Lugh, and Nuadu.

Cultural Legacy

The crow of battle rode the Celtic revival in English dress. W. M. Hennessy's 'The Ancient Irish Goddess of War' (1870) gathered the Morrígan material for scholars, and Lady Gregory's Gods and Fighting Men (1904) carried Cath Maige Tuired and its war-goddesses to a wide reading public, crow-form and prophecy complete. Modern fantasy and games have made the war-goddess's crow-shape one of the stock images of the genre, and contemporary pagan practice honors Badb among the three of the Morrígan. In Irish the word never needed reviving — badhbh has meant the hooded crow all along — and the Scottish bean nighe kept her folk shape washing at the ford deep into the era of the great folklore collections.

A Meditation

Badb is the rare theonym that refuses to be a metaphor: the word for the bird and the name of the goddess are one, and the medieval writers never felt the need to choose between them. Other gods have attributes; she has a species. And her power is the moment before: in no surviving text does she strike a blow. She shrieks over the marching column, washes at the boundary water, settles on the pillar-stone — the goddess of foreknowledge and of aftermath, of everything in a battle except the fighting. The name keeps the question open in every grey bird on a fence-post: if you could see the end of the day at its beginning, what would you do with the hours between?

The Unicode Restoration

Badb is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Irish badb carries none of these. The manuscripts write four plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the goddess; it is an honest report about her name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Badb mean? "Crow, scald-crow" — and it is literal: the theonym is the Old Irish common noun for the hooded crow. Her standing phrase is badb catha, 'the crow of battle', and the etymology runs to Proto-Celtic bodwā, 'battle, fighting'.

Which tradition does Badb belong to? Badb is catalogued in the Celtic pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon — the Irish war-goddess complex of the Táin, the Lebor Gabála, and Cath Maige Tuired.

Why is Badb classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Old Irish form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the goddess's standing is beside the point.

Is badb.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

How does Badb appear in the sources? By voice and shape rather than deed: shrieking over Medb's hosts in the Táin until a hundred warriors die of fright; named a daughter of Ernmas and a wife of the war-god Neit in the Lebor Gabála; and settling as a scald-crow on Cú Chulainn's pillar-stone in the hero's death-tale.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Badb the goddess, badhbh the living Irish word for the hooded crow, Cathubodua the Gaulish altar-name, badb.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps them sorted.
