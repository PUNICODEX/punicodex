Auðhumla — the one true spelling, and the forms that fail it

Court is in session. In the matter of Auðhumla, the register hears the evidence — the attestation, the marks, and the forms that stand accused.

At a Glance

The Charge

That the name Auðhumla is routinely written in forms that move, drop, or invent its marks — and that each such form teaches the reader a different, lesser name. The register's task is to separate the honest fallback from the outright false.

The Defense of the Canonical Form

The attested form is ᛅᚢᚦᚢᛘᛚᛅ, from the tradition's own record. The canonical transliteration is Auðhumla.

No diacritics are required here: the canonical form is the exactness of the spelling itself, the form normalized Old Norse spelling — þ, ð, ǫ, æ and length marks preserved as the editions print them demands.

The Character Witness

The plain form audhumla is not false — it is a fallback, and the register is exact about what that means. In systems that cannot carry diacritics (legacy databases, older tooling, parts of the address-bar pipeline) the flattened form is the accepted vehicle, and the rulebook names it explicitly as the final rung of the fallback hierarchy: full restoration first, circumflex where attested, macron-only by the academic standard, an attested variant, and only then plain ASCII.

What the fallback status forbids is promotion: audhumla may carry the name where marks are impossible, but it may not represent the name where marks are possible. A fallback is a vehicle, never the primary.

Cross-Examination

The witness Audhumla

Where it comes from: the publisher compromise — older books and hurried websites that print the "English equivalent" as if it were the name itself. Why it cannot stand: every substitution here (ð→d) is a romanization of convenience, not an attested spelling — it teaches the reader to guess, not to read.

The witness Auþhumla

Where it comes from: the near-miss — a form that looks right at a glance and circulates on wikis and forums. Why it cannot stand: eth and thorn are not interchangeable: one is voiced, one is not.

The witness audumla

Where it comes from: the anglicized respelling — the name written the way English guesses it sounds. Why it cannot stand: rewrites attested consonant clusters to fit English spelling habits; every cluster simplified this way erases a letter the sources write.

Mitigating Circumstances

No alternate form of this name carries documented scholarly support in the lexicon's record. The convention here — normalized Old Norse spelling — þ, ð, ǫ, æ and length marks preserved as the editions print them — produces exactly one defensible transliteration: Auðhumla. Forms that differ from it are not contested; they are simply wrong.

The Convention's Own History

Old Norse orthography is a primary source in itself: þ (thorn) and ð (eth) are letters the language used natively, not transliteration marks — English once had them too, and lost them. ǫ (o with ogonek) is a nasal vowel with its own saga-era history, and normalized Old Norse spelling preserves it because the Eddas' editors do. To write th for þ or o for ǫ is not modernization; it is a different spelling of a different word. The Eddic poems survive in one manuscript tradition each — the register spells the names the way those manuscripts do.

Registration, Honestly

Three facts govern every domain this project registers. One: IDNA 2008 welcomes the marks — punycode encodes them legally and browsers show them faithfully. Two: welcome is not availability — registrars hold partial inventory, so the ideal form of a name may exist in the protocol yet not in the shop window today. Three: typeability is a separate virtue — a form no one can type on a phone is a form no one will visit, however canonical.

Hence the hierarchy the rulebook enforces: take the most complete form that is both attested and actually registrable, and never accept a wrong mark for the sake of availability. The wrong accent is not a rung on that ladder; it is off the ladder entirely.

The Verdict

So the register finds: Auðhumla, canonical; audhumla, a sanctioned fallback; the contested forms, recorded with their support; the false forms, ruled against with their violations named. Every line of that finding cites its evidence, and the evidence is open to any reader — the register asks to be checked, not trusted. The temple keeps the canonical form at /audhumla/.
