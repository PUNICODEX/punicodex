01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Wyrd (wyrd) ​— Fate, Destiny ‌· Fate ‌(cognate with Urðr) ​— is the ​Old English word ​for what happens: ​fate, destiny, ​the shape of ‌events, built on ​the verb weorðan ​'to become'. It ‌is not ​a goddess but ‌a noun, and ​its dossier is ‌a set of ​sentences: 'Wyrd ​bið ful ārǣd', ​says the Wanderer ​— fate is ​wholly fixed; 'Gǣð ‌ā wyrd ​swā hīo sceal', ‌says Beowulf on ​the threshold of ‌every fight — ​fate goes ‌always as she ​must; and the ‌same poet grants ​the exception that ​governs his ‌hero's career, 'Wyrd ‌oft nereð unfǣgne ‌eorl, þonne his ​ellen dēah' — ​fate often ‌spares the undoomed ​man when his ​courage avails.[1]

Bosworth–Toller ‌record the full ​semantic range ​— event, fate, ‌destiny, and a ​half-personified power glossing ‌Latin fatum, ​fortuna, even the ​Parcae — and ​Timmer's classic study ​shows how the ‌word hovers ‌between process and ​person without ever ‌becoming a culted ​goddess.[2] Its exact ​Old Norse ‌cognate is not ‌a noun but ‌a name: Urðr, ‌eldest of the ​three Norns ‌who lay down ​laws beside Verðandi ​and Skuld at ​the well beneath ‌Yggdrasill.[3]

PuniCodex ​restores the name ​as Wyrd — ​identical to the ‌ASCII input, because ​normalized Old ​English writes no ​diacritic in this ‌word. The mechanical ​rule therefore places ‌it in ​Tier 2: nothing ‌distinctive is lost ​in transit, a ‌statement about orthography ‌and never ​about the concept. ​The temple is ​domain-less: the plain-ASCII ​wyrd.com lies outside ‌the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The word ​is attested ‌in Old ‌English as ​wyrd, a feminine ​noun whose ​first sense ​is simply 'what ​happens, event', ‌and whose ​developed senses ​are 'fate, destiny, ‌fortune' — ​with a ‌personified stratum the ​dictionaries mark ‌with capitals: ​Wyrd, the ​power that determines ​events.[1] Its ​etymology is ​transparent: it is ‌the verbal ​abstract of ‌weorðan 'to ​become, to happen' ‌(Gothic waírþan, ​Old Norse ‌verða, Old High ​German werdan), ‌from Proto-Germanic ​*werþaną — ​'becoming' nominalized. Fate, ‌in this ‌word, is ‌the past tense ​of the ​world.

The ‌manuscript spelling ​deserves note: Anglo-Saxon ​bookhand writes ‌the word ​ƿyrd with wynn, ​the runic ‌letter borrowed ​into the ‌Latin alphabet for ​the /w/ ​sound; normalized ​editions print w. ​The Unicode ‌restoration Wyrd ‌therefore preserves ​everything the manuscripts' ‌normalized form ​contains, and ​the mechanical rule ‌classifies the ‌name Tier ‌2 — ‌an orthographic fact, ​not a ‌judgment on ​the concept.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • ‌w → ​W — Same, ​capitalized
  • ​y → ‌y — Same

​- r ​→ r ​— Same
‌- d → ​d — ‌Same

The ​project holds no ‌domain for ​this name. ‌The plain-ASCII ‌wyrd.com lies outside ​the collection ​and is ​watched for release; ​this temple ‌claims nothing ‌it does ​not hold.[3]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the word ‌is /ˈwyrd/ ​— Old ​English Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- W- ​— Labial-velar ‌approximant [w], ​written wynn ​(ƿ) in ‌the manuscripts, voiced ​and plain ‌as in ​modern 'water'
‌- -y- ​— Short ​close front rounded ​[y], the ​vowel English ​later unrounded: ‌shape the ​lips for 'oo' ‌while saying ​'ee', as ‌in German ​ü

  • ‌-rd — ​Alveolar trill or ‌tap [r], ​fully sounded ​even before ‌the consonant, ‌closing on ‌a released [d]

​For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest ​approximation is: ​'WÜRD' — ‌one syllable, the ​ü rounded ​as in ‌German 'Würde', ​the r ‌rolled, the ​d stopped hard.

​Kindred and ​historical forms ​of the ‌word:

  • ‌Old English ​— wyrd, feminine ‌noun, 'that ​which happens, ​fate, destiny'

‌- Anglo-Saxon ‌Futhorc — ‌ᚹᚣᚱᛞ (w-ȳ-r-d), the ‌runic rendering; ​unattested in ‌surviving inscriptions
​- Old ​Norse — ​Urðr, the exact ‌cognate, eldest ​of the ​three Norns[2]
​- Modern ‌English — weird, ​the fate-noun ​worn down ​to an ‌adjective of ​strangeness

Wyrd ‌is Tier 2 ​because the ‌restoration preserves ​nothing the ‌ASCII form ‌loses: no ​diacritic, no distinctive ​letter, no ​vowel length. ​English before ‌and after ‌writes the ​word identically.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The word ​is preserved ‌in Anglo-Saxon ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet with ​the runic ​letter wynn: ​ƿyrd — ‌Old English ​(West Germanic), ​attested in ‌codices of ​the tenth ‌and eleventh ​centuries recording ‌verse and ​prose centuries ​older in ​composition.[2] The ​indigenous writing ​system of ‌pre-Viking England ​was the ‌Anglo-Saxon futhorc; ​in that ‌row the ​word would ‌run ᚹᚣᚱᛞ ​(w-ȳ-r-d) — a ‌normalized reconstruction, ​since no ​runic inscription ‌of the ‌word survives, ‌and the ​manuscript form ​remains the ‌primary witness.[1]

​The scholarly ​rendering proceeds ‌step by ​step:

  • ​The word ‌is a ​simplex, not ‌a compound: ​four letters, ​one syllable, ​the verbal ​abstract of ‌weorðan 'to ‌become'.
  • ​Old English ‌orthography needs ​no diacritic ​here: the ‌vowel y ‌is a ‌plain letter of ‌the Anglo-Saxon ​alphabet, not ‌a marked ​u.
  • ​The manuscripts ​write wynn ‌(ƿ), borrowed ​from the ​runes; normalized ​editions print ‌w — ​the only ​regular substitution ​between bookhand ‌and modern ​type.
  • ‌The Unicode ​restoration Wyrd ‌is therefore ​identical to ‌the ASCII ‌form plus ​its capital ​— the ​restoration is ​a matter ‌of canonical ‌reference, not ​rescued orthography.[2]

Sources

  1. Page, R. I., An Introduction to English Runes, 2nd ed. (Woodbridge: Boydell, 1999).
  2. Bosworth–Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1898), s.v. wyrd; Hogg, Richard M., An Introduction to Old English (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2002).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Wyrd is ​catalogued under ‌Fate, Destiny ‌— and ​the pairing ​is exact: the ​word is ​not a ​being with ‌a sphere ​of influence but ​the English ‌language's own ​name for ‌the sphere ​itself.[1]

Fate

‌The developed ​sense, 'that ​which is ​fated to ​happen': the unalterable ​course the ‌Wanderer calls ​wholly fixed ‌and Maxims II ​ranks strongest ‌of all ​forces. This ‌is wyrd ​as decree — ‌impersonal, grammatical, ​absolute.[1]

​Destiny

The ‌personified half ‌of the word: ‌the manuscripts ​capitalize Wyrd ​where the ‌glosses render ​Latin fatum and ​fortuna, and ‌the pronoun ​'she' (hīo) ​of the ‌feminine noun lets ​the poets ‌half-stage a ​goddess who ​is never ​named, never housed, ​never worshipped.[2]

‌### Becoming

‌The root ​sense: from ‌weorðan, 'to become'. ​Before wyrd ​is a ‌power it ‌is a ‌process — the ‌shape events ​take as ‌they come ​about — ​and that verbal ​past is ‌what distinguishes ​the English ​word from the ​spindle-goddesses of ‌the Mediterranean.[1]

​### Providence

​The Christian ​re-reading: Alfred's Boethius ‌keeps the ​word and ‌subordinates it, ​making wyrd ‌God's instrument — ​providence as ‌felt from ‌below — ​so that ​the pagan noun ​of unalterable ​event survives ‌inside a ‌theology of ​divine forethought.[2]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Wyrd is ‌lexical rather ​than cultic ​— no ​temple regalia ​exists ​for a ‌common noun, ​but the ​poems fixed ‌around the ​word a ‌small ​set of ‌figures no ​discussion can ​omit:

  • ​The decree ​(wyrda ​gesceaft) — ‌the Wanderer's ​'shaping of ‌fates' that ​turns the ‌world under ​heaven: ‌fate as ​ordinance written ‌into things, ​not accident[1]

​- The ‌fixed resolve ‌(ful ‌ārǣd) — ​'fate is ​wholly determined', ‌the exile's ​five-word verdict: ​the hardest ‌consolation ​Old English ​knows[1]

  • ‌The spared ​undoomed man ‌(unfǣgne eorl) ​— ​Beowulf's exception, ​'fate often ​spares the ‌undoomed man ‌when his ​courage avails': ‌the ​loophole that ​makes heroism ‌rational[2]
  • ‌The wheel ‌declined — ‌Alfred's Boethius ​keeps ‌the word ​but strips ​Fortune's wheel ​of its ‌independence: fate ​re-founded as ​God's ​instrument, the ‌nave still ​while the ​rim turns[3]

​- The ‌well ​of Urðr ‌— the ​northern mirror: ‌the norn ​at the ‌spring beneath ‌Yggdrasill ​where the ​Æsir hold ​their judgements, ​the word's ‌exact cognate ‌given a ​face[3]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Wyrd has no mythology ​in the narrative sense — no ‌birth, no hall, no kin — ‌because it is not a figure but ​the Old English word for fate ​itself. What it has is a ​dossier of sentences, the moments where the ​poets reach for the word and ​the whole Anglo-Saxon theory of destiny ‌shows through.

The Wanderer: Fate Is ​Wholly Fixed (Wanderer 5, 106–107)

The ​Exeter Book's exile opens his elegy ‌with the coldest sentence in Old English: ​'Wyrd bið ful ārǣd' — fate ‌is wholly fixed, fully resolved, past ​appeal. He has buried his lord, crossed ‌the winter seas, and found no ​hall to take him in; wyrd ​is the name he gives the weight ​of it. Near the poem's end ​the word returns in its grandest ​frame: 'onwendeð wyrda gesceaft weoruld under heofonum' ‌— the decree of fates turns ​the world under heaven. The poem ‌gives Wyrd no face and no spindle; ​its consolation is not that fate ‌can be moved but that God ​alone is the fastness when her turning ‌is done.[1]

Beowulf: Fate Goes ​as She Must (Beowulf 455, 572–573)

‌Beowulf speaks the word at every threshold. ​Before Grendel: 'Gǣð ā wyrd swā ​hīo sceal' — fate goes always ‌as she must. After the mere: 'Wyrd ‌oft nereð unfǣgne eorl, þonne his ‌ellen dēah' — fate often spares ​the undoomed man when his courage avails. ​The two sentences are the poem's ‌whole theology: doom is real for ​the doomed (fǣge), but for the undoomed, ​courage and fate divide the work. ‌When the dragon comes, the old ​king feels himself fǣge, and wyrd no ​longer negotiates.[2]

The Proverb Poems: ‌Fate Is Strongest (Maxims II 5; ​Solomon and Saturn)

The gnomic tradition ‌keeps the word's power in its bluntest ​form. Maxims II ranks her first ​among forces — 'Wyrd byð swīðost', ​fate is strongest, ahead of winter's cold. ​Solomon and Saturn gives Saturn the ‌matching proverb: fate is hard to ‌turn, and neither wealth nor kin will ​turn her. This is the word's ‌working sense outside the heroic hall: ​the unalterable course of what happens.[3]

​Alfred's Boethius: The Christian Taming (Consolation, ‌OE version)

When Alfred's circle translated ‌Boethius, Fortuna had to be rendered — ‌and the native word took her ‌place. Wyrd translates Fortuna throughout the ​Old English Consolation, but the translation is ‌a conquest: wyrd is declared God's ​work and instrument, providence as experienced ​from below, the wheel's rim turning while ​the nave — God — stands ‌still. The pagan noun of unalterable ​event is retained, baptized, and set to ​work inside Christian providence; every later ​English use of wyrd and weird ‌lives downstream of this choice.[4]

The ​Northern Sister: Urðr (Völuspá 19–20; Gylfaginning ​15–16)

The Old Norse cognate is ​not a common noun but a name: ‌Urðr, 'what has become', eldest of ​the three Norns. Völuspá sets the ‌well beneath Yggdrasill, and from it come ​three maidens, deep in wisdom: Urðr ‌one is called, Verðandi the second, ​Skuld the third; they laid down laws ‌and allotted life to the children ‌of men. Snorri adds the daily ​rite: the Norns draw water from Urðr's ​well and pour it with the ​well's clay over the tree's roots ​so its branches do not wither. England ‌kept the word a noun; Iceland ‌made it a person — one ​inheritance in two grammars.[5]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Wyrd's ​learned afterlife ‌begins as ‌translation. ​The glosses ​recorded by ​Bosworth–Toller put ​the ​word in ‌place of ​Latin ​fatum, fortuna, ‌and even ​the ‌Parcae — ​the three ‌spinning ​Fates — ​and Alfred's ​Boethius makes ​the ​equation systematic, ‌substituting wyrd ​for ‌Fortuna while ​denying Fortune ‌her ​independence. The ‌comparison with ​the ‌Greek Moirai ​— Klotho ​spinning, Lachesis ‌measuring, ‌Atropos cutting ‌— is ​therefore ​as old ‌as the ​word's ​manuscript life.[1]

‌But Timmer's ​classic ​study sounds ‌the necessary ​caution: Old ‌English ​wyrd is ​fundamentally the ​event-word, ​'what happens', ‌and its ‌personification ​is grammatical ‌and poetic, ​never ​cultic — ‌no spindle, ‌no altar, ‌no ‌priesthood is ​attested anywhere ‌in ​the English ​record.[1] The ​honest ‌alignment is ​structural and ​linguistic: ​with the ‌Norns of ​Völuspá, who ​lay ​down laws ‌and allot ​life, ‌and above ​all with ‌Urðr ​herself — ‌the exact ‌cognate, ​the past ​tense of ​becoming enthroned ​beside ‌Verðandi and ‌Skuld beneath ​Yggdrasill.[2]

Sources

  1. Timmer, B. J., 'Wyrd in Anglo-Saxon Prose and Poetry', Neophilologus 26 (1941); Bosworth–Toller, An Anglo-Saxon Dictionary (1898), s.v. wyrd.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Urðr, Norns; Völuspá 19–20, trans. Larrington (2014).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

The ​word's modern ‌career is ‌the strangest ​semantic journey ​in English. ​In medieval ​Scots and ​northern usage ‌'weird' stayed ​a noun ​of ‌fate — ​to 'dree ‌one's weird' ​is to ‌endure one's ​destiny — ​and the ​prophetic women ​of Holinshed's ​chronicle were ‌'the ​weird sisters, ‌that is ​(as ye ‌would say) ​the goddesses ‌of destinie'. ​Shakespeare took ‌the phrase ​into Macbeth; ​the sisters' ‌supernatural ‌strangeness rubbed ‌off on ​the word, ​and by ‌the nineteenth ​century 'weird' ​meant the ‌uncanny, then ​merely the ​odd: the ‌noun ​of absolute ‌fate worn ​down to ​an adjective ​of mild ​strangeness.[1]

Modern ‌fantasy literature ‌and gaming ​revived 'wyrd' ‌as a ​deliberate ​archaism for ‌deep, woven ‌fate; runic ‌revivalism added ‌the so-called ​'wyrd rune', ‌a blank ​stone invented ​in the ​1980s with ‌no ​medieval attestation ​— a ​modern ornament ‌the inscriptions ​do not ​know.[2] Through ​all of ‌it the ​old word ‌keeps its ​dignity ‌in the ​Exeter Book ‌and Beowulf, ‌where it ​still means ​what it ​meant: what ​happens, wholly ‌fixed, and ‌faced with ​courage.

Sources

  1. Holinshed, Raphael, Chronicles of England, Scotland, and Ireland (1577), s.v. the weird sisters; Shakespeare, Macbeth (1623 folio); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. weird, n. and adj.
  2. Page, R. I., An Introduction to English Runes, 2nd ed. (Woodbridge: Boydell, 1999), on the attested runic row and modern inventions.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Wyrd ​is a ‌word of ‌manuscripts, not ​monuments: ​no runic ​inscription carves ​it, ​no amulet ‌invokes it, ​and no ​material ‌image can ​be identified ‌with a ​personified ‌Wyrd.[2] Its ​witnesses are ​codices. ​The Exeter ​Book (Exeter ​Cathedral Library ‌MS ​3501, written ‌c. 975) ​preserves The ‌Wanderer ​and Maxims ‌II; the ​Nowell ‌Codex (British ​Library, Cotton ​Vitellius A.xv, ‌c. ‌1000), scorched ‌in the ​Ashburnham House ​fire ‌of 1731, ​alone preserves ​Beowulf; ‌the homiletic ​prose where ​wyrd is ‌debated ​survives in ‌the Vercelli ​Book and ​the ​great Corpus ​and CCCC ‌collections.[1]

‌The Anglo-Saxon ​futhorc is ‌itself materially ​attested ​— the ‌ninth-century Thames ‌scramasax bears ‌the ‌full inscribed ​row — ‌and ​in that ​row the ​word would ‌run ​ᚹᚣᚱᛞ (w-ȳ-r-d), ​though no ​surviving carving ‌spells ​it.[2] The ​bookhand's wynn ​(ƿ), ‌borrowed from ​the runes ‌into the ​Latin ‌alphabet for ​the /w/ ‌sound, is ‌the ​last material ​trace of ​the ​runic world ‌inside the ‌word's own ​spelling.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never meets ‌the word ​wyrd — ​that word ​is English ​— ​but it ‌stages its ​exact cognate ​as a ‌person. Völuspá, ​in its ‌survey ​of the ‌world's frame, ​sets the ​ash Yggdrasill ​above the ​well, ​and then: ‌'Þaðan koma ​meyjar, margs ‌vitandi, þrjár ​ór þeim ‌sal er ​und ‌þolli stendr: ​Urð hétu ‌eina, aðra ​Verðandi, Skuld ​ina þriðju' ‌— thence ‌come ‌maidens, deep ​in wisdom, ​three from ‌the hall ​beneath the ​tree: one ‌is ​called Urðr, ​the second ‌Verðandi, Skuld ​the third. ‌They carved ​on ​wood, they ​laid down ​laws, they ‌allotted life ‌to the ​children of ‌men, ​fates to ​be told.[1]

‌The grammar ‌is the ‌whole point. ‌Urðr is ​the ‌past participle ​of verða, ​'to become' ​— what ‌has become ​— the ​same ​derivation as ‌the English ​wyrd from ​weorðan. The ​Edda's norn ‌is ​the English ‌noun's sister, ​personified at ‌the well ​where the ‌Æsir hold ‌their ​judgements: the ​corpus's nearest ​portrait of ​the power ‌the English ‌poets left ​nameless.[1]

Sources

  1. Völuspá 19–20 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014); de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. Urðr, verða.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Snorri ​gives the ‌cognate her ‌dwelling ​and her ​daily work. ​Gylfaginning ​15 places ​the well ‌— ​Urðarbrunnr, the ​well ‌of Urðr ​— beneath ‌the ​third root ‌of Yggdrasill, ​holy ​beyond measure, ​where the ​gods ​have their ‌judgement-seat and ​ride ‌up daily ​over Bifrǫst. ‌Gylfaginning ​16 brings ‌the ​maidens: the ‌Norns who ​shape ​men's lives ‌dwell by ‌the ‌well, and ​their names ​are ‌Urðr, Verðandi, ​and Skuld; ​they ‌draw water ​from the ​spring ‌and with ​it ‌the clay ​that lies ​about ​it, and ​pour it ‌over ‌the ash's ​roots so ‌its ​branches do ​not rot ‌or ‌wither.[1]

Snorri's ‌ordering is ‌deliberate: ​the past ‌(Urðr), ​the becoming ​(Verðandi), the ​owed ‌(Skuld) — ​tense made ​into ​persons. The ‌norn-name lists ​(Norna ​heiti) preserved ​with Skáldskaparmál ‌keep ​Urðr first ‌among the ​Norns, ‌the same ​bureaucratic ‌fidelity the ‌English dictionaries ​show ​when they ​capitalize Wyrd: ​two ‌scribal cultures ‌registering one ​inheritance.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 15–16, trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur (Norna heiti), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic record ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of the ​word ​wyrd. No ​Anglo-Saxon ​inscription carves ‌it, no ​rune-stone ​or amulet ‌invokes ​a personified ‌Wyrd, ​and the ‌famous 'wyrd ​rune' ​of modern ​esotericism ​— the ​blank ‌stone — ​is a ‌twentieth-century ​invention with ‌no ​medieval witness; ‌the historical ​rows ‌know no ​blank ​rune.[1]

What ‌can ‌be said ‌runologically is ​the ​rendering itself. ‌The ​Anglo-Saxon futhorc ​possesses ‌exactly the ​letters the ​word ‌needs: wynn ​ᚹ, ‌ȳr ᚣ, ​rað ᚱ, ​dæg ​ᛞ — ​the ‌word would ‌have ​been carved ‌ᚹᚣᚱᛞ (w-ȳ-r-d), ​and ​the row ‌is ‌materially attested ‌by ‌the ninth-century ​Thames scramasax, ‌which ​bears the ​full ​futhorc in ‌order.[1] The ​runes ​also survive ​inside ‌the word's ​own ​manuscript spelling: ​bookhand wynn ‌(ƿ) ​is a ‌rune ​borrowed into ‌the ​Latin alphabet, ‌so every ‌medieval ​copy of ​'ƿyrd' ​carries the ​runic world ‌in ‌its first ​letter.[2]

Sources

  1. Page, R. I., An Introduction to English Runes, 2nd ed. (Woodbridge: Boydell, 1999), on the futhorc row, the Thames scramasax, and unattested modern additions.
  2. Hogg, Richard M., An Introduction to Old English (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2002), on wynn in the bookhand.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the poems, ‌Wyrd's medieval ‌afterlife is ​prose — ​translation, homily, ​and chronicle. ​The ​decisive document ‌is the ​Old English ​Boethius, where ‌Alfred's circle ​renders Boethius's ‌Fortuna as ​wyrd ‌throughout and ​re-founds her: ​fate is ​God's work, ​providence as ​it is ‌felt ​from below, ‌the wheel's ​turning rim ‌while the ​nave stands ‌still. The ​translation ‌keeps the ​pagan noun ​and baptizes ‌it — ‌the single ‌most consequential ​act in ​the ‌word's history.[1]

​The homilists ​resist from ‌the other ​side. Ælfric ​warns his ‌congregations ​against attributing ‌events to ​fate, insisting ​that things ​come about ​by God's ‌ordinance, ‌not by ​any power ‌called wyrd ​— the ​sermon tradition's ‌running argument ‌with a ‌word ‌that would ​not lie ‌down.[2] Four ​centuries later ​the chronicle ​tradition gives ‌the ​word its ​second life: ​Holinshed's prophetic ‌women are ​'the weird ​sisters, that ​is ‌(as ye ​would say) ‌the goddesses ​of destinie', ‌and from ​that phrase ‌— through ‌one ​Scottish play ​— the ​fate-noun began ​its long ‌slide into ‌the modern ​adjective.[3]

Sources

  1. King Alfred's Old English Version of Boethius, De consolatione philosophiae, ed. Malcolm Godden and Susan Irvine (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2009).
  2. Ælfric, De Auguriis (Lives of Saints XVII), ed. W. W. Skeat, Ælfric's Lives of Saints, EETS o.s. 76, 82 (London, 1881–1885); Timmer, B. J., 'Wyrd in Anglo-Saxon Prose and Poetry', Neophilologus 26 (1941).
  3. Holinshed, Raphael, Chronicles of England, Scotland, and Ireland (1577); Shakespeare, Macbeth (1623 folio); Oxford English Dictionary, s.v. weird.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Wyrd ​is the ‌rare fate-word ‌that never ​pretends to ​be a ​person. The ​Greeks spun ​fate ‌into three ​women with ​shears; the ‌Icelanders gave ​her a ‌name and ​a well; ‌the ​English kept ​her a ​common noun ​— the ​shape of ‌what happens, ​grammatically feminine ‌by ​accident of ‌declension, addressed ​by no ‌prayer and ​moved by ‌no sacrifice.[1]

​And yet ​the ‌Beowulf poet ‌finds the ‌loophole that ​keeps the ​word honest: ‌fate often ​spares the ​undoomed ‌man when ​his courage ​avails. Doom ‌is real, ​but it ‌is not ​everything; between ​the ​fixed decree ​and the ‌falling sword ‌there is ​a space ‌the size ​of a ​man's ‌ellen, his ‌courage, and ‌in that ‌space the ​whole heroic ‌ethic of ​the poem ​lives.[1] ​The Wanderer ‌ends where ​Alfred ends: ​the decree ​turns the ‌world under ​heaven, and ​the ​one fastness ‌is not ​in the ‌turning but ​above it.[2] ‌A word ​for fate ‌that ‌leaves room ​for courage ​and for ​God — ​no tradition ‌stated that ‌settlement more ​economically.

Sources

  1. Beowulf 572–573, ed. Fulk, Bjork, and Niles, Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th ed. (Toronto, 2008).
  2. The Wanderer 5 and 106–115, ed. Krapp and Dobbie, The Exeter Book, ASPR III (1936).
