01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Sindri (sindri) ​— Dwarf Smith ‌· Sparkling ‌— is the ​dwarf at the ​anvil in the ​Prose Edda's great ​forge-scene: brother ​of Brokkr, who ‌works the bellows ​while Sindri works ​the fire, and ‌maker of ​the gods' three ‌greatest treasures — ​the golden-bristled boar ‌Gullinbursti for Freyr, ​the ring ​Draupnir for Óðinn, ​and the short-handled ​hammer Mjǫllnir for ​Þórr — cast ‌in three ​heats to win ‌Loki's wagered head.[1] ​The verse tradition ‌knows a grander ​figure: Völuspá ‌counts a hall ​of gold standing ‌north on Niðavellir, ​belonging to the ​kindred of ‌Sindri — a ‌dwarf chieftain with ‌a family and ​a golden house, ​not merely ‌a wagered-against craftsman.[2]

​The name is ​the trade. Old ‌Norse sindra means ​'to sparkle, ​throw off sparks', ‌and sindr is ​the slag and ‌spark of ​the forge — ​cognate with Old ​English sinder and ​the modern English ‌'cinder'. The ‌smith is named ​for what his ‌fire throws off.[3] ​Part of the ​manuscript tradition ‌of Skáldskaparmál names ‌Brokkr's brother Eitri ‌rather than Sindri, ‌and the handbooks ​treat the ‌two as one ​smith under two ​forge-names.

PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Sindri — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, because ​normalized Old Norse ‌writes no diacritic ​in this ​name. The mechanical ​rule therefore places ‌the name in ​Tier 2: nothing ‌distinctive is ​lost in transit, ‌a statement about ​orthography and never ‌about the smith. ‌The temple ​is domain-less: the ​plain-ASCII sindri.com is ​registered to a ​third party outside ‌the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse ‌as Sindri — ​the dwarf's name, ​and in ​modern times an ​unbroken Icelandic male ​given name. ‌Its etymology is ​among the most ​transparent in ‌the northern onomasticon: ​it is built ‌on the ​forge-word sindra, 'to ‌sparkle, to throw ​off sparks', ​with the noun ​sindr, 'slag, sparks ​from the ​smith's fire', beside ‌it. The smith ​is the sparkler ‌— named ​for the bright ‌debris of his ​own work.[1] ‌The Germanic cognates ​run straight into ‌English: Old ​English sinder, 'slag, ​dross of metal', ‌is the ‌same word, and ‌modern English 'cinder' ​is its ​descendant — the ‌cooled spark of ​the forge ​surviving in every ‌fireplace.[2]

The ASCII ​form is ​not a fallback ‌here but the ​form itself: normalized ‌Old Norse ​writes six plain ​Latin letters and ​a capital. ​The Unicode restoration ‌Sindri therefore preserves ‌nothing the ​ASCII alphabet loses, ‌and the mechanical ​rule classifies ​the name Tier ‌2 — an ‌orthographic fact, ‌not a judgment ‌on the smith.

​The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • ​s → S ​— Same, ​capitalized
  • i ‌→ i — ​Same
  • n ​→ n ​— Same
  • ‌d → d ​— Same

​- r → ​r — Same
‌- i ​→ i — ‌Same

The project ​holds no ‌domain for this ​name. The plain-ASCII ‌sindri.com is ‌registered to a ​third party outside ​the collection ​and is watched ​for release; this ‌temple claims ‌nothing it does ​not hold.[3]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈsin.dri/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- Si- ​— Voiceless ‌alveolar sibilant ​[s] and short ​close front ‌unrounded [i], ​stressed on ‌the first ​syllable as ‌in every Old ​Norse word
​- -ndri ​— Nasal ​[n] plus ​the epenthetic stop ‌[d] that ​Old Norse ‌grew between ​n and ‌r, then trilled ​or tapped ‌[r], and ​a final ‌short [i], ​fully sounded rather ​than reduced ‌to schwa

‌For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ​is: 'SIN-dri' ‌— two ​short beats, ​the weight ‌on the first, ​the d ​clicked crisply ‌between the ​n and ‌the r ​as in 'laundry' ​said fast.

​Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the ‌name:

  • Old ​Norse — ‌Sindri, brother ​of Brokkr, ​maker of ‌Gullinbursti, Draupnir, and ‌Mjǫllnir
  • ‌Younger Futhark ‌— ᛋᛁᚾᛏᚱᛁ ​(s-i-n-t-r-i), the ‌Viking-Age runic rendering

​- Manuscript ​variant — ​Eitri, the ‌name part ​of the Skáldskaparmál ​tradition gives ​the same ‌smith[2]

  • ​Modern Icelandic ​— Sindri, an ​unbroken living ‌male given ​name

Sindri ‌is Tier ​2 because ‌the restoration preserves ​nothing the ‌ASCII form ‌loses: no ​diacritic, no ​distinctive letter, no ​vowel length. ​English and ‌Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Sindri, Eitri.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in ​the Latin alphabet ​as Sindri ​— Old Norse ​(North Germanic), ​attested 13th c. ‌CE in ​the Codex Regius ​of the ‌Prose Edda and ​related codices, ‌recording material centuries ​older.[1] Before ‌the Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ​names were carved ​in the ​Younger Futhark; no ​runic attestation ‌of this theonym ​survives, so ‌the manuscript form ​is the ‌primary witness. The ​runic rendering ‌is ᛋᛁᚾᛏᚱᛁ (s-i-n-t-r-i), ​a normalized ‌phonetic reconstruction.

The ​scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • ‌The name is ‌a simplex, ​not a compound: ​six letters, ‌two syllables, built ​on the ​forge-word sindra, 'to ‌sparkle'.
  • ​The n-d-r cluster ​of the ‌Latin spelling reflects ​an epenthetic ‌stop grown between ​nasal and ​r; the Younger ​Futhark's undifferentiated ​stop rune ᛏ ‌writes the ‌cluster without distinguishing ​the voicing.

‌- Old Norse ​orthography needs ​no diacritic here: ‌both vowels ‌are short i, ‌and no ‌distinctive letter (þ, ​ð, æ, ‌ǫ) occurs in ​the name.
​- The Unicode ​restoration Sindri ‌is therefore identical ​to the ​ASCII form — ​the restoration ‌is a matter ​of canonical ​reference, not rescued ​orthography.

  • ‌The name's etymology ​— sindra, ‌'to sparkle, throw ​off sparks', ‌beside sindr, 'forge-slag', ​cognate with ‌Old English sinder ‌and modern ​English cinder — ​is registered ​in the lexica, ​and unlike ‌most northern names ‌it is ​not disputed.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. sindra, Sindri; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. sindr, sindra.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Sindri is ​catalogued under ‌Dwarf Smith ‌— and the ​gloss is ​exact: the ​sources give him ​one office, ​one scene, and ‌one product ​line, and ​all three are ‌the forge.[1]

​### The ‌Anvil

Sindri's station ​in Skáldskaparmál ‌35 is ​the forge itself: ​he lays ​the metal, chooses ​the heats, ​and takes ‌the work out ​of the ‌fire, while ​his brother Brokkr ‌holds the ​bellows. The ‌chapter names the ​forge by ‌the pair, but ​the craft ​by the ‌smith alone — ‌'Sindri feared ‌the work ​would fail' is ​the story's ‌one glance ​into a craftsman's ​mind.[1]

‌The Three Treasures

​Gullinbursti for ​Freyr, Draupnir ‌for Óðinn, Mjǫllnir ​for Þórr: ‌one afternoon's ​output, and the ​Æsir's judgment ​that the ​marred third piece ‌is the ‌finest treasure ever ​made. Everything ‌the gods ​trust at Ragnarǫk ​came off ‌his anvil.[1]

‌### The Wager

‌The forge-scene ‌is staked ​on Loki's head, ‌and Sindri's ​victory ends with ​the trickster's ​lips sewn ‌shut with the ​awl-thread Vartari ​— the ​smith who cannot ‌take his ​stake taking ​the voice that ​staked it.[1]

‌### The Golden ​Hall

Völuspá ‌adds what ​Snorri withholds: a ‌hall of ​gold north ‌on Niðavellir belonging ‌to Sindri's ​kindred — ​the craftsman of ​the prose ​tradition revealed as ‌a chieftain ‌of the ​verse one.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Völuspá 37 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Sindri is ‌narrative rather ​than ​cultic — ​the corpus ​gives him ​no ‌temple regalia, ​but fixed ​around his ‌one ​forge-scene and ‌one eddic ​stanza a ‌set ​of objects ​no retelling ​can omit:[1]

​- ​The bellows ‌— the ​wager's condition ‌and ​the forge's ‌heartbeat: the ​one tool ‌whose ​failure, for ‌a single ​instant, marks ​the ‌hammer forever
‌- The ‌fly that ​stung ​— Loki's ‌sabotage in ​miniature: the ​god ‌who cannot ​win fairly ​trying to ‌spoil ​the work, ‌and succeeding ​only at ​the ​handle

  • ​Draupnir — ‌the ring ‌that ​drips eight ‌rings of ​equal weight ​every ‌ninth night: ‌wealth that ‌renews itself, ‌later ​laid on ‌Baldr's pyre ​and sent ​back ​from Hel[2]

‌- Mjǫllnir's ​short handle ​— ​the flaw ‌in perfection: ​the Æsir's ​surest ​defense carrying ‌the permanent ​mark of ‌the ​interrupted bellows-stroke
‌- The ​golden hall ‌at ‌Niðavellir — ​Völuspá's witness: ​the house ​of ​gold of ‌Sindri's kindred ‌in the ​dwarf-fields[3]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Sindri's mythology is ​one long scene in Snorri and ‌one stanza in Völuspá — a ‌forge and a hall.

​The Wager of the Head (Skáldskaparmál ​35)

It begins with a crime: ​Loki, for mischief, cuts off ​all the hair of Sif, Þórr's ​wife. Under Þórr's hands Loki swears ‌to get the svartálfar to ​make hair of gold that will ​grow like living hair, and he ‌goes first to the sons ​of Ívaldi, who make the golden ‌hair and two more treasures — ​the ship Skíðblaðnir and the ‌spear Gungnir. Then, flushed with success, ​Loki goes to the dwarf Brokkr ​and wagers his own head ​that Brokkr's brother Sindri cannot make ​three treasures just as good. The ​bet is the engine of ‌the chapter: a trickster's tongue staked ​against a smith's hands.[1]

‌Three Castings, Three Stings (Skáldskaparmál 35)

​Sindri lays a pigskin in the ‌forge and tells Brokkr to ​blow and never cease; a fly ‌stings Brokkr's hand, but out comes ​Gullinbursti, the boar with bristles ‌of gold. Next gold goes into ​the fire; the fly stings Brokkr's ​neck, harder, but out comes ‌the ring Draupnir. Last, iron — ‌and now Sindri himself fears the ‌work will fail if the ​blowing falters. The fly settles between ​Brokkr's eyes and stings until the ‌blood blinds him; Brokkr sweeps ​it away, the bellows fall silent ​for one instant, and the hammer ‌that comes out has a ​handle short by a finger's breadth. ​The fly is Loki, who cannot ‌win fairly: the sabotage succeeds ​exactly as far as the handle ‌and no farther.[1]

The Judgment ​and the Sewn Lips (Skáldskaparmál ​35)

The Æsir judge the six ​treasures with Óðinn, Þórr, and Freyr ​as assessors. The boar runs ‌through air and sea and lights ‌the night; the ring drips eight ​rings every ninth night; the ‌hammer never misses, never breaks, always ​returns to the hand, and shrinks ​small enough to carry inside ‌the shirt — its one fault ‌the short handle. The judgment is ‌unanimous: Mjǫllnir is the best ‌of all the treasures, and the ​brothers have won. Loki offers ransom, ‌flees on his sky-shoes, is ​caught, and pleads his last legalism ​— the head was staked, but ​not the neck. Brokkr takes ‌an awl instead and sews Loki's ​lips together; the thread is ​called Vartari.[1]

The Golden Hall ​at Niðavellir (Völuspá 37)

The verse ‌tradition knows a grander Sindri. ​Völuspá counts a hall that stands ​north on Niðavellir, made of gold, ​belonging to the kindred of ‌Sindri — beside the giant Brimir's ​beer-hall at Ókólnir. Here Sindri is ‌no lone craftsman but a ​dwarf chieftain, the head of a ‌family rich enough to roof its ​house in the metal his ‌name throws off as sparks.[2] The ‌dwarf þulur preserved with Skáldskaparmál keep ​the name in the catalogues ​of dwarf heiti, and the manuscript ​tradition splits on the forge-scene itself: ​where part of the tradition ‌names Brokkr's brother Sindri, another part ‌calls him Eitri — one smith ​under two forge-names.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Völuspá 37 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Sindri, Eitri; Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur (dverga heiti), trans. Faulkes (1998).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Sindri's ​closest kin ‌are inside ‌the corpus ​itself. His ​brother Brokkr ​is the ​other half ​of one ‌workshop — ​bellows and ​anvil, never ‌separated in ​the sources.[1] ‌Andvari is ​the corpus's ‌other great ​dwarf of ​the gold, ​but inverted: ​Andvari's hoard ​is won ‌by robbery ​and carries ‌a curse, ​while Sindri's ‌gold is ​made in ‌three heats ​and carries ‌only a ​wager. Vǫlundr ​of Vǫlundarkviða, ‌'prince of ‌elves', is ‌the verse ​tradition's other ​smith narrative ‌— the ​smith wronged ​who takes ‌revenge, where ​Sindri is ​the smith ‌wagered ​against who ‌wins.[2]

Cross-culturally ​the comparisons ​are structural, ​not borrowed: ​Hephaistos, the ‌Greek smith ‌who forges ​the gods' ‌masterworks and ​is himself ​marred; Ilmarinen ‌of the ‌Kalevala, who ‌forges the ‌Sampo under ​a bargain's ‌pressure; Goibniu ​of the ​Irish Tuatha ​Dé Danann, ‌whose smith-work ​arms the ​gods for ​their last ‌battle. The ​pattern is ​old — ​the divine ‌weapon must ​come from ‌an other-than-divine ​hand — ‌but Sindri's ​version is ‌the only ‌one where ​the masterpiece's ​flaw is ​timed to ​a single ‌held breath ‌of the ​bellows.[3]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Sindri's ​afterlife is ‌the afterlife ‌of ​his three ​treasures: ​every retelling ​of Norse ​myth ‌that reaches ​the forging ​of ‌Mjǫllnir keeps ​his one ‌afternoon ​and its ‌three ​stings — ​the short ​handle ​is the ​detail no ‌adapter ​has ever ‌dared to ​cut, ‌because it ​is ‌the story.[1] ​The smith ‌himself ​has had ​a quieter ‌reception ‌than his ‌work, and ​much ​of it ‌flows ​under his ​manuscript double: ‌the ​variant name ​Eitri carries ‌the ​same dwarf-smith ‌into a ​robust ​modern afterlife ​in ​games and ‌film, where ‌the ​forge that ‌made the ​hammer ​is restaged ‌for new ‌audiences ‌— the ‌inventions ​are modern, ‌but the ​forge, ​the brother, ​and the ‌interrupted ​casting are ​Skáldskaparmál's own.

​The ‌sturdiest legacy ​is ​the name: ​Sindri never ‌died ​out in ‌Iceland and ​remains ‌a living ​male given ‌name, ‌borne today ​by ​men who ​inherit the ​forge's ‌spark in ‌its plainest ​form.[2]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Sindri is ​a wholly ‌textual dwarf: ‌no runestone ​invokes him, ​no votive deposit ​names him, ​and no ​image can ‌be securely ​identified with him. ​His attestation ‌hangs on ​the manuscript ‌witnesses — ​the Codex ‌Regius and related ​codices of ​Snorri's Edda ​for Skáldskaparmál ​35, with ​the Codex Upsaliensis ‌tradition reading ​the brother's ‌name as ​Eitri, and ‌the Codex Regius ​of the ‌Poetic Edda ​for Völuspá's ‌golden hall ​at Niðavellir.[1]

The ​material context ‌is nonetheless ‌richer for ‌him than ​for most dwarfs, ​because his ‌products are ​findable. Thor's-hammer ​pendants — ‌Mjǫllnir in miniature ​— are ​the commonest ‌amulets of ​the late ‌Viking Age, ​known by the ​hundred from ​tenth-century graves, ​hoards, and ‌settlement sites ‌across Scandinavia and ​the diaspora: ‌the forge's ​most famous ​casting, worn ‌at the neck ‌of the ‌converted and ‌unconverted alike.[2] ​Gold arm-rings ‌of Draupnir's type ​fill the ​hoards. And ​the corpus's ‌visual language ​of the smithy ​survives on ​the Gotland ‌picture stones ​— Ardre ​VIII shows a ​smithy scene ‌with tools ​and a ‌headless body, ​usually read ‌as Vǫlundr — ​proof that ‌the smith ‌at his ​forge was ​a carved subject ​in the ​Viking-Age north, ‌though no ‌carving names ​Sindri himself.[3]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌never stages ‌Sindri at ​the forge: ​no surviving ​eddic poem ​shows ​him casting, ‌and the ​wager belongs ​to Snorri's ‌prose alone. ​What the ‌poems ​supply is ‌the grander ​frame the ​prose withholds. ​Völuspá, in ​its ​survey of ‌the world's ​dwellings, counts ‌a hall ​that stands ‌north on ​Niðavellir, ‌made of ​gold, belonging ‌to the ​kindred of ​Sindri — ‌a dwarf ‌chieftain ‌of the ​verse tradition, ​the head ‌of a ​family with ​a golden ‌house ​in the ​dwarf-fields, set ‌in the ​catalogue beside ‌the giant ​Brimir's ​beer-hall at ​Ókólnir.[1]

Grímnismál ​fills in ‌the rival ‌workshop: Ívaldi's ​sons, the ‌poem ​says, went ​in days ‌of old ‌to make ‌Skíðblaðnir, best ‌of ships ​— ‌the eddic ​witness to ​the treasures ​whose superiority ‌provoked the ​wager against ​Sindri ​in the ‌first place.[2] ​Between the ​two poems ​the verse ‌tradition ​holds both ‌halves of ​the contest: ‌the sons ​of Ívaldi ‌who made ‌the ​first three ​treasures, and ​the golden ​hall of ‌the kindred ‌that answered ​them.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Sindri's whole ‌narrative witness, ​and it ​is enough. ​Skáldskaparmál 35 gives ​him the ​corpus's longest ‌unbroken scene ​of craft: ​the wager ‌of Loki's head ​against Brokkr's ‌faith in ​his brother, ‌the three ​metals laid ​in the fire, ​the three ​stings, the ​short handle, ‌the judgment ​of the Æsir, ‌and the ​sewing of ‌the trickster's ​lips with ‌Vartari. Snorri's ​craft deserves note: ‌in a ​chapter built ​of catalogues ‌he stops ‌for narrative, ‌and the one ​interior sentence ​he grants ‌anyone — ​Sindri fearing ​the work ‌will fail if ​the blowing ​falters — ‌is granted ​to the ‌smith.[1]

Gylfaginning ​gives his work ​its later ​history without ​naming him: ‌the ring ‌Draupnir is ​laid on Baldr's ‌pyre aboard ​Hringhorni, and ​when Hermóðr ‌rides down ‌to ransom ‌the dead god, ‌Baldr sends ​the ring ‌back to ​Óðinn — ​the forge's ​second casting making ‌the round ​trip to ​Hel and ​home again.[2] ‌The dwarf þulur ​preserved with ​Skáldskaparmál keep ​Sindri in ‌the catalogues ​of dwarf ‌heiti, and the ​manuscript tradition ‌itself splits ​on his ‌name, part ‌of it ​reading Eitri for ​Brokkr's brother ​— one ​smith, two ‌forge-names, the ‌scribes unable ​to choose.[3]

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains no ‌invocation of ​the ​dwarf Sindri. ​No runestone ​asks ​his favour, ‌no amulet ​bears his ​name, ‌and no ​inscription yet ‌found links ​a ‌dedicant to ​his cult ​— ​a silence ​he shares ​with virtually ‌every ​dwarf in ‌the corpus ​and one ‌that ​marks him ‌as a ​figure ‌of the ​literary-mythological tradition ​rather than ‌an ‌independently culted ‌being.[1]

What ​can be ​said ‌runologically is ​the rendering ​itself: ‌in the ​Younger Futhark ​the name ‌would ​have been ‌carved ᛋᛁᚾᛏᚱᛁ ​(s-i-n-t-r-i), a ​normalized ​phonetic reconstruction. ​The short-twig ‌row's ‌stop runes ​do not ‌distinguish voiced ​from ​voiceless, so ‌the ᛏ ‌(Týr) rune ‌writes ‌the cluster ​that the ‌Latin ​spelling gives ​as n-d-r ​— the ‌epenthetic ​stop Old ​Norse grew ​between nasal ‌and ​r is ​exactly the ​sound ‌a single ​undifferentiated rune ‌was built ​to ‌carry. No ​Viking-Age carving ‌of the ‌theonym ​is known; ​the manuscript ​spelling ​of the ‌Eddas remains ‌the primary ​witness.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. dwarfs.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Sindri's medieval ‌afterlife is ​thin, and ​the record ​should ​say so ​plainly. The ‌fornaldarsǫgur and ​chivalric sagas ​know dwarfs ‌well — ​as ‌smiths, treasure-keepers, ​and reluctant ‌helpers of ​heroes — ​but they ​do ​not stage ​Sindri himself; ‌his one ​afternoon at ‌the forge ​belongs ‌to Snorri's ​telling, and ‌the legendary ​tradition borrowed ‌the motif ​of ​the dwarf-forged ‌weapon without ‌borrowing the ‌dwarf's name.[1]

​His second ​medieval life ‌is ​instead scribal. ​The manuscript ‌tradition of ​Skáldskaparmál itself ​splits on ‌the ​smith's name: ‌where the ​Codex Regius ​line names ​Brokkr's brother ​Sindri, ‌the Codex ‌Upsaliensis tradition ​reads Eitri ‌— 'the ​venomous one', ​from eitr, ‌'poison' ‌— and ‌the handbooks ‌treat the ​two as ‌one smith ​under ​two forge-names.[2] ​The variant ‌is not ​a confusion ​to be ​cleaned ‌up but ​a fact ​of transmission: ​the medieval ‌Icelanders who ​copied ‌the forge-scene ​were already ‌telling it ​with two ‌names, and ‌this temple ​keeps ​both in ​the record ​while restoring ‌the elder ‌and better-attested ​one.

  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Sindri, Eitri.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Sindri ​is the ‌Edda's shortest ‌sermon ​on how ​perfection fails. ​The gods' ​three ​greatest treasures ‌are made ​in ​one afternoon ‌by a ​smith whose ‌name ​is a ‌forge-word for ​sparks, ​and two ​of them ​are ​flawless. The ‌third — ​the best, ‌the ​one the ‌Æsir will ​trust ‌in the ​last battle ‌— ​is marred ​by a ‌single instant: ‌a ‌fly, an ​eyelid, a ​held ‌breath of ​the bellows, ​and the ‌handle ​is short ​forever.[1]

The ‌tradition ​does not ‌call the ​hammer ​ruined; it ​calls it ​the finest ‌thing ‌ever forged, ​short handle ‌and ​all. That ​is the ‌forge's verdict ‌and ‌the story's ‌mercy: the ​masterpiece ‌is not ​the work ​that ​never faltered ‌but the ​work that ​survived ​its interruption. ‌Loki wins ​the ​sting and ​loses the ‌wager. ​Sindri wins ‌everything except ​the head ‌he ​was promised ‌— and ‌takes ​the trickster's ​voice instead, ​one stitch ​at ‌a time, ‌with an ​awl.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
