01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Māra (mara) ​— Death, ‌Temptation, Illusion ‌· The ​Tempter, Lord of ​Death — ​is Buddhism's ​personification of ​everything that keeps ‌beings bound: ​death, desire, ​and distraction, ‌the Evil One ​who assailed ‌the future ​Buddha under ‌the Bodhi tree ​and has ​never stopped ​working since.[1]

​The name comes ​from the ‌root √mṛ, ​‘to die’: ‌māra, ‘death, the ​one who ‌causes death, ​the killer’. ‌The Pāli texts ​know him ‌also as ​Namuci, ‘he ​who does not ‌let go’, ‌and as ‌Pāpimā, ‘the ​Evil One’ — ​three names ‌for one ​office.[2] His ​great scene is ‌the night ​of the ​awakening, when ‌he rode against ​the seated ‌sage with ​his armies ​and his daughters ​and lost; ​but his ‌subtler work ‌is the daily ​one — ‌the whispered ​suggestion, the ​timely fear, the ‌offer of ‌an easier ‌road — ‌and the tradition ​insists he ‌is at ​once a ​real devaputta of ​the highest ‌sense-sphere heaven ​and a ​name for the ​mind's own ‌resistance to ​waking.

PuniCodex ​restores the name ​as Māra ‌and serves ​its temple ‌at māra.com. The ​restoration keeps ‌the long ​ā of ‌the Sanskrit; the ‌plain ASCII ​mara is ​a modern ​convenience of the ​early domain-name ‌system, not ‌an attested ​ancient spelling.[2]

Sources

  1. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (New York: Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Māra’.
  2. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. māra, √mṛ.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Sanskrit and Pāli ‌as मार ​(IAST māra): from ​the root √mṛ, ​‘to die’, the ​noun māra means ​‘death, killing, the ‌killer’ — personified, ​the god ​of death and ‌the tempter.[1] His ​bynames complete the ‌dossier: Namuci, ‘the ​one who does ‌not let go’ ​— the ​old asura name ​the Pāli texts ​give him, death ​as grip rather ‌than blow; Pāpimā ​(Pāli; Sanskrit Pāpīyas), ‌‘the Evil ​One’, the tradition's ‌most common address; ​and in Pāli ‌also Kaṇha, ‘the ​Dark One’, pairing ‌him against ​the Buddha as ​darkness against light.[2]

‌The Buddhist analysis ‌later multiplied the ‌name into four ​Māras: the Māra ​of the ‌defilements (kleśa), the ​Māra of the ​aggregates (skandha), the ‌Māra who is ​death (mṛtyu), and ​the devaputta Māra, ‌the deity ​of the Paranimmitavasavattin ‌heaven who commands ​the sense-world's delights. ​The multiplication is ​the doctrine: most ​of what ‘Māra’ ‌does, the ‌texts concede, the ​mind does to ‌itself.[3]

The ASCII ​form mara survives ​only because the ‌early domain-name system ‌could not ‌carry diacritics; it ‌is a technological ​compromise, not an ‌ancient spelling. The ​Unicode restoration Māra ​recovers the ​long ā directly ‌in the address ​bar. Because the ​restoration preserves vowel ​length the ASCII ‌form loses, the ​name is ​classified Tier 1.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • m ​→ M — ‌Same, capitalized
  • ​a → ā ‌— Long ​vowel ā
  • ‌r → r ‌— Same
  • ​a → a ​— Same

The ​project holds the ​domain māra.com ‌(xn--mra-1oa.com) as the ‌canonical home of ​this name.

Sources

  1. Monier-Williams, Monier, A Sanskrit-English Dictionary (Clarendon Press, 1899), s.v. māra, √mṛ.
  2. Edgerton, Franklin, Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit Grammar and Dictionary, vol. 2 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1953), s.v. Māra, Namuci.
  3. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Māra’.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed pronunciation ‌of ‌the name ​is ​/ˈmaː.rə/ — ​Sanskrit ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by ‌phoneme:

  • ​Mā- ​— Bilabial ‌[m] with ​long ‌open [aː]; ​the ‌root syllable ​of ​√mṛ, ‘to ​die’, ​stressed and ​held ‌long
  • ​-ra ‌— Trilled ​or ‌tapped [r] ​with ‌short [ə]

​For ‌the modern ​speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation is: ‌‘MAA-ruh’ ‌— the ​first ​vowel twice ‌the ​length of ​the ‌second.

Kindred ​and ​historical forms ‌of ​the name:

‌- ​Sanskrit / ​Pāli ​— मार ​/ ‌Māra, ‘death, ‌the ​killer’; bynames ‌Namuci, ​Pāpimā, Kaṇha
​- Sinhala ‌— ‌Māra; Thai ‌— ‌Māra, whence ​Māra ‌in the ​idiom ​‘defeat Māra’ ​for ‌any great ​overcoming
​- Tibetan ​— ‌bDud (Dü), ​the ​standard translation: ​‘the ‌Demon’

Māra ​is ‌Tier 1 ​because the ‌long ​ā is ‌the ‌name's very ​root: ​māra from ​mṛ, ​death itself, ‌lengthened ‌in the ​dying.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name ‌is preserved ‌in ​Devanagari ​as मार ​— ​Brahmic ​abugida, attested ‌Sanskrit ​and ​Pāli (via ‌the ​Brāhmī-derived ‌scripts), in ​the ‌Buddhist ​manuscript traditions ​of ​India; ​the script ​is ‌written ​left-to-right.[1]

The ‌scholarly ​transliteration ‌is Māra ​(IAST), ‌giving ​the normalized ‌reading ​/ˈmaː.rə/.

​The rendering ‌proceeds ‌step ‌by step:

​- ​Sanskrit ‌Māra is ​written ​मार ‌in Devanagari: ​मा ​(mā) ‌+ र ​(ra), ‌two ​open syllables; ​the ​vowel ​sign ा ‌marks ‌the ​long ā.
‌- ​The ​word derives ‌from ‌√mṛ, ‌‘to die’: ‌māra, ​‘death, ‌the killer’ ​— ​personified, ​the Buddhist ‌Tempter.
​- ​IAST encodes ​vowel ‌length ​with the ​macron ​(ā).
‌- The ​Unicode ‌restoration ​Māra is ‌registrable ​in ‌.com; the ‌Devanagari ​form ​is not ​supported ​in ‌the .com ‌IDN ​table.[2]

Sources

  2. Edgerton, Franklin, Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit Dictionary (Yale University Press, 1953), s.v. Māra.
05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Māra ​is the ‌lord of ‌everything ​that binds: ​death ​first, but ​also desire, ​fear, ‌and the ​ten thousand ​distractions ‌by which ​a ‌being talks ​itself out ‌of ​waking.[1]

​Death

His ​oldest ​office: mṛtyu-māra, ​the ‌mortality that ​ends every ‌project ​— the ‌king of ​the ‌sense-sphere because ​everything ‌in it ​dies.

​Temptation

‌Kāma-māra: desire ‌as strategy ‌— ​the daughters ​Taṇhā ‌(Craving), Aratī ​(Discontent), and ​Ragā ‌(Passion) sent ​to do ​what ‌armies could ​not.[2]

‌### The ​Armies of ​the ​Mind

His ​ten armies ‌in ‌the Padhāna ​Sutta ‌are psychological ​states: sense-pleasure, ​discontent, ‌hunger and ‌thirst, craving, ‌sloth ‌and torpor, ​fear, ‌doubt, hypocrisy ​and obstinacy, ​self-seeking ​glory, and ‌self-exalting contempt ​for ​others.[2]

​The ‌Household of ​Illusion

As ​devaputta ​of the ‌Paranimmitavasavattin heaven ​he ‌‘controls what ​others ‌create’ — ​the deity ‌of ‌a world ​where everything ​delightful ​is someone ​else's ‌work, and ‌nothing is ​free.

Sources

  1. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Māra’.
  2. Sutta Nipāta 3.2 (Padhāna Sutta); Saṃyutta Nikāya 4 (Mārasaṃyutta).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography ‌of Māra ‌is ​the ​negative image ​of ​the ​Buddha's calm:[1]

‌- ​The ​elephant Girimekhala ‌— ​His ‌war-mount at ​the ‌Bodhi ​tree, the ​beast ​of ​the world's ​bulk ‌thrown ​against ‌one seated ​man
‌- ​The demon ‌army ​— ‌The grotesque ​host ​of ‌Gandhāran reliefs ‌and ‌Thai ​murals: weapons ​that ‌turn ​to flowers ​in ‌mid-air ​over the ​Buddha's ‌head
​- ‌The daughters ​— ​Taṇhā, ​Aratī, Ragā: ​Craving, ‌Discontent, ‌Passion, his ​three ‌envoys ​of charm ​after ‌force ‌fails

  • ‌The ‌bow ​and flower-arrows ‌— ​Later ​art arms ​him ‌as ​a ​dark inversion ​of ‌the ​love-god: arrows ​that ​are ‌blossoms, killing ​by ‌attraction[2]

​- The ‌dark ​body ‌— Kaṇha, ‌‘the ​Dark ​One’: the ​iconographic ​shadow ‌against the ‌Buddha's ​gold

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Māra's mythology ​is the counter-narrative of ‌the Buddha's whole career: ‌wherever the Dharma ​advances, he is on ​the scene within ​a verse.[1]

The ​Assault under the Tree ​(Padhāna Sutta; Lalitavistara ‌21)

As the future ​Śākyamuni sat immovable ​beneath the Bodhi tree, ‌Māra came in person. ​The Padhāna Sutta ‌(Sn 3.2) has him ​name his armies — ‌desire first, then ​discontent, hunger and thirst, ​craving, sloth and ​torpor, fear, doubt, hypocrisy ​and obstinacy, vainglory, and ​self-exalting contempt — ‌and the Buddha answer ​that he would ‌rather die in battle ​than live defeated. The ‌Sanskrit Lalitavistara expands ​the night into epic: ‌Girimekhala the elephant, ​the grotesque host, the ‌weapons falling as flowers, ​the daughters sent ​after the rout — ‌and the earth itself ‌called to witness, ‌quaking its testimony until ​the army breaks.[2]

​### The Daughters (SN ‌4.25)

Force having failed, ​Māra sent Taṇhā, ​Aratī, and Ragā — ‌Craving, Discontent, Passion. ​The Buddha saw them ​as they were, and ‌they withdrew as ​a crow withdraws from ‌a stone it has ​pecked.[3]

The ​Persistent Tempter (Mārasaṃyutta; Bhikkhunīsaṃyutta)

​The canon refuses ​to retire him. For ‌forty-five years Māra returns: ‌to the Buddha, ​suggesting that the Dharma ‌is too deep ​to teach; to the ​nuns, taunting that women ‌cannot awaken (Sister ‌Somā's reply is one ‌of the canon's great ‌retorts); to the ​monks, as a snake ‌in the grass, ​a voice in the ​ear, a sudden doubt. ​He is, in ‌Ling's phrase, the mythology ​of evil domesticated ​into a working psychology.[4]

​### The Last Invitation ‌(DN 16)

His ​final recorded success is ​the gentlest: at ​the Mahāparinibbāna he comes ‌not with armies but ​with a courteous ‌request — the Tathāgata ​has done his work; ‌let him pass ​away now. The Buddha ‌agrees, setting the ‌date of the parinirvāṇa. ​The Tempter's last temptation ​is the one ​even the Buddha grants: ​everything compounded decays ‌— and Māra, who ‌is death, is owed ​his due.[5]

08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Māra ​is the ‌closest figure ‌Buddhism has ​to the ​Devil, and ​the comparison ​has been ​drawn since ‌the first ​Western studies ​— but ‌the tradition ​itself resists ‌the equation: ​Māra ‌creates no ​beings and ​damns no ​one, is ​subject to ​kamma like ‌every other ​being, and ‌in some ​Mahāyāna texts ‌is himself ​converted and ‌destined for ​Buddhahood. ‌T. O. ​Ling's classic ​study maps ‌the difference ‌precisely: not ‌a principle ​of evil ​opposed to ‌God, but ​the personification ​of the ‌forces within ​saṃsāra that ​resist awakening ‌— ​a tempter ‌inside the ​system, not ​a rebel ​against it.[1]

​The tantric ‌tradition absorbed ‌him into ​the protector ‌cults (the ​subjugation of ​Māra is ‌a standard ‌maṇḍala rite), ‌Tibetan ‌tradition translates ​his name ‌as bDud, ​‘the demon’, ​and the ​comparative literature ‌pairs his ​fourfold analysis ​— kleśa, ​skandha, mṛtyu, ‌devaputta — ​with every ​psychology that ​distinguishes ‌the enemy ​without from ‌the enemy ​within. Kindred ‌figures in ​the PuniCodex ‌index include ‌Śākyamuni (the ​one he ​could not ​move) and ​Thánatos (Greek ‌death, his ‌nearest structural ​cousin).

Sources

  1. Ling, Trevor O., Buddhism and the Mythology of Evil (Allen & Unwin, 1962).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Māra's ​deepest legacy ‌is therapeutic: ‌the ​Buddhist analysis ​of ​temptation — ​the ten ​armies ‌as states ​of ​mind — ‌is a ​working ‌psychology of ​distraction that ‌modern ​meditation teaching ​uses ​daily, and ​‘Māra’ has ​become ‌the standard ​shorthand, ‌in Dharma ​halls worldwide, ‌for ​whatever shows ‌up ​to pull ‌the mind ​off ​the cushion.[1] ‌The Dhammapada's ‌victory ‌verses — ​‘seeking ​the builder ‌of this ​house ​I wandered ‌through ​countless births... ​now you ‌are ​seen, O ‌builder; you ​shall ​not build ​again’ ​— remain ‌the most ‌quoted ​lines in ‌the ​Pāli canon, ​the winner's ‌speech ‌over the ‌Tempter.

His ‌image ​built the ‌iconography ​of the ​Buddha's greatest ​moment: ‌the bhūmisparśa ​earth-touching ​gesture — ​the right ‌hand ​down, calling ​the ​earth to ‌witness against ​Māra ‌— is ​the single ‌most ​reproduced pose ‌of ‌the Buddha ​in Southeast ​Asia, ​the Tempter's ​defeat ‌fixed as ‌the teacher's ​emblem.[2]

Sources

  1. Ling, Trevor O., Buddhism and the Mythology of Evil (Allen & Unwin, 1962).
  2. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Māra’, ‘Buddhist Art’.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Māra's ​monuments ‌are the ‌assault ​scenes ​of Buddhist ​art. ​The ​Gandhāran schist ‌reliefs ​(1st–3rd ​c. CE) ‌established ​the ‌composition: ​the serene ‌seated ​Buddha ​at the ​centre, ​the ​grotesque host ‌collapsing ​around ‌him, weapons ​arrested ‌mid-flight ​— ‌the visual ​charter ‌of ​every later ​Māravijaya ‌(‘Victory ‌over Māra’) ‌scene.[1]

​The ​type then ‌became ​Southeast ​Asia's ‌signature: the ​great ​Māravijaya ‌images of ​Bagan, ‌Sukhothai, ​and the ​Thai ​and ​Burmese ‌temple traditions, ‌where ​the ‌earth-touching Buddha ​— ​often ‌with the ‌earth-goddess ‌Vasundharā ‌wringing the ​flood ‌of ​merit ​from her ​hair ‌to ​drown the ​demon ​host ‌— anchors ​the ​whole ​iconographic program. ‌The ​armies ‌of ​the murals, ‌half ​nightmare ‌and half ‌carnival, ​are ​among the ​masterpieces ​of ‌narrative painting ‌in ​Asia.[2]

Sources

  2. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Buddhist Art’.

Pāli Canon

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Māra ​is everywhere ‌in ‌the Pāli ​Canon — ​the ​Tempter is ​one ​of its ‌best-documented characters. ​The ​Padhāna Sutta ‌(Sn ​3.2) preserves ‌the ​austere early ‌account of ​the ​assault: Māra ​as ​Namuci addressing ​the ‌striving Buddha ​with the ‌eight-plus ​armies, each ‌a ​state of ‌mind, and ​the ‌Buddha's answer ​that ​death in ‌the ‌struggle is ‌better than ​life ​in retreat.[1] ‌The ​Mārasaṃyutta (SN ​4) ‌collects twenty-five ​short encounters ​across ‌the whole ​teaching ‌career — ​Māra as ​farmer, ​as herdsman, ​as ‌meteor, as ‌voice ​— including ‌the daughters' ​embassy ​(4.25); the ‌Bhikkhunīsaṃyutta ‌(SN 5) ‌records ‌his ten ​taunts to ‌the ​nuns and ​their ​triumphant dismissals.

‌The Mahāparinibbāna ​Sutta ​(DN 16) ​gives ‌him the ​last ​word of ​the biography: ‌his ​courteous request ‌that ​the Buddha ‌pass ​away now, ‌and the ‌Buddha's ​consent — ​death, ​even for ​the deathless ‌teaching, ‌arriving on ​schedule.[2]

Sources

  1. Sutta Nipāta 3.2; Bodhi, Bhikkhu, trans., The Connected Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 2000): SN 4, SN 5.
  2. Walshe, Maurice, trans., The Long Discourses of the Buddha (Wisdom, 1995): DN 16.

Mahāyāna Sūtras

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Sanskrit tradition ‌enlarged ‌the ​assault into ​epic. ​The ​Lalitavistara (ch. ​21) ‌stages ​the night ​at ‌Bodhgayā ​at full ‌scale: ​the ‌summons to ​the ​demon host, ​Girimekhala ​the ​elephant, the ‌weapons ​turning ‌to flowers, ​the ‌earth ​shaking her ‌witness ​— ‌the version ​the ​art ‌of Asia ‌paints.[1]

‌The Mahāyāna ​sūtras ​then ‌interiorize him. ​The ​Perfection ‌of Wisdom ​literature ​warns ‌the bodhisattva ​against ‌‘Māra's ​deeds’ — ​subtle ​discouragements, ​spiritual pride, ‌the ‌temptation ​to abandon ‌beings ​for private ​peace ‌— ‌and the ‌Vimalakīrtinirdeśa ‌teaches ​that the ‌bodhisattva ​works ​precisely in ​Māra's ‌fields, ​‘among the ​hosts ​of ‌Māra’ as ​among ​flowers. Some ​Mahāyāna ‌texts ​go further ‌and ​prophesy ‌the devaputta ​Māra's ‌own ‌eventual awakening: ​the ​Tempter ​himself is ​not ‌outside ‌the Dharma's ​reach.[2]

Sources

  1. Lalitavistara Sūtra, ch. 21.
  2. Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (Macmillan Reference, 2004), s.v. ‘Māra’; Vimalakīrtinirdeśa Sūtra.

Commentarial Tradition

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Pāli commentarial ‌tradition ‌built the ​canon's ​scattered encounters ​into the ​full ​biography: the ‌Nidānakathā, ​Buddhaghosa's introduction ​to ‌the Jātaka ​commentary, ‌gives the ​Theravāda's ‌complete narrative ​of the ​assault ​— the ​earth ​called to ‌witness ​the perfections, ‌the ​army's rout, ‌the ​tree-deities' jubilation ‌— and ​the ‌commentaries on ​SN ​4 gloss ‌each ‌apparition with ‌the ​working psychology ​of ‌meditation.[1]

The ​systematic analysis ​belongs ‌to the ​Abhidharma ​and śāstra ‌traditions: ​the fourfold ‌Māra ​— the ​defilements ​(kleśa), the ​aggregates (skandha), ‌death ‌(mṛtyu), and ​the ‌devaputta deity ​— ​turns the ‌myth ‌into a ‌diagnostic ‌scheme in ​which three ‌of ​the four ​Māras ​are internal, ‌a ​framework the ​Mahāprajñāpāramitāśāstra ​and the ‌Tibetan ​graduated-path literature ​made standard. ​T. ‌O. Ling's ​modern ‌study remains ​the ‌standard interpretation: ​Buddhism's ‌mythology of ‌evil ​as the ​personified friction ​of ​a mind ‌against ‌its own ​liberation.[2]

Sources

  1. Nidānakathā of the Jātaka commentary; the commentaries on SN 4.
  2. Ling, Trevor O., Buddhism and the Mythology of Evil (Allen & Unwin, 1962); Buswell, Robert E., ed., Encyclopedia of Buddhism (2004), s.v. ‘Māra’.

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Māra ​is the ‌most psychologically ‌honest ​figure in ​Buddhist ​mythology. The ​canon never ​lets ‌him be ​merely ​a monster: ‌his armies ​are ‌moods, his ​daughters ‌are cravings, ​his weapons ​are ​the ones ​the ​mind uses ‌on itself. ​The ‌night under ​the ‌tree is ​everyone's night ‌before ​any real ‌change ​— the ​sudden appetite, ‌the ‌timely fear, ‌the ​reasonable voice ​suggesting tomorrow.

‌What ​defeats him ​is ‌not force ​but recognition. ​The ‌Buddha does ​not ‌fight the ​armies; he ​names ​them, touches ​the ‌earth, and ‌stays seated. ​The ‌tradition's whole ​counsel ​on temptation ‌is in ‌that ‌gesture: you ‌do ​not have ‌to win ​the ​argument with ​the ‌voice — ​you have ​to ​know whose ‌voice ​it is. ​Namuci, they ​called ‌him: the ​one ‌who does ​not let ‌go. ​The answer ‌to ‌a grip ​is not ​a ​blow. It ​is ‌not needing ‌to pull ​free.

Sources

  1. Sutta Nipāta 3.2 (Padhāna Sutta); Ling, Trevor O., Buddhism and the Mythology of Evil (1962).
