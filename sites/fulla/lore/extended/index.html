Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Fulla in Later Traditions

Fulla's cross-border life runs through one word and one relationship. The word is fullr: Proto-Germanic *fullaz gives English 'full', German 'voll', Gothic fulls, and Old Norse Fulla — a goddess whose name is the ordinary adjective turned abstract noun, 'Fullness'. The relationship is the one the Merseburg Charm preserves: Volla beside Friia, as Fulla beside Frigg — and since Grimm, the identification of the two has been the standing reading, with Simek registering both the fit and the doubt. The deeper syncretic question is what kind of being she is. Gylfaginning's ásynjur catalogue is full of abstract-noun goddesses — Sjǫfn ('affection'), Lofn ('praise, permission'), Vár ('vows') — and scholars have long suspected that Fulla, Gná, and their sisters are facets of Frigg herself, the queen's plenitude and far-ranging given names and errands of their own. Whether she began as an independent goddess of plenty or as an aspect that walked off on its own, the sources keep her a person: she receives a ring, runs an errand, and wears her own gold.

Modern Legacy

Fulla's afterlife is scholarly and liturgical rather than popular. Grimm devoted a chapter of Teutonic Mythology to her and Volla, and every handbook since — Simek, Orchard, Lindow — keeps her as the standing example of the abstract-noun ásynja, the goddess whose name tells you exactly what she is. Her image — loose hair, golden band, the queen's casket on her arm — became the fixed Victorian and modern picture of the trusted northern handmaid, and she appears in nearly every retelling of the Baldr cycle by virtue of one finger-ring. Modern Heathen and Ásatrú practice keeps her at Frigg's side as a goddess of plenty, household trust, and well-kept secrets, invoked where abundance must be held in confidence. She never became a Marvel character or a video-game boss; she remained what the Edda made her — the most trusted person in the queen's hall, and the proof that in the northern pantheon even abundance has a keeper.

In the Earth

Fulla is an entirely textual goddess: no cult site, toponym, votive deposit, or inscription names her, and the runic corpus is silent — unsurprising for a figure the sources place inside the queen's household rather than in the public cult. Her witnesses are manuscripts. The Grímnismál prose, which preserves her one errand, survives in the Codex Regius (c. 1270); her portrait in Gylfaginning 35 and her ring in Gylfaginning 49 come with the Prose Edda codices of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and her name recurs in the þulur, the metrical name-lists appended to Skáldskaparmál. The one continental witness is the Second Merseburg Charm, copied into a theological manuscript at Fulda in the tenth century and now in the Merseburg cathedral library: its Volla, sister of Friia, is the only attestation of the figure outside Iceland and, if the identification holds, the oldest by centuries.

The Meditation

What Fulla means — beyond the dictionary

Fulla is the goddess of what is held in trust. Every detail of her portrait is a form of custody: the casket she carries but does not own, the shoes she tends but does not wear, the counsels she shares but does not speak. Even her name works this way — Fullness is not a thing she does but a thing she keeps. The tradition gives her no adventure because her office is the adventure's opposite: Gná rides out with the queen's words; Fulla stays, and what is entrusted to her stays with her. Nanna's ring from Hel is the tradition's own verdict on that office — when the dead send gifts home, they send one to the keeper. In a mythology that ends in fire and betrayal, Fulla is the quiet counterweight: the proof that some things, once entrusted, are simply kept.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Fulla, Abundance, Secrets, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Fulla?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Fulla is /ˈful.la/ — approximately FULL-ah — a short, rounded u as in 'put', a genuinely long, doubled l, and a short open 'ah'; two even syllables, stress on the first.

02What does Fulla mean?

Fulla means Bountiful in the norse tradition.

03What are the symbols of Fulla?

Fulla is associated with The eski (little casket) (Frigg's portable treasure-box, entrusted to the hand that never lets it go — custody as a form of intimacy), The golden hair-band (The virgin's loose hair bound with gold — unmarried freedom dressed in plenty), Frigg's shoes (The queen's footwear in her keeping — the lowest duty held by the highest confidence), The finger-ring from Hel (Nanna's gift carried back by Hermóðr — a token from the dead addressed, of all the ásynjur, to her).

04Why restore Fulla in Unicode?

Fulla is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Fulla?

“The Keeper of the Casket” — Fulla has no saga of her own: her dossier is four precise notices, and every one of them places her at Frigg's side. What the tradition lost in narrative it kept in definition — no goddess in the Edda is more exactly described in a single sentence. The full telling lives on the lore page.
