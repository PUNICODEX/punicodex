01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Fulla (fulla) ​— Abundance, ‌Secrets · Frigg's ‌Handmaid, the ​Keeper of the ​Casket — ​is the most ​trusted figure in ​the queen's ‌hall: the virgin ​ásynja who ​goes with her ‌hair loose ​and a golden ‌band about ​her head, who ‌carries Frigg's ​little box, looks ​after her ​shoes, and shares ​her secret ​counsels.[1]

Old Norse ‌Fulla is a ​transparent formation ‌on fullr 'full' ​— the ‌same word as ​English 'full' ‌and German 'voll' ​— so ‌the name means ​'Fullness, Plenty': ​an abstract noun ‌worn as ‌a personal name, ‌like her ​sister ásynjur Sjǫfn ​('affection') and Lofn ‌('praise, permission').[5] ​Her dossier is ​four precise ‌notices: Snorri's portrait ​in Gylfaginning ​35, her errand ‌to King ​Geirröðr in the ‌prose introduction ​to Grímnismál, the ​finger-ring Nanna ​sends her from ​Hel in ‌Gylfaginning 49, and ‌— if the ​identification holds ‌— her continental ​echo as ​Volla, sister of ‌Friia, in ‌the Second Merseburg ‌Charm.[1][2][3][4]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Fulla ‌— identical to ​the ASCII ​input, because normalized ​Old Norse ‌writes no diacritic ​in this name. ​The mechanical ​rule therefore places ‌it in ​Tier 2: nothing ​distinctive is ​lost in transit, ‌a statement ​about orthography and ‌never about ​the goddess. This ‌temple is ​domain-less: the plain-ASCII ‌fulla.com is ‌registered to a ​third party outside ​the collection ​and is watched ​for release; ‌no form of ‌the name ​is claimed.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old ‌Norse as ​Fulla — ​a feminine ​weak noun formed ​directly on ​the adjective ‌fullr 'full', ​hence 'Fullness, ​Plenty, the Bountiful ‌one'.[1] It ​belongs to ‌the family ​of abstract-noun ‌goddess-names in ​Gylfaginning's catalogue of ​the ásynjur: ​Sjǫfn from ​sjafni 'affection', ​Lofn from ‌lof 'praise, permission', ​Vár from ‌várar 'vows' ​— qualities ‌and states ​given names ‌and errands of ​their own.[3] ‌The name ​answers her ​office exactly: ‌the keeper of ‌the queen's ‌treasure-box is ​herself called ​Plenty.

The ‌ASCII form ​and the restored ​form are ‌identical because ​the normalized ​Old Norse ‌spelling carries no ​diacritic and ‌no distinctive ​letter: five ​plain Latin ​letters. The ​mechanical tier rule ‌therefore classifies ‌the name ​Tier 2 ‌— the ​restoration preserves nothing ​the ASCII ‌form loses. ‌This is ‌an orthographic ‌report, not ​a judgment of ‌standing.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • ​f → ‌F — Same, ​capitalized
  • ​u → ​u — ‌Same
  • ​l → ​l — Same

​- l ‌→ l ​— Same
‌- a ​→ a — ‌Same

No ​form of ‌the name ‌is held ​in the ​collection: the plain-ASCII ​fulla.com is ​registered outside ‌the project ‌and watched ​for release.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈful.la/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Fu- — ​Voiceless labiodental [f], ‌short close ​rounded [u] ​as in English ‌put; stressed ​first syllable, as ‌in every ​Old Norse ‌word
  • -lla ​— A ​true geminate ​[lː], the double ​l held ​twice as long ‌as a ​single l, ‌then a short ​open [a]

‌For the ​modern speaker, the ‌closest approximation ​is: 'FULL-ah' — ‌but with ​the vowel ​of put, not ‌pool, and ‌the l ‌genuinely doubled, two ​even syllables ​with the weight ‌on the ​first.

Kindred ​and historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • ​Old Norse — ‌Fulla, Frigg's ​handmaid; from fullr, ‌'full'
  • ​Old High ​German — Volla, ​Friia's sister ​in the ‌Second Merseburg Charm, ‌widely read ​as the same ‌figure[2]
  • ​Proto-Germanic — ​*fullaz, 'full' — ‌the ancestor ‌of English ‌'full', German 'voll', ‌Gothic fulls, ​and Norse fullr[3]

‌- Owned ​form — ​None; the temple ​claims no ‌domain, and ​fulla.com is held ​outside the ​collection

Fulla is ‌Tier 2 ​by rule: ​the normalized Old ​Norse form ‌is already ​plain ASCII, and ‌the restoration ​loses nothing in ‌transit. The ​doubled l ‌— which the ‌runic row ​would write ​with a single ​ᛚ — ​is the one ‌feature no ‌plain spelling ​can show.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Fulla — Old ​Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ‌13th c. CE ​in the ​Codex Regius ‌(the Grímnismál prose) ​and the ‌Prose Edda ​codices, recording material ‌centuries older.[1] ​Before the ​Latin bookhand, Old ​Norse names ​were carved ​in the Younger ‌Futhark; no ​runic attestation of ‌this theonym ​survives, so ‌the manuscript form ​is the ‌primary witness.

​The scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

​- The name ‌is a ‌feminine weak ‌noun formed on ​the adjective ​fullr 'full'; ‌as an a-stem ​it carries ​no nominative ‌-r, which is ​why the ​name ends ‌in plain -a.
​- The ‌double l ​is a true ​geminate /lː/; ​the Younger ​Futhark rendering ᚠᚢᛚᛅ ‌writes it ‌with a ​single ᛚ, as ‌the runic ​row does ​not mark consonant ‌length.

  • ‌The vowels ‌are short: no ‌length marks ​appear in ‌normalized Old Norse ​orthography, and ​none are ​restored.
  • The ‌Unicode restoration ​Fulla is therefore ​identical to ​the ASCII ‌form apart from ​the capital: ​the name ​carries no diacritic ‌and no ​distinctive letter, ‌and is Tier ​2 by ‌the mechanical ​rule.[2]

The temple ‌claims no ‌domain: the ​plain-ASCII fulla.com is ​held by ​a third ​party outside the ‌collection and ‌is watched ​for release.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Fulla.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Fulla ​is catalogued ‌under Abundance, ‌Secrets — ​and the ​sources earn ​both terms ​of it: ​her name ‌is Fullness ​itself, and ​her defining ‌duty is ​the keeping ‌of what ​the ‌queen does ​not say ​aloud.[1]

​Abundance

Her ​name is ​the abstract ‌noun of ​fullr, 'full': ‌Plenty given ​a person, ‌a golden ​band, and ‌a casket ​to ‌carry — ​the goddess ​in whose ‌keeping the ‌queen's portable ‌wealth actually ​lives.

​Secrets

She ‌shares Frigg's ​leyniráðagørðir, the ​queen's secret ‌deliberations: of ​the three ​duties in ‌her ​portrait, the ‌counsels are ​the one ​that admits ​her past ​every door.

‌### Fidelity ‌and Service

​The casket ‌and the ​shoes are ​the badges ‌of the ‌trusted chamber-servant ‌— ‌the lowest ​duties of ‌the hall ​held, in ​her case, ​at the ‌highest level ​of confidence.[1]

​### The ​Queen's Hall

‌Every notice ​the tradition ​keeps of ​her ‌places her ​at Frigg's ‌side: named ​beside Gná, ‌Frigg's errand-rider, ​in the ‌catalogue of ‌the ásynjur, ​and remembered ​beside the ​queen herself ​when Nanna's ‌gifts come ‌back from ​Hel.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35 and 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Fulla's ​iconography is ‌the iconography ‌of ​trust — ​Snorri fixes ​her ​appearance and ​office in ‌a ​single sentence, ​and the ‌tradition never ​adds ‌to it:[1]

​- The ‌eski ​— Frigg's ​little casket, ​which ​Fulla carries: ​the portable ‌treasury ​of the ‌queen of ​the ‌gods, and ​the clearest ‌badge ​of her ‌office

  • ​The ​golden band ‌— the ‌gold snood ‌she ​wears about ​her loose ‌hair: ​unmarried freedom ​dressed in ‌plenty, ​and Snorri's ​one detail ‌of ​dress
  • ‌The loose ​hair ​itself — ​the virgin's ​unbound ‌hair, the ‌visible sign ​of ‌her status ​in a ​hall of ‌married ‌goddesses
  • ‌Frigg's shoes ‌— ​the queen's ‌footwear in ​her ​keeping: the ​humblest duty ‌in ​the catalogue, ​and the ​surest ‌sign of ​a servant ​admitted ​to every ‌chamber
  • ​The ‌finger-ring from ​Hel — ‌Nanna's gift, ​carried ‌back by ‌Hermóðr: a ​token ​from the ​dead addressed, ​of ‌all the ‌ásynjur, to ​her[1]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Fulla has no ​saga of her own: her mythology ‌is four precise notices, and every ‌one of them places her ​at Frigg's side. What the tradition ​lost in narrative it kept in ​definition — no goddess in ​the Edda is more exactly described ​in a single sentence.

The ‌Keeper of the Casket (Gylfaginning ​35)

Snorri's catalogue of the ásynjur ​gives Fulla her entire portrait ‌at once: 'Fulla is a virgin ​and goes with her hair loose ‌and a golden band about ​her head; she carries Frigg's eski, ‌looks after her footwear, and shares ​her secret counsels with her.' ​The three duties descend in rank ​and rise in intimacy — the ​box is treasure, the shoes ​are service, the counsels are trust ‌itself. She is named immediately after ​Gná, Frigg's errand-rider, and the ‌pairing has stuck: where Gná carries ​the queen's words out through the ‌worlds, Fulla keeps the queen's ​words that never leave the hall.[1]

‌### The Warning to Geirröðr ​(Grímnismál, prose)

Her one errand in ‌the Poetic Edda is given in ​prose. Óðinn and Frigg had ​quarrelled over their foster-sons: Frigg accused ‌King Geirröðr, Óðinn's favourite, of being ‌so stingy he tortured his ‌guests, and Óðinn laid a wager ​and set off disguised to test ​him. Frigg sent her handmaid ‌Fulla to Geirröðr ahead of him, ​to warn the king to beware ​of a sorcerer coming to ‌bewitch him — with the token ​that no dog, however fierce, would ​leap at the man. The ‌warning is the trap: Geirröðr seized ​the stranger, named Grímnir, and ‌tortured him between two fires, and ​out of that fire the god ​spoke the whole catalogue of ​the worlds. Fulla appears only to ​deliver the message and vanish, but ‌the wager, the torture, and ‌the poem's entire revelation turn on ​her errand.[2]

The Ring from ‌Hel (Gylfaginning 49)

When Hermóðr ​rode nine nights to ransom Baldr ​and came home with the tidings, ‌he brought tokens from the ‌dead: Baldr sent back the ring ‌Draupnir to Óðinn, and Nanna — ‌Baldr's wife, who had died ​of grief on his pyre — ‌sent gifts out of Hel ​to the two women of Frigg's ​hall: a linen headdress for Frigg, ​and a finger-ring for Fulla. ‌When a dead goddess thinks of ​Ásgarðr's household, she thinks of the ​queen and of the queen's ​handmaid by name.[3]

Volla, Friia's ‌Sister (Second Merseburg Charm)

The one ​continental witness is the Second ​Merseburg Charm, the only pagan theogonic ​text in Old High German: Phol ‌and Wodan rode to the ​wood, Balder's foal wrenched its foot, ‌and the goddesses chant over it ​in sequence — Sinthgunt, Sunna ‌her sister; Friia, Volla her sister; ​and at last Wodan, who ‌knows how. Since Jacob Grimm, Volla ‌— Friia's sister — has widely ​been read as Fulla: the ​name is the exact Old High ​German form of the same word, ​and the charm puts her, ‌precisely as the Edda does, at ‌Friia's right hand. The identification is ​argued, not proven.[4]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Fulla's cross-border ​life runs ‌through one word ‌and one ​relationship. The ​word is fullr: ​Proto-Germanic fullaz ​gives English 'full', ​German 'voll', ‌Gothic fulls, ​and Old Norse ​Fulla — ‌a goddess whose ​name is ‌the ordinary ​adjective turned abstract ‌noun, 'Fullness'.[2] ​The relationship is ​the one ​the Merseburg ​Charm preserves: Volla ​beside Friia, ‌as Fulla beside ​Frigg — ‌and since Grimm ​the identification ‌of the ​two has been ‌the standing ​reading, with Simek ‌registering both ​the fit ​and the doubt.[3][4]

‌The deeper ‌question is what ‌kind of ​being she ​is. Gylfaginning's ásynjur ‌catalogue is ​full of abstract-noun ​goddesses — ‌Sjǫfn, Lofn, ​Vár — and ​scholars have ‌long suspected that ​Fulla, Gná, ‌and their sisters ​are facets ​of Frigg ​herself: the queen's ​plenitude and ‌far-ranging given names ‌and errands ​of their ‌own.[3] Whether she ​began as ​an independent goddess ‌of plenty ‌or as ‌an aspect that ‌walked off ​on its own, ‌the sources ​keep her ​a person: she ​receives a ‌ring, runs an ​errand, and ​wears her own ​gold. Within ‌the corpus, ​her closest kin ​are the ​figures of Frigg's ‌household: Frigg ​herself, Nanna, ‌who sends her ​the ring, ‌and the other ​ásynjur of ‌Snorri's catalogue, ‌among them Syn, ​who guards ​the door against ​those who ​should not ‌enter, and Eir, ‌the best ​of physicians.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Fulla's ​afterlife is ‌scholarly and ‌liturgical ​rather than ​popular. Grimm ​devoted sustained ​attention ​to her ‌and Volla ​in ​Teutonic Mythology, ‌and every ​handbook ‌since — ​Simek, Orchard, ‌Lindow ​— keeps ​her as ​the standing ​example ​of the ‌abstract-noun ásynja: ​the ‌goddess whose ​name tells ‌you ​exactly what ‌she is.[2][3][4] ​Her ‌image — ​loose hair, ​golden band, ‌the ‌queen's casket ‌on her ​arm ​— became ‌the fixed ​picture ​of the ‌trusted northern ​handmaid, ​and she ‌enters nearly ​every retelling ‌of ​the Baldr ​cycle by ​virtue ​of one ‌finger-ring.[5]

In ‌contemporary ​practice, modern ‌Heathen and ​Ásatrú ​communities keep ‌her at ‌Frigg's side ‌as ‌a goddess ​of plenty, ‌household ​trust, and ​well-kept secrets, ​invoked ‌where abundance ​must be ​held ​in confidence. ‌She never ​became a ​pop-culture ​figure; she ‌remained what ​the ‌Edda made ​her — ‌the ​most trusted ‌person in ‌the ​queen's hall, ​and the ​tradition's proof ​that ‌even abundance ‌has a ​keeper.[4]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Fulla is ​an entirely ‌textual goddess: ‌no cult ​site, toponym, ​votive deposit, ​or inscription ​names her, ​and the ‌runic corpus is ​silent — ​unsurprising for ‌a figure ​the sources ‌place inside ​the queen's ‌household rather ​than in ​the public cult.[1] ​Her witnesses ​are manuscripts. ​The Grímnismál ‌prose, which ​preserves her ‌one errand, ​survives in ‌the Codex Regius ​(c. 1270); ‌her portrait ​in Gylfaginning ‌35 and ​her ring ​in Gylfaginning ‌49 come ‌with the ‌Prose Edda codices ​of the ​thirteenth and ‌fourteenth centuries; ​and her ​name recurs ‌in the ​þulur, the ​metrical name-lists ‌appended to Skáldskaparmál.[2][3][4]

​The one ‌continental witness ​is the ​Second Merseburg ​Charm, copied ​into a ‌theological manuscript ‌at Fulda ​in the tenth ‌century and ​now in ​the Merseburg ‌cathedral library: ‌its Volla, ‌sister of ‌Friia, is ​the only ‌attestation of the ​figure outside ​Iceland and, ​if the ‌identification holds, ​the oldest ​by centuries.[5] ​Against this ‌single Carolingian-era survival, ​the whole ​archaeological record ​— the ‌Migration-Age gold ​of the ‌period's hoards, ​the picture ‌stones, the ​amulets — offers ‌atmosphere rather ‌than attribution: ​golden bands ​are abundant ​in the ​ground, but ‌none can ‌be assigned ​to Fulla.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda knows ‌Fulla only ‌in prose ​— but ​the notice ​it gives ​her is ​her one ‌errand in ​the entire ​corpus, and ‌the machinery ​of a ‌whole poem ​turns on ‌it. The ​prose introduction ​to Grímnismál ​sets the ​wager between ​Óðinn and ‌Frigg over ​their foster-sons, ‌and it ​is Fulla ‌whom Frigg ​sends to ‌King Geirröðr ​with the ‌warning about ​the coming ​sorcerer and ‌the token ‌of the ‌dogs. Geirröðr ​acts on ​it, seizes ‌the blue-cloaked ​Grímnir, and ​tortures him ‌between the ​fires — ​and the ‌poem that follows, ​the god's ‌great catalogue ​of the ​worlds, is ​the direct ​consequence of ‌the handmaid's ‌message.[1]

The ​Edda's silence ‌elsewhere is ​itself information. ​Grímnismál's verse ‌catalogue of ‌the dwellings ‌of the ‌gods names ​hall after ‌hall — ​Þrúðheimr, Breiðablik, ​Himinbjǫrg, Fensalir ​— but ‌assigns Fulla ​none: she ​lives, as ​her office ‌says, inside ​Frigg's own ​hall of ​Fensalir, without ‌an address ​of her ‌own. The ​þulur tradition, ‌which survives ​with the ‌Eddic materials, ‌keeps her ​name in ​the metrical ​lists of ​the ásynjur, ‌preserving her ‌where narrative ​does not.[3]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál, prose introduction (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Grímnismál 4–13 (the dwellings of the gods; Fensalir), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur of the ásynjur, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Fulla's home ‌text: it contains ​her portrait, ​her place in ​the pantheon's ​order, and ​her one moment ‌in the ​great narrative. Gylfaginning ​35 names ‌her among ​the ásynjur, immediately ‌after Gná, ​and gives the ‌sentence on ​which her ​entire tradition rests ​— the ​virgin with the ​loose hair ‌and golden ​band, who carries ‌Frigg's eski, ​tends her footwear, ‌and shares ​her secret ‌counsels.[1] Snorri's catalogue ​is systematic, ‌and the company ​he keeps ​her in ‌matters: she stands ‌among the ‌named ásynjur — ​Sága, Eir, ​Gefjon, Syn, ‌and the rest ​— not ​as a metaphor ‌but as ​one of ​the numbered goddesses ‌of Ásgarðr.

​Gylfaginning 49 gives ‌her the ​narrative moment. ​As Hermóðr prepares ​to ride ​back from Hel ‌with the ‌ransom almost ​won, Nanna commits ‌her gifts ​to him: a ​linen headdress ‌for Frigg, ‌and a finger-ring ‌for Fulla. ‌In the longest ​and most ‌carefully staged ​episode of the ​whole Edda ​— the death ‌of Baldr ​— Fulla's ​part is a ​single word ‌of bequest, and ​it is ​exactly proportioned ​to her office: ‌the dead ​remember the queen's ‌household by ​naming its ‌two women.[2] The ​þulur of ‌Skáldskaparmál then preserve ‌her name ​in the ​metrical lists, the ​memorial verse-catalogues ​where the tradition ‌filed what ‌it could ​not narrate.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur of the ásynjur, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The runic ​corpus contains ‌no attestation ‌of Fulla. ​No runestone ​invokes her, ​no amulet ​carries her name, ​and no ‌inscription links ​a dedicant ​to her ‌cult — ​a silence ‌she shares ​with nearly all ‌the ásynjur ​of Snorri's ​catalogue and ​one that ​marks her ​as a ‌figure of the ​household mythology ‌rather than ​of public ‌dedicatory practice.[1]

​What the ‌runic tradition ​contributes is orthographic. ‌In the ​sixteen-rune Younger ​Futhark, the ‌name would ‌have been ‌carved ᚠᚢᛚᛅ ​— f-u-l-a ​— and the ‌rendering teaches ​the usual ​lesson: the ‌runic row ​underspecifies what ​the manuscripts ‌fix. The ᛚ ​rune writes ‌both single ​and geminate ​l, so ​the carving ​cannot show ‌the doubled consonant ‌that defines ​the name's ‌sound; and ​the name ​carries no ‌final nominative ‌-r, since ‌Fulla is a ‌feminine weak ​noun. No ‌Viking-Age carving ​of the ​theonym is ​known, so ‌the runic form ​remains a ​normalized reconstruction, ​with the ‌manuscript spelling ​as the ​primary witness.

​The nearest epigraphic ‌kin is ​not runic ‌at all: ​the Second ‌Merseburg Charm's ​Volla, written ‌in a ‌tenth-century Carolingian bookhand ​at Fulda, ​is the ​only written ​attestation of ‌the figure ‌outside the ​Icelandic codices.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Fulla.
  2. The Second Merseburg Charm, ed. Braune–Ebbinghaus (Niemeyer, 1979).

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Fulla's ‌medieval afterlife ‌is thin ​and telling: ​the kings' ​sagas, the ​sagas of ​Icelanders, and ‌Saxo Grammaticus ​know nothing ​of her. ‌Heimskringla's euhemerized ​gods at ‌Uppsala keep ​Óðinn, Njǫrðr, ‌and Freyr, ​but Frigg's ​household — and ​with it ​Fulla — ​does not ‌survive the ​translation into ‌history. Saxo's ​Gesta Danorum, ‌which retells ​the Baldr ‌story at ​length with ‌its own ​Høtherus and ​Balderus, gives ‌no corresponding ‌role to ‌a Fulla: in ​his version ​the divine ‌household is ​stripped to ​its principals, ‌and the ​queen's handmaid ​vanishes with ‌the queen's ​other attendants.

‌Her continental ​survival is ​earlier and ​stranger than ​any saga: ‌the Second ‌Merseburg Charm, ​copied at Fulda ‌in the ​tenth century, ​preserves Volla ‌beside Friia ‌in the ‌healing sequence ‌for Balder's ​foal — ‌a fossil ​of the ​figure from ​four centuries ‌before the ​Icelandic books, ​in a ​manuscript that ‌had no literary ​reason to ​keep her.[1] ​Between that ‌Carolingian survival ​and Snorri's ‌thirteenth-century catalogue, ​the written ‌record is ​empty; the ‌figure lived, ‌if she ​lived anywhere, ​in the ​oral tradition ​the þulur ‌and the ‌Eddas finally ​wrote down.[3]

Sources

  1. The Second Merseburg Charm, ed. Braune–Ebbinghaus (Niemeyer, 1979).
  2. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 3 (Høtherus and Balderus), trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur of the ásynjur, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Fulla ​is the ‌goddess of ‌what ​is held ​in ​trust. Every ​detail of ​her ‌portrait is ​a ​form of ‌custody: the ​casket ‌she carries ​but does ‌not ​own, the ​shoes ​she tends ​but does ​not ‌wear, the ​counsels ‌she shares ​but does ‌not ​speak. Even ‌her ​name works ‌this way ​— ​Fullness is ‌not a ‌thing ‌she does ​but ​a thing ‌she keeps.[1]

​The ​tradition gives ‌her ​no adventure ​because her ‌office ​is the ‌adventure's opposite: ​Gná ​rides out ​with ​the queen's ‌words; Fulla ‌stays, ​and what ‌is ​entrusted to ​her stays ‌with ‌her. Nanna's ‌ring from ‌Hel ​is the ‌tradition's ​own verdict ​on that ​office ‌— when ​the ​dead send ​gifts home, ‌they ​send one ​to ​the keeper.[1] ‌In a ​mythology ‌that ends ​in fire ‌and ​betrayal, Fulla ‌is ‌the quiet ​counterweight: the ​proof ​that some ​things, ‌once entrusted, ‌are simply ​kept.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 35 and 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
