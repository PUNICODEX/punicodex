The hidden history behind Fulla

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Fulla is the quieter case: a goddess whose name is a whole theology compressed into one ordinary adjective — fullr, 'full' — and whose normalized Old Norse spelling is already unbroken ASCII. There is nothing to restore, and a surprising amount to explain: a golden band, a little box, a queen's shoes, a queen's secrets, an errand that sets an entire Eddic poem in motion, and a ring sent back from the land of the dead. This post walks the full record — and ends where the record gets honest: Frigg's most trusted handmaid has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII fulla.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Fulla (fulla) — Abundance, Secrets · Bountiful — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Abundance, Secrets". The name's traditional gloss is "Bountiful", from the Old Norse adjective fullr, 'full'.

Fulla is Frigg's handmaid and confidante: Snorri's whole portrait of her is one sentence, and the tradition never varies it — she is a virgin who goes with her hair loose and a golden band about her head, carries Frigg's little box, looks after her shoes, and shares her secret counsels. Her one errand, in the prose before Grímnismál, sets that poem's entire revelation in motion; her one bequest, a finger-ring sent from Hel by Nanna, places her exactly where her office puts her — the second woman of the queen's hall.

PuniCodex restores the name as Fulla — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the goddess. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII fulla.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Old Norse as Fulla — a feminine weak noun formed directly on the adjective fullr 'full', hence 'Fullness, Plenty, the Bountiful one'. It belongs to the family of abstract-noun goddess-names in Gylfaginning's catalogue of the ásynjur: Sjöfn from sjafni 'affection', Lofn from lof 'praise, permission', Vár from várar 'vows' — qualities and states given names and errands of their own. The name answers the office exactly: the keeper of the queen's treasure-box is herself called Plenty.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᚠᚢᛚᛅ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which the name would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs f-u-l-a.

That rendering teaches the usual lesson: the runic row underspecifies what the manuscripts fix. The ᛚ rune writes both single and geminate l, so a carving cannot show the doubled consonant that defines the name's sound; and the name carries no final nominative -r, since Fulla is a feminine a-stem. No Viking-Age carving of the name is known — the runic form is a normalized reconstruction, and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Eddas, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈful.la/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: FULL-ah — but with the vowel of put, not pool, and the l genuinely doubled, two even syllables with the weight on the first.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Fulla, Frigg's handmaid; from fullr, 'full'
  • Younger Futhark — ᚠᚢᛚᛅ, the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Old High German — Volla, Friia's sister in the Second Merseburg Charm, widely read as the same figure
  • Proto-Germanic — *fullaz, 'full' — the ancestor of English 'full', German 'voll', and Norse fullr
  • Owned form — none; the temple claims no domain

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. The u is short and rounded. And the double l is a true geminate — held twice as long as a single l, the one feature of the name no plain spelling can show.

Mythology

Fulla has no saga of her own: her mythology is four precise notices, and every one of them places her at Frigg's side.

The Keeper of the Casket (Gylfaginning 35)

Snorri's catalogue of the ásynjur gives Fulla her entire portrait at once: she is a virgin, goes with her hair loose and a golden band about her head, carries Frigg's eski — the queen's little box — looks after her footwear, and shares her secret counsels. The three duties descend in rank and rise in intimacy: the box is treasure, the shoes are service, the counsels are trust itself. She is named immediately after Gná, Frigg's errand-rider, and the pairing has stuck: where Gná carries the queen's words out through the worlds, Fulla keeps the queen's words that never leave the hall.

The Warning to Geirröðr (Grímnismál, prose)

Her one errand in the Poetic Edda is given in prose. Óðinn and Frigg had quarrelled over their foster-sons: Frigg accused King Geirröðr, Óðinn's favourite, of being so stingy he tortured his guests, and Óðinn laid a wager and set off disguised to test him. Frigg sent Fulla to Geirröðr ahead of him, to warn the king to beware of a sorcerer coming to bewitch him — with the token that no dog, however fierce, would leap at the man. The warning is the trap: Geirröðr seized the stranger, named Grímnir, and tortured him between two fires, and out of that fire the god spoke the whole catalogue of the worlds. Fulla appears only to deliver the message and vanish — but the wager, the torture, and the poem's entire revelation turn on her errand.

The Ring from Hel (Gylfaginning 49)

When Hermóðr rode nine nights to ransom Baldr and came home with the tidings, he brought tokens from the dead: Baldr sent back the ring Draupnir to Óðinn, and Nanna — Baldr's wife, who had died of grief on his pyre — sent gifts out of Hel to the two women of Frigg's hall: a linen headdress for Frigg, and a finger-ring for Fulla. When a dead goddess thinks of Ásgarðr's household, she thinks of the queen and of the queen's handmaid by name.

Volla, Friia's Sister (Second Merseburg Charm)

The one continental witness is the Second Merseburg Charm, the only pagan theogonic text in Old High German: Phol and Wodan rode to the wood, Balder's foal wrenched its foot, and the goddesses chant over it in sequence — Sinthgunt, Sunna her sister; Friia, Volla her sister; and at last Wodan, who knows how, and the limb is knit bone to bone and blood to blood. Since Jacob Grimm, Volla — Friia's sister — has widely been read as Fulla: the name is the exact Old High German form of the same word, and the charm puts her, precisely as the Edda does, at Friia's right hand. The identification is argued, not proven — but if it holds, Fulla is one of the very few Norse goddesses with a continental footprint centuries older than the Icelandic books.

Symbols & Iconography

Fulla's iconography is the iconography of trust — Snorri fixes her appearance and office in a single sentence, and the tradition never adds to it:

  • The eski (little casket) — Frigg's portable treasure-box, entrusted to the hand that never lets it go
  • The golden hair-band — the gold snood about her loose hair: unmarried freedom dressed in plenty
  • The loose hair itself — the virgin's unbound hair, the visible sign of her status in a hall of married goddesses
  • Frigg's shoes — the humblest duty in the catalogue, and the surest sign of a servant admitted to every chamber
  • The finger-ring from Hel — Nanna's gift: a token from the dead addressed, of all the ásynjur, to her

Archaeology & Evidence

Fulla is an entirely textual goddess: no cult site, toponym, votive deposit, or inscription names her, and the runic corpus is silent — unsurprising for a figure the sources place inside the queen's household rather than in the public cult. Her witnesses are manuscripts. The Grímnismál prose survives in the Codex Regius (c. 1270); her portrait and her ring come with the Prose Edda codices of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries; and her name recurs in the þulur, the metrical name-lists appended to Skáldskaparmál. The one continental witness is the Second Merseburg Charm, copied into a theological manuscript at Fulda in the tenth century and now in the Merseburg cathedral library — its Volla the only attestation of the figure outside Iceland and, if the identification holds, the oldest by centuries.

Realm & Domain

Fulla's domain is Abundance, Secrets — and the sources earn both terms of it.

Abundance

Her name is the abstract noun of fullr, 'full': Plenty given a person, a golden band, and a casket to carry — the goddess in whose keeping the queen's portable wealth actually lives.

Secrets

She shares Frigg's leyniráðagørðir, the queen's secret deliberations: of the three duties in her portrait, the counsels are the one that admits her past every door.

Fidelity and Service

The casket and the shoes are the badges of the trusted chamber-servant — the lowest duties of the hall held, in her case, at the highest level of confidence.

The Queen's Hall

Every notice the tradition keeps of her places her at Frigg's side: named beside Gná in the catalogue of the ásynjur, sent ahead on the queen's errand to Geirröðr, and remembered beside the queen herself when Nanna's gifts come back from Hel.

Across Cultures

Fulla's cross-border life runs through one word and one relationship. The word is fullr: Proto-Germanic fullaz gives English 'full', German 'voll', Gothic fulls, and Old Norse Fulla — a goddess whose name is the ordinary adjective turned abstract noun. The relationship is the one the Merseburg Charm preserves: Volla beside Friia, as Fulla beside Frigg — the standing reading since Grimm, with Simek registering both the fit and the doubt. The deeper question is what kind of being she is: Gylfaginning's catalogue is full of abstract-noun goddesses — Sjöfn, Lofn, Vár — and scholars have long suspected that Fulla, Gná, and their sisters are facets of Frigg herself, the queen's plenitude and far-ranging given names and errands of their own. Within the corpus, her closest kin are the figures of Frigg's household: Frigg herself, Nanna, who sends her the ring, and the other ásynjur of the catalogue, among them Syn, who guards the door, and Eir, the best of physicians.

Cultural Legacy

Fulla's afterlife is scholarly and liturgical rather than popular. Grimm devoted sustained attention to her and Volla in Teutonic Mythology, and every handbook since — Simek, Orchard, Lindow — keeps her as the standing example of the abstract-noun ásynja: the goddess whose name tells you exactly what she is. Her image — loose hair, golden band, the queen's casket on her arm — became the fixed picture of the trusted northern handmaid, and she enters nearly every retelling of the Baldr cycle by virtue of one finger-ring. Modern Heathen and Ásatrú practice keeps her at Frigg's side as a goddess of plenty, household trust, and well-kept secrets. She never became a pop-culture figure; she remained what the Edda made her — the most trusted person in the queen's hall.

A Meditation

Fulla is the goddess of what is held in trust. Every detail of her portrait is a form of custody: the casket she carries but does not own, the shoes she tends but does not wear, the counsels she shares but does not speak. Even her name works this way — Fullness is not a thing she does but a thing she keeps. The tradition gives her no adventure because her office is the adventure's opposite: Gná rides out with the queen's words; Fulla stays, and what is entrusted to her stays with her. Nanna's ring from Hel is the tradition's own verdict — when the dead send gifts home, they send one to the keeper. In a mythology that ends in fire and betrayal, Fulla is the quiet counterweight: the proof that some things, once entrusted, are simply kept.

The Unicode Restoration

Fulla is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Norse Fulla carries none of these. The manuscripts write five plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the goddess; it is an honest report about her name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Fulla mean? The traditional gloss is "Bountiful" — from Old Norse fullr, 'full', making the name 'Fullness, Plenty': an abstract noun worn as a personal name, like the love-named ásynjur Sjöfn and Lofn.

Which tradition does Fulla belong to? Fulla is catalogued in the Norse pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon.

Why is Fulla classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the goddess's standing is beside the point.

Is fulla.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

What does Fulla actually do in the sources? Three things: she carries Frigg's casket, shoes, and secret counsels (Gylfaginning 35); she carries Frigg's warning to King Geirröðr, setting Grímnismál in motion; and she receives Nanna's finger-ring from Hel (Gylfaginning 49). The Second Merseburg Charm's Volla, Friia's sister, is widely — though not universally — read as the same figure.
