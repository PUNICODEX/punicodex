The hidden history behind Myrddin

Every reader knows Merlin — the wizard with the tower, the dragons, and Arthur at his elbow. Almost no one meets Myrddin: the Welsh original, a prophet who went mad at a northern battle in 573 and fled into the Caledonian forest to live as a wild man, grieving his dead lord and speaking the fate of Britain to an apple tree. There is no diacritic to rescue here — Middle Welsh wrote the name in plain ASCII — but there is an entire figure to restore, and this post walks the full record: the name's descent from a Roman sea-fort, the two Merlins the medieval tradition itself counted, the wild man's poems, and the honest ending — the plain-ASCII myrddin.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Myrddin (myrddin) — Seer, Wild Man · Merlin — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Seer, Wild Man". The name's traditional gloss is "Merlin", because Myrddin is the original Welsh form of the name Geoffrey of Monmouth Latinized as Merlinus.

Myrddin is the prophet of the British Old North: son of Morfryn in the Welsh Triads, brother of Gwenddydd, and — after the battle of Arfderydd in 573, where his lord Gwenddolau son of Ceidio fell — a madman in Coed Celyddon, the Caledonian forest, living from the woodland's gifts and uttering the laments and prophecies preserved in the Black Book of Carmarthen. Geoffrey's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) and Vita Merlini (c. 1150) carried him into Latin and grafted him onto the Arthurian world; the romances turned the prophet into the enchanter the world now knows.

PuniCodex restores the name as Myrddin — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Middle Welsh writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the prophet. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII myrddin.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in Middle Welsh as Myrddin, and its origin is one of the neatest reversals in Celtic philology. Geoffrey of Monmouth loved the folk etymology: Caerfyrddin — Carmarthen — was for him Kaermerdin, "the city of Merlin", and he built his Merlinus out of it. The scholarly account runs the arrow the other way. The personal name derives from the place-name, and the place-name descends from the Roman fort at Carmarthen, Moridunum — mori- 'sea' + dūnon 'fort' — the "sea-fortress" attested in the Antonine Itinerary. On the standard account (Jackson; Jarman), the prophet is at bottom "the man of Moridunum": a fortress remembered as a person.

The medieval tradition also counted the name's bearer twice. Gerald of Wales, writing around 1191, distinguishes Merlinus Ambrosius — the boy prophet of Vortigern's tower — from Merlinus Celidonius or Sylvester, "the Caledonian", the wild man of the forest. Welsh keeps the same doubleness: Myrddin Emrys for the Ambrosian, Myrddin Wyllt for the Wild.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Middle Welsh in the Latin alphabet as Myrddin — attested in the Black Book of Carmarthen (Aberystwyth, NLW Peniarth MS 1, c. 1250), the earliest surviving manuscript in Welsh, and in the Red Book of Hergest (Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, c. 1382–1410).

Two details of the orthography repay attention. The digraph dd writes the voiced dental fricative [ð] — the soft "th" of English "this" — and y writes two different vowels depending on position: the clear [ə] of the stressed penult and the high central [ɨ] of the final syllable. Both are conventions of Welsh spelling, not characters the ASCII alphabet lacks — which is exactly why the name sits in Tier 2. The manuscript form and the keyboard form are the same seven letters.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈmər.ðɨn/ — Modern Welsh.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: MUR-thin — two short beats, stress on the first syllable (the Welsh penult), the dd soft as in "this", and never the three-syllable "MER-lin" the Latin form made famous.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Middle Welsh — Myrddin, son of Morfryn; also Myrddin Wyllt, "the Wild"
  • Medieval Latin — Merlinus, Geoffrey of Monmouth's Latinization; Merlinus Ambrosius and Merlinus Celidonius in Gerald of Wales
  • Name origin — Caerfyrddin (Carmarthen), from Roman Moridunum, "sea-fort"
  • The world's form — Merlin, the French and English descendant that eclipsed the original

Mythology

Myrddin's mythology divides into a northern dossier and a Latin one, and the medieval tradition itself already kept them apart.

The Battle of Arfderydd (Annales Cambriae; Trioedd Ynys Prydein)

The Annales Cambriae enter under 573 the battle of Arfderydd, fought between the sons of Eliffer and Gwenddolau son of Ceidio. The Welsh Triads count it among the futile battles of the Island of Britain — brought about, in the triad's famous understatement, for a lark's nest — and record the consequence that founds the legend: Gwenddolau fell, and Myrddin son of Morfryn became mad.

The Laments of the Black Book (Yr Afallennau; Yr Oianau)

The Black Book of Carmarthen gives the wild man his own voice. In Yr Afallennau, "The Apple Trees", Myrddin addresses the sweet tree that shelters him, laments Gwenddolau dead at Arfderydd, grieves the warrior-company he has lost, and hides from Rhydderch's men; Yr Oianau keeps the same outcast voice calling through the forest, prophecy threaded through lament. These poems made Myrddin the patron and mouthpiece of the whole Welsh prophetic genre, the brud.

The Conversation with Gwenddydd (Cyfoesi Myrddin a Gwenddydd)

The Red Book of Hergest preserves the tradition's most human document: Gwenddydd — Rhydderch's wife and Myrddin's sister — questions her brother across the gulf his madness has opened, mourning the mead-feast companion he was, and he answers with the fate of Wales, the prophecy breaking against the grief. Geoffrey's Latin keeps her as Ganieda — and at the end of the Vita Merlini, she takes up the prophetic gift herself.

The Vita Merlini (Geoffrey of Monmouth)

Geoffrey's poem of c. 1150 is the fullest biography: the madness at a northern battle; the forest life among roots, fruit, and snow; the capture by Rhydderch's men; the laughter that is always prophecy — including the boy's foretold threefold death, from a rock, in a tree, and in the water, fulfilled when he falls from a cliff, catches in a tree, and dies with his head in the stream; the return to court riding a stag, driving the wild herds; the long discourse with Taliesin on winds, waters, fishes, and the world's making; and the retirement to the forest for good, the observatory left to Ganieda.

The Boy at Vortigern's Tower (Historia Brittonum; Historia Regum Britanniae)

Geoffrey's other Merlin came from another book. The ninth-century Historia Brittonum has the fatherless boy Ambrosius, brought to Vortigern to be sacrificed for his collapsing tower at Dinas Emrys, reveal the pool and the two dragons — the red of the Britons, the white of the Saxons — beneath its foundations. Geoffrey gave Nennius's boy Myrddin's name: Merlinus Ambrosius. The graft — tower-boy plus wild man — is the seed of the Arthurian enchanter.

Symbols & Iconography

Myrddin's attributes are not regalia but the furnishings of an exile — a tree, a forest, a wild mount, and the three elements of a foretold death:

  • The apple tree — the sweet tree of the Afallennau that shelters him and hears his laments for Gwenddolau
  • The forest of Celyddon — the Caledonian wood: madness, exile, and second sight in one landscape
  • The stag — the wild mount he rides to court in the Vita Merlini, driving the forest's herds before him
  • Rock, tree, and water — the three elements of the threefold death, fulfilled despite every precaution
  • Gwenddydd's questions — the sister's dialogue: prophecy as the speech left between the mad and those who loved them

Archaeology & Evidence

Myrddin is a textual figure, and his witnesses are among the most precisely identified in Welsh letters: the Black Book of Carmarthen (c. 1250), the earliest Welsh manuscript, for the Afallennau and Oianau; the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1382–1410) for the Cyfoesi; a single thirteenth-century manuscript, Cotton Vespasian E.iv, for the complete Vita Merlini. The landscape behind the texts is real: Arfderydd is traditionally identified with Arthuret near Longtown in Cumbria; Caerfyrddin overlays the excavated Roman fort of Moridunum, attested in the Antonine Itinerary; and Dinas Emrys, the Gwynedd hillfort of the tower legend, yielded post-Roman occupation material in Savory's excavations of 1954–56. No inscription names Myrddin — Welsh tradition kept its prophets in verse and its dead in stone.

Realm & Domain

Myrddin's domain is Seer, Wild Man — two words that name the whole of his legend and the whole of its price.

Seer

He is the patron of the Welsh prophetic genre: the Black Book poems put the fate of Britain in his mouth, Geoffrey's Prophetiae Merlini circulated for centuries as the most copied political prophecy of the Latin Middle Ages, and every later Welsh vaticination quietly claimed his authority.

Wild Man

The tradition is exact about the price: madness at Arfderydd, exile in the forest, food from the forest floor, speech addressed to a tree. Myrddin Wyllt is the type-figure — the seer who had to leave the human settlement before the future would speak to him.

The Two in One

The Vita Merlini holds the pair together: the mad king dragged to court whose laughter is always prophecy, riding home on a stag at the head of the woodland herds — the only sovereignty he can still keep.

Across Cultures

Myrddin's clearest cross-border witness is northern: Jocelyn of Furness's Life of St Kentigern (c. 1185) knows a mad prophet at Rhydderch Hael's court named Lailoken, who foretold his own threefold death, and a fragmentary Latin life of "Merlin the Wild" tells the same penitence and triple end — stones, a stake, and the Tweed. Nikolai Tolstoy argued Lailoken and Myrddin Wyllt are two records of one north-British figure. Ireland offers no namesake but a perfect parallel: Suibhne Geilt, the king driven mad by battle in Buile Shuibhne, who lives in trees and speaks in verse — the same type, never the same name. Within Wales his truest kin is Taliesin, the other great prophetic poet, with whom the Vita Merlini pairs him in discourse; and the deepest split is internal — Gerald of Wales's two Merlins, fused into one enchanter only by the later romances.

Cultural Legacy

No Celtic figure has had a stranger second life. Geoffrey's Prophetiae Merlini circulated separately from the Historia and became the most copied political prophecy of the Latin Middle Ages, mined for omens by kings and rebels. The romances then turned the prophet into the enchanter: by the thirteenth-century Vulgate cycle Merlin is Arthur's maker and the Lady of the Lake's prisoner. Lady Charlotte Guest's Mabinogion (1838–49) carried the Welsh roots back into English view, and Jarman's scholarship rebuilt the northern prophet behind the wizard. In Welsh memory the original never left: Caerfyrddin still bears the folk etymology, and the Black Book's Myrddin remains the figure every later Welsh prophecy quietly pretended to be.

A Meditation

Myrddin is the figure who paid for knowledge with the ordinary world. Every version of him loses something before he sees: a lord, a battle, his reason, his hall. The tradition is honest about the transaction — the prophecy is real, and so is the madness, and the same event, the slaughter at Arfderydd, gives him both. His sister comes to the edge of the forest to ask what will happen; he tells her, and stays. That is the shape of the gift — the future, at the price of the present. And his name tells the same story in miniature: not a theonym but a place-name, Caerfyrddin, the sea-fort, given to a man. The wild prophet carries a fortress in his name — the ordered world he was driven out of, remembered every time anyone calls him.

The Unicode Restoration

Myrddin is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Middle Welsh Myrddin carries none of these; the digraph dd is orthography, not a lost character. The manuscripts write seven plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the prophet; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Myrddin mean? The lexicon's gloss is "Merlin", because Myrddin is the original Welsh form of the name Geoffrey of Monmouth Latinized as Merlinus. The name itself derives from the place-name Caerfyrddin (Carmarthen), which descends from the Roman Moridunum, "sea-fort" — the prophet is, at bottom, the man of the sea-fortress.

Which tradition does Myrddin belong to? Myrddin is catalogued in the Celtic pantheon of the PuniCodex lexicon — specifically the Welsh tradition of the Old North.

Why is Myrddin classified as Tier 2? Because the normalized Middle Welsh form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter — so the restoration preserves nothing the ASCII form loses. The rule is mechanical; the prophet's standing is beside the point.

Is myrddin.com a working domain? It is a registered domain, but it is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection. The project does not own it, claims no control over it, and watches it for release.

Are Merlin and Myrddin the same figure? Geoffrey of Monmouth Latinized Myrddin as Merlinus, but the medieval tradition itself counted two Merlins — the Ambrosian boy of Vortigern's tower and the Caledonian wild man — and Geoffrey grafted them together. The Welsh Myrddin Wyllt, the mad prophet of the forest, is the original the world-famous wizard grew from.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines inherit whatever spelling the web normalizes — and for a plain-ASCII name, normalization is not the risk; confusion is. Myrddin the Welsh prophet, Merlin the Latinized wizard, Merlin the film character, myrddin.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps them sorted. This temple is the lexicon's fixed point for the wild man of the Caledonian forest — tier, sources, etymology and all — whether or not the address ever comes home.
