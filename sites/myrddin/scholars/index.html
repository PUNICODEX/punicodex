01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Myrddin (myrddin) ​— Seer, Wild ‌Man · Merlin ‌— is the ​original Welsh form ​of the name ​the world knows ​as Merlin: the ​prophet of the ‌British Old North ​who went mad ​at the ‌battle of Arfderydd ​in 573, fled ‌into Coed Celyddon, ​the Caledonian forest, ‌and lived there ​as a wild ​man, lamenting his ​dead lord Gwenddolau ​and uttering the ​prophecies that made ‌him the canonical ​voice of Welsh ‌vaticination.[1]

The Welsh ​sources give him ‌a family and ​a grief: he ‌is Myrddin son ​of Morfryn in ‌the Triads, brother ​of Gwenddydd — ​wife of Rhydderch ‌— and ‌the speaker of ‌the Black Book ​of Carmarthen's great ​laments, the Afallennau ‌and the Oianau, ​addressed from hiding ​to the apple ‌tree that shelters ​him.[2] Geoffrey of ​Monmouth carried him ‌into Latin as ​Merlinus: the Historia ‌Regum Britanniae (c. ​1136) made him ​the boy prophet ​of Vortigern's tower ​and the dragons, ‌and the Vita ‌Merlini (c. 1150) ​gave the wild ‌man his fullest ​biography — ​the forest life, ‌the threefold death ‌foretold to a ‌boy, the stag ‌he rides to ​court, the long ‌discourse with Taliesin, ​and the sister ​Ganieda who succeeds ​him in prophecy.[1]

‌PuniCodex restores the ​name as Myrddin ​— identical to ​the ASCII input, ‌because normalized Middle ​Welsh writes no ​diacritic in this ​name. The mechanical ‌rule therefore places ​it in Tier ‌2: nothing distinctive ​is lost ‌in transit, a ​statement about orthography ‌and never about ‌the prophet. No ​domain is claimed: ​the plain-ASCII myrddin.com ​is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.[3]

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Middle ‌Welsh as Myrddin, the ‌form underlying Geoffrey of ​Monmouth's Latin Merlinus ​— the spelling that ​carried the figure into ​French, English, and ​world literature.[1] The scholarly ‌account of the name's ​origin reverses the folk ​etymology Geoffrey loved. ‌Geoffrey connects his Merlin ​with Carmarthen — Kaermerdin, ‌the 'city of Merlin' ​— but the ‌direction of naming runs ​the other way: the ​personal name derives ​from the place-name Caerfyrddin, ​and Caerfyrddin descends from ​the Roman name of ‌the fort at ​Carmarthen, Moridunum, 'sea-fort' (mori- ‌'sea' + dūnon 'fort'), ​attested in the Antonine ‌Itinerary. On Jackson's ​and Jarman's account the ‌prophet is, at bottom, ​'the man of ‌Moridunum' — a fortress ​remembered as a person.[2]

​The medieval tradition itself ‌split the name's ‌bearer in two. Gerald ‌of Wales, writing around ​1191, distinguishes Merlinus Ambrosius ​— the boy ‌prophet of Vortigern's tower, ​whose story Geoffrey took ​from the fatherless ‌Ambrosius of the Historia ​Brittonum — from Merlinus ​Celidonius or Sylvester, 'the ‌Caledonian' and 'the ​man of the woods', ‌the northern prophet who ​went mad after battle. ​Welsh preserves the ​same doubleness in the ​by-forms Myrddin Emrys (the ‌Ambrosian) and Myrddin ‌Wyllt (the Wild).[3]

The ​ASCII form myrddin loses ‌nothing, because the normalized ​Middle Welsh spelling ​is already plain Latin ‌letters; the digraph dd ‌for the soft 'th' ‌[ð] is orthography, ‌not a lost character. ​The restoration is therefore ‌a matter of ​canonical reference, not rescued ​diacritics, and the name ​is classified Tier 2 ‌by the project's ​mechanical rule.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • m ​→ M — Same, ‌capitalized
  • y ​→ y — Same

​- r → r ​— Same

  • ‌d → d — ​Same
  • d → ‌d — Same
  • ​i → i ‌— Same
  • n ​→ n — Same

‌The project holds no ‌domain for this ​name: myrddin.com is registered ​to a third party ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is watched ‌for release. The temple ‌lives at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈmər.ðɨn/ ​— Modern ​Welsh.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- My- ​— Bilabial ‌[m] with ​the Welsh ​clear y, ‌a central ​[ə] like ‌the 'uh' ​of 'about'; ‌stressed, because ​Welsh stress ​falls on ​the penult
​- -rddin ​— Tapped ‌[r]; dd, ​the voiced ‌dental fricative ​[ð] of ‌English 'this'; ​final unstressed ‌-in with ​the high ‌central [ɨ] ​that Welsh ​writes y ‌in the ‌last syllable

‌For the ​modern speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation is: ​'MUR-thin' — ​two short ‌beats, the ​dd soft ​as in ‌'this', never hardened, ​and never ‌the three-syllable ​'MER-lin' the ​Latin form ​made famous.

​Kindred and ‌historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • ‌Middle Welsh ​— Myrddin, ​son of ‌Morfryn; also ‌Myrddin Wyllt, ‌'the Wild'

‌- Medieval ​Latin — ‌Merlinus, Geoffrey ​of Monmouth's ​Latinization; Merlinus ​Ambrosius and ‌Merlinus Celidonius/Sylvester ​in Gerald ​of Wales
​- Name ‌origin — ​Caerfyrddin (Carmarthen), ​from Roman ​Moridunum, 'sea-fort'[2]

‌Myrddin is ​Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves ‌nothing ASCII ​loses: the ‌letters of ‌the manuscript ​form are ​the letters ​of the ​keyboard. The ‌capital alone ‌marks the ​temple form.

Sources

  1. Evans, D. Simon, A Grammar of Middle Welsh (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1964), on Welsh stress, the value of y, and the dd digraph.
  2. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on Moridunum > Caerfyrddin.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌Middle Welsh in ‌the Latin alphabet ​as Myrddin — ​attested Middle Welsh, ​c. 13th–14th c. ​in the surviving ​manuscripts, with the ‌underlying tradition reaching ​centuries earlier — ​in the Black ‌Book of Carmarthen, ​the Red Book ‌of Hergest, and ​the Welsh Triads.[1] ‌Middle Welsh was ​written in the ​Latin alphabet using ​the Insular script ​tradition; the spelling ​Myrddin requires no ‌diacritic.

The scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • The ​earliest continuous witnesses ‌are the Black ​Book of Carmarthen ‌(NLW Peniarth MS ​1, c. 1250) ‌and the Red ​Book of Hergest ​(Jesus College MS ‌111, c. 1382–1410); ‌the poems they ‌carry are substantially ​older than their ​copies.[1]
  • The ‌digraph dd writes ​the voiced dental ​fricative [ð], and ‌y writes two ​distinct vowels by ​position — the ‌clear [ə] of ​the stressed penult ‌and the high ​central [ɨ] of ​the final syllable; ​both are orthographic ​conventions of Welsh, ‌not characters the ‌ASCII alphabet lacks.[2]

​- Geoffrey of ‌Monmouth's Merlinus is ​a Latinization, not ​a separate attestation: ‌he attached the ‌name to Caerfyrddin, ‌and the modern ‌derivation runs from ​Moridunum, 'sea-fort', through ‌the place-name to ​the person.[3]

  • ​The Unicode restoration ​Myrddin is therefore ‌identical to the ASCII ​form apart from ​the capital; the ​temple form is ‌a canonical reference, ​not a rescued ​spelling.

Two cautions ​attach to the ‌record: the medieval ​tradition itself counted ‌two Merlins — ​the Ambrosian and ‌the Caledonian — ​and the 'two ‌names one wizard' ‌of modern fiction ​is a fusion, ​not a source;[4] ​and the folk ​etymology 'Myrddin's fortress' ‌runs backward — ‌the fortress named ​the man.[3]

Sources

  1. Jarman, A. O. H., 'Llyfr Du Caerfyrddin: The Black Book of Carmarthen', Proceedings of the British Academy 71 (1985), 333–56.
  2. Evans, D. Simon, A Grammar of Middle Welsh (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1964).
  3. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on Moridunum > Caerfyrddin.
  4. Gerald of Wales, Itinerarium Cambriae II.8, ed. J. F. Dimock, Giraldi Cambrensis Opera, Rolls Series 21, vol. 6 (London, 1868).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Myrddin is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌'Seer, Wild Man' ​— two ​words that ​name the ​whole of his ​legend and ‌the whole ​of his price.[1]

​### Seer

‌Myrddin is ​the patron ‌and mouthpiece of ​the Welsh ‌prophetic genre, ​the brud: the ​Black Book ​poems put ​the fate of ​Britain in ‌his mouth, ​Geoffrey's Prophetiae ‌Merlini — Historia ​Book VII ‌— circulated ​separately for centuries ‌as the ​most copied ‌political prophecy of ​the Latin ​Middle Ages, ‌and every ‌later Welsh vaticination ‌quietly claimed ​his authority.[2]

​### Wild Man

‌The same ​tradition is ​exact about ‌the price: madness ​at Arfderydd, ​exile in ‌Coed Celyddon, food ​from the ‌forest floor, ​speech addressed to ​a tree. ​The Welsh ​term is ‌wyllt — wild ‌— and ​Myrddin Wyllt ‌is the type-figure: ​the seer ​who had ‌to leave ‌the human settlement ‌before the ‌future would ​speak to him.[1]

‌### The ​Two in ​One

Geoffrey's Vita ​Merlini holds ‌the pair ​together in ​a single poem: ​the mad ‌king dragged ​to court who ​laughs at ​what no ‌one else can ​see — ‌and whose ​laughter is ‌always a prophecy ​— then ‌rides home ‌to the forest ​on a ​stag, driving ​the woodland ​herds before him ‌like a ‌shepherd of ​the wild.[1]

Sources

  1. Clarke, Basil, ed. and trans., Life of Merlin: Geoffrey of Monmouth, Vita Merlini (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1973).
  2. Jarman, A. O. H., 'The Merlin Legend and the Welsh Tradition of Prophecy', in The Arthur of the Welsh (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1991), 117–45.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Myrddin's ​attributes are ‌not regalia ‌but the ​furnishings ​of an ​exile — ​a tree, ​a ‌forest, a ​wild mount, ​and the ‌three elements ​of ‌a foretold ​death:[1]

  • ‌The apple ​tree ​— The ​sweet tree ​of the ​Afallennau ‌that shelters ​him and ‌hears his ​laments ‌for Gwenddolau: ​prophecy's one ‌roof in ​the ‌forest of ​Celyddon
  • ​The forest ‌of Celyddon ‌— ‌The Caledonian ​wood itself: ​madness, exile, ‌and ​second sight ​in a ‌single landscape

​- ​The stag ‌— The ​wild mount ‌he ​rides to ​Rhydderch's court ​in the ​Vita ‌Merlini, driving ‌the forest's ​herds before ‌him: ​the king ​become herdsman ‌of the ‌unowned

  • ‌Rock, ‌tree, and ​water — ‌The three ​elements ​of the ​threefold death ‌he prophesies ​for ​the boy ​at court, ‌fulfilled to ​the ​letter despite ​every precaution[2]

‌- Gwenddydd's ​questions ‌— The ​sister's dialogue ‌from the ​Red Book ‌of ‌Hergest: prophecy ​as the ​speech left ​between ​the mad ‌and those ‌who loved ​them[1]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Myrddin's mythology divides ​into a northern dossier and ‌a Latin one, and the ‌medieval tradition itself already kept ​them apart.[1]

The Battle ​of Arfderydd (Annales Cambriae; Trioedd ​Ynys Prydein)

The Annales Cambriae ​enter under 573 the battle ​of Arfderydd, fought between the ‌sons of Eliffer and Gwenddolau ​son of Ceidio.[2] The Welsh ​Triads count it among the ‌futile battles of the Island ​of Britain — brought about, ‌in the triad's famous understatement, ​for a lark's nest ‌— and record the consequence ​that founds the legend: Gwenddolau ​fell, and Myrddin son of ​Morfryn became mad.[3]

The ​Laments of the Black Book ​(Yr Afallennau; Yr Oianau)

The ‌earliest Welsh manuscript, the Black ​Book of Carmarthen (c. 1250), ‌preserves the wild man's own ​voice. In the Afallennau Myrddin ‌addresses the sweet apple tree ​that shelters him, laments Gwenddolau ‌dead at Arfderydd, grieves the ​warrior-company he has lost, and ‌hides from Rhydderch's men; the ​Oianau keeps the same outcast ​voice calling its mournful cry ‌through the forest, prophecy threaded ‌through lament.[4]

The Conversation ‌with Gwenddydd (Cyfoesi Myrddin a ​Gwenddydd)

In the Red Book ​of Hergest dialogue, Gwenddydd — ‌Rhydderch's wife and Myrddin's sister ​— questions her brother across ​the gulf his madness has ‌opened, mourning the mead-feast companion ​he was; he answers with ​the fate of Wales, and ‌the prophecy keeps breaking ​against grief.[4]

The Vita ‌Merlini (Geoffrey of Monmouth)

Geoffrey's ​poem of c. 1150 is ​the fullest biography: the madness ​at a northern battle; the ​life of the forest among ‌roots, fruit, and snow; the ‌capture by Rhydderch's men; the ​laughter that is always a ‌prophecy, including the boy's foretold ​threefold death — from a ​rock, in a tree, and ‌in the water — fulfilled ‌when he falls from a ‌cliff, catches in a tree, ‌and dies with his head ​in the stream; the return ‌to court riding a stag ​and driving the wild herds; ​the long discourse with Taliesin ​(Telgesinus) on winds, waters, fishes, ‌and the world's making; and ​the end, when Merlin gives ​his observatory to his sister ​Ganieda — Geoffrey's Gwenddydd — ‌who takes up the prophetic ​gift, and retires to the ​forest for good.[1]

The ​Boy at Vortigern's Tower ‌(Historia Brittonum; Historia Regum Britanniae)

​Geoffrey's other Merlin came from ‌another book. The ninth-century Historia ​Brittonum has the fatherless boy ‌Ambrosius, brought to Vortigern to ​be sacrificed for his collapsing ‌tower at Dinas Emrys, reveal ‌the pool and the two ​dragons — the red of ​the Britons, the white of ​the Saxons — beneath its ​foundations. Geoffrey gave Nennius's boy ‌Myrddin's name: Merlinus Ambrosius — ‌the graft from which the ​Arthurian enchanter grew.[5]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Myrddin's clearest ​cross-border witness ‌is northern, ‌not continental. ​Jocelyn of ​Furness's Life ​of St ​Kentigern (c. 1185) ​knows a ‌mad prophet ​at the ​court of ‌Rhydderch Hael ​named Lailoken, ‌who foretold ​his own threefold ‌death; a ​fragmentary Latin ​life of ​'Merlin the ​Wild' in ​the Cotton ‌collection tells the ​same penitence, ‌conversion by ​Kentigern, and ‌triple end ​— stones, ‌a stake, ​and the Tweed. ‌Tolstoy argued ​that Lailoken ​and Myrddin ‌Wyllt are ‌two records ‌of one ​north-British wild-man ​figure, and the ‌handbooks treat ​the kinship ​as real ‌and its ​exact shape ​unproven.[1]

The ‌Irish tradition offers ​no namesake ‌but a ​perfect parallel: ​Suibhne Geilt, ​the king ​driven mad ‌by battle in ‌Buile Shuibhne, ​who lives ‌in trees, ​speaks in ​verse, and ‌is watched ‌from the ‌court he left ‌— the ​same type, ‌the wild ​prophet as ​battle-survivor, with ​no shared ‌name.[2] Within Wales ​his truest ​kin is ​Taliesin, the ‌other great ​prophetic poet, ​with whom ​the Vita Merlini ‌pairs him ​in discourse; ‌and the ​deepest split ‌is internal ​— Gerald ‌of Wales's ‌two Merlins, the ​tower-boy Ambrosius ​and the ​wild Caledonian, ​whom the ‌romances later ‌fused into ​one enchanter.[3]

  1. Tolstoy, Nikolai, The Quest for Merlin (London: Hamish Hamilton, 1985); Jocelyn of Furness, Vita Sancti Kentigerni, ed. A. P. Forbes, Lives of S. Ninian and S. Kentigern (Edinburgh, 1874).
  2. O'Keeffe, J. G., ed. and trans., Buile Shuibhne (The Frenzy of Sweeney), Irish Texts Society 12 (London: Irish Texts Society, 1913).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

No Celtic ​figure has ‌had a ‌stranger second ​life. Geoffrey's ​Prophetiae Merlini ​— Historia ​Book VII ​— circulated ‌separately from ​the work ​that contained ‌it and ​became the ‌most copied ​political prophecy ‌of the ​Latin Middle ​Ages, mined ​for omens ​by kings ​and rebels ‌and furnished ​with learned ‌commentaries into ​the fifteenth ‌century.[1] The ​romances then ‌turned the ​prophet into ‌the enchanter: ​by the ​thirteenth-century Vulgate ‌cycle Merlin ‌is Arthur's ‌maker — ​arranger of ​Uther's night ‌at Tintagel, ​keeper of ​the Round ‌Table's founding, ​and prisoner ​of the ‌Lady of the ​Lake.

The ‌Welsh original ​never disappeared. ​Lady Charlotte ​Guest's Mabinogion ​translation (1838–49) ‌and the ‌nineteenth-century Arthurian ​revival carried ‌the Welsh ​roots back ​into English ‌view; Jarman's ‌scholarship rebuilt ‌the northern ‌prophet behind ​the wizard; ‌and Caerfyrddin ​— 'Myrddin's ​fortress', on ​the folk ‌etymology — ​still bears ​his name ​in every ‌address in ​Carmarthen.[2] Modern ​Druidry and ​the broader ‌Arthurian imagination ​now run ‌both figures ​at once: ‌Merlin the ​wizard of ‌the films, ‌and Myrddin ​Wyllt, patron ​of Welsh ​prophecy and ​of every ‌wild man ‌the forests ​still keep.[3]

  1. Eckhardt, Caroline D., ed., The Prophetia Merlini of Geoffrey of Monmouth: A Fifteenth-Century English Commentary (Cambridge, MA: Medieval Academy of America, 1982), introduction, on the prophecy's separate circulation.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Myrddin ​is a ‌textual figure, ‌and his ​witnesses are ​among the ​most ​precisely identified ​in Welsh ‌letters. The ​Afallennau and ​Oianau stand ‌in ​the Black ‌Book of ​Carmarthen (Aberystwyth, ‌NLW Peniarth ​MS 1, ​c. ​1250), the ​earliest surviving ​manuscript in ‌Welsh; the ​Cyfoesi ‌Myrddin a ​Gwenddydd stands ‌in the ​Red Book ‌of Hergest ​(Oxford, ‌Jesus College ​MS 111, ​c. 1382–1410); ‌Geoffrey's Vita ‌Merlini survives ‌complete ​in a ​single manuscript ‌(British Library, ​Cotton Vespasian ​E.iv, thirteenth ‌century).[1]

​The landscape ​behind the ‌texts is ​real and ‌partly recoverable. ​Arfderydd ​is traditionally ​identified with ​Arthuret near ‌Longtown in ‌Cumbria, in ​the ‌Old North ​the poems ​remember.[2] Caerfyrddin ‌(Carmarthen) overlays ‌the Roman ‌fort ‌of Moridunum, ​'sea-fort', attested ‌in the ​Antonine Itinerary ​and confirmed ​by ‌excavation — ​the place-name ​from which, ​on the ‌scholarly ​account, the ​prophet's name ​derives.[3] And ‌Dinas Emrys, ​the Gwynedd ‌hillfort ​where the ‌Historia Brittonum ​sets the ‌fatherless boy ‌and the ​dragons, ​yielded post-Roman ​occupation material ​in H. ‌N. Savory's ‌excavations of ​1954–56.[4]

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Myrddin has ​no Irish ‌namesake and ‌no Irish ​reflex — ​but he ​has a ​perfect Irish ​parallel, and ‌the parallel ​is one ​of the ‌glories of ​the early ‌Celtic literatures. ​Suibhne Geilt, ‌the king ​of Dál ​nAraidi in Buile ​Shuibhne ('The ​Frenzy of ​Sweeney'), is ‌cursed by ​St Ronan ‌and driven ​mad at ‌the battle ​of Mag ‌Rath: he ​flees the ‌field, lives ​in trees, ​eats watercress, ‌speaks in ‌verse of ‌great beauty, and ​is watched, ​pursued, and ‌grieved for ​by the ​court and ‌wife he ​left.[1] The ​type is ‌identical to ​Myrddin Wyllt's ‌— the ​warrior-king whom ​battle-madness makes ​a wild ​man and ‌a poet ‌— and ​the comparison is ‌standard in ​the handbooks; ​what the ‌two traditions ‌never did ‌was share ‌a name.[2]

​The Irish ‌material also ​sharpens what ​is distinctive ​in the ‌Welsh. Suibhne's ​madness is ​a saint's ​curse; Myrddin's ‌is grief at ​a lord's ​death. Suibhne's ​verse is ‌lyric; Myrddin's ​is prophecy. ‌The Irish ​tale resolves ‌toward penitence ​and the ‌Church; the ‌Welsh material ​resolves toward ​the brud, ​the prophetic ​future of ‌the nation. ‌Same tree, ​different fruit.[3]

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Myrddin is ​a Welsh ‌figure before he ‌is anything else, ​and the ​Welsh dossier is ​unusually complete. ​Its spine is ​the Black ‌Book of Carmarthen ​(c. 1250), the ​earliest manuscript ‌in Welsh: the ​Afallennau, in ‌which the wild ​man addresses ‌the apple tree ​and laments Gwenddolau ​dead at ​Arfderydd, and the ​Oianau, the ​outcast's mournful cry ‌through the forest ​— laments ‌threaded with prophecy ​that made ‌Myrddin the patron ​of the ‌whole brud genre.[1] ​The Red Book ‌of Hergest ​adds the Cyfoesi ​Myrddin a ‌Gwenddydd ei chwaer, ‌the dialogue ‌with his sister; ​the Welsh Triads ​add the ‌frame — Myrddin ​son of ​Morfryn, driven mad ‌in the futile ​battle fought ​'for a lark's ‌nest'; and ​the Annales Cambriae ‌date the ​battle to 573.[2]

​Around that Welsh ​core the ​Latin tradition built ‌the world-famous ‌figure. Geoffrey of ​Monmouth's Historia Regum ‌Britanniae (c. ​1136) gave Myrddin's ​name to ‌the fatherless boy ‌of the ‌Historia Brittonum and ‌attached him to ​Caerfyrddin; the ‌Vita Merlini (c. ​1150) wrote ​the wild man's ​Latin biography; ‌and Gerald of ​Wales, a generation ​later, recorded ​that the tradition ‌already counted ​two Merlins — ​Ambrosius and the ​Caledonian.[3] The ‌prophetic poetry of ​Wales kept ‌his name as ​its license: ‌from Taliesin, with ​whom the Vita ‌pairs him, ‌to Ceridwen's cauldron ​of inspiration, ​the awen tradition ​and the ​Myrddin tradition are ‌the two springs ‌of Welsh ​vatic verse.[4]

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Myrddin, and ‌none ​was to ​be ​expected. The ​early inscribed ​stones ‌of Wales ​— the ​Latin ‌and ogham ​memorial stones ‌of ​roughly the ‌fifth ​to seventh ​centuries — ​record ​personal names ​in funerary ‌formulae, ​not prophets ‌or theonyms, ​and ‌no reading ​of ‌Myrddin has ​ever been ‌proposed ​for any ​of them.[1]

‌The ‌name's one ‌epigraphic-adjacent witness ​is ​the place-name ‌it ​descends from: ​Moridunum, 'sea-fort', ‌the ​Roman fort ​beneath Carmarthen, ‌is ​attested in ‌the Antonine ​Itinerary ​— the ​road-book ​of the ‌empire — ‌and ​the identification ‌is confirmed ​by ​the excavated ‌Roman remains ‌at ‌Caerfyrddin.[2] The ‌itinerary ​does not ‌name the ​prophet; ​it names ​the fortress ‌that ​would, centuries ​later, supply ​his ‌name. Beyond ​that ​single thread ​the record ‌is ​manuscript alone, ‌which is ​characteristic: ‌Welsh tradition ​kept its ‌prophets ‌in verse ​and ​its dead ​in stone, ​and ‌Myrddin belongs ‌to the ​verse.[3]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Myrddin is ​the figure ‌who paid ‌for knowledge ​with the ​ordinary world. ​Every version ​of him ​loses something ‌before he sees: ​a lord, ​a battle, ‌his reason, ​his hall. ‌The tradition ​is strikingly ‌honest about ​the transaction ​— the prophecy ​is real, ​and so ​is the ‌madness, and ​the same ‌event, the ​slaughter at ‌Arfderydd, gives him ​both.[1] He ‌is the ​answer Celtic ‌literature gives ​to the ​question of ‌what a ‌seer is: ‌not a priest ​with a ​technique but ‌a survivor ​whom grief ​has put ‌outside the ​human settlement, ​close enough ‌to the trees ​that the ‌future can ​get a ​word in. ​His sister ​comes to ‌the edge ‌of the ​forest to ask ‌what will ​happen; he ​tells her, ‌and stays. ‌That is ‌the shape ‌of the ​gift — ‌the future, at ​the price ​of the ​present.

And ‌his name ​tells the ​same story ​in miniature. ‌It is not ​a theonym ​but a ​place-name: Caerfyrddin, ‌the sea-fort, ​Moridunum, given ‌to a ​man. The ‌wild prophet ​carries a fortress ‌in his ‌name — ​the ordered ​world he ​was driven ​out of, ‌remembered every ‌time anyone ​calls him.
