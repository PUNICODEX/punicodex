Mythology

Stories of Xīwángmǔ

Xīwángmǔ's myths trace an arc no other Chinese deity matches: from wilderness terror to the most gracious sovereign of the Taoist heavens. Each age kept something of the last, so that the serene hostess of the Peach Banquet still carries the teeth and tail of the mountain power she once was.

Shan Hai Jing

The Fearsome Portrait

Her oldest portrait is the strangest. In the Classic of Mountains and Seas, compiled from Warring States to Han materials, the Queen Mother "has the form of a human, the tail of a leopard, the teeth of a tiger, and is good at whistling; her tangled hair is bound with a sheng headdress" (ch. 2, Xishan jing). A later stratum adds her office — she "presides over the calamities of heaven and the five punishments" (ch. 16, Dahuang xi jing), seated on Mount Kūnlún with three azure birds to fetch her food. She is not yet a queen in any courtly sense. She is the numinous danger of the far west itself: the lands where the sun goes down, source of plague and of everything the settled world cannot control. Later ages would dress her in silk and seat her among goddesses — but the earliest text keeps her wild, and the wildness never entirely left the throne.

Huainanzi

The Elixir for Hòuyì

It is from Xīwángmǔ that immortality flows. The Huainanzi records that Hòuyì, the archer who shot down the nine excess suns, "begged the elixir of undying from the Queen Mother of the West" — and that his wife Héng'é stole it and fled to the moon, where she became the moon's goddess (her name later softened to Cháng'é under a Han emperor's name-taboo). Versions disagree about the theft — greed, fear, or rescue of the elixir from unworthy hands — but they agree on the source: no one in the Chinese cosmos rises beyond death except through the Queen Mother's treasury. The same storehouse reappears in every later system: her peaches ripen once in three thousand years; her banquet seats the assembled immortals; her garden is raided in every retelling of the Monkey King. Sovereignty over the boundary between life and death was her first office and remained her last — whether she wore a leopard's tail or a phoenix crown.

Book of Han

The Movement of 3 BCE

The Queen Mother was never only a goddess of mirrors and palace ladies. In 3 BCE — recorded in the Book of Han (ch. 11, Annals of Emperor Ai) — her cult ignited across the empire: crowds in more than twenty commanderies passed her tally-tokens (chóu) from hand to hand, sang and danced in the streets, wore her tokens, and converged on the capitals "thousands strung together", awaiting the fulfilment of her promise. The movement alarmed the court enough to be entered in the dynastic history — one of the earliest documented popular religious movements in China, three centuries before the organized Daoist church. The tallies were small pieces of wood, and their passing was the whole theology: a pledge, from the goddess of the west, that the world as it was could not last. The chroniclers record the phenomenon and not the promise. The crowds, clearly, knew what they were waiting for.

Journey to the West

The Theft of the Peaches

Once in three thousand years the Peaches of Immortality ripen in the Queen Mother's orchard, and once in three thousand years she hosts the pántáohuì, the Peach Banquet, for the assembled immortals, gods, and buddhas of the three religions. In Journey to the West (ch. 5) the task of guarding the orchard is given, with fatal optimism, to Sūn Wùkōng — who samples the fruit systematically, dining on the 3,000-year, 6,000-year, and 9,000-year trees alike, and then crashes the feast itself in a disguise borrowed from a serving goddess. It is one of literature's great heists, and its comedy rests on real theology: the peaches are the most tightly held treasure in heaven, ripening on a schedule longer than recorded history, and the Queen Mother is their unanswerable custodian. The Monkey King's appetite did what no god could. It made her orchard — and her three-thousand-year patience — the most famous garden in Chinese fiction.

Mu Tianzi Zhuan

The Hostess at the Jasper Pool

Before she was a Taoist sovereign she was a royal hostess. The Mu Tianzi Zhuan (Account of King Mu) — a Warring States text unearthed in 281 CE from a Wei royal tomb — sends King Mu of Zhou to her Jasper Pool (Yáochí) at the western edge of the world. There she receives the traveling king as an equal: she sings to him in the text's archaic verse — "white clouds in the sky, the road long, mountains between us; may you not die, and come again" — and he answers in kind before turning his eight-horse team east. The scene is her earliest humane portrait, and it fixed two things: the title by which Taiwanese temples still know her, Golden Mother of the Jasper Pool, and her oldest character — not empress, not bureaucrat, but the hostess at the end of the road, inviting the greatest traveler of the age to live long enough to come back. Every later banquet descends from that first cup of wine at the edge of the world.

02

The Western Paradise

The Peaches of Immortality, Mount Kūnlún, and the Taming of a Wilderness Goddess

Xīwángmǔ, the Queen Mother of the West, has the strangest biography of any great Chinese goddess: in the oldest text she is a tiger-toothed power of plague on Mount Kūnlún; by the Han she is enthroned in the western paradise; by the Tang she hosts the Peach Banquet and guards the elixir. The wilderness was never banished from her — it was enthroned.

Mount Kūnlún

Her paradise stands on Mount Kūnlún at the western edge of the world, the axis where heaven leans down to meet the wild earth.

The Peaches of Immortality

The pántáo ripen once every three thousand years; to taste one is to step outside death's jurisdiction — which is why the Monkey King could not resist them.

Tiger's Teeth, Leopard's Tail

Her archaic form keeps its claws: the Shān Hǎi Jīng gives her a tiger's teeth and a leopard's tail, the regalia of a mistress of plague and calamity.

Sovereign of Yin and the West

In mature Taoist theology she rules yin, the west, and all goddesses, balanced against Dōngwánggōng's yang and east — the two poles of a complete cosmos.

Sacred Symbols

The shèng headdress Her ancient insignia of authority — the distinctive headdress she wears even in her oldest, wildest images
Tiger's teeth and leopard's tail The zoomorphic vestiges of her primordial form, marking her dominion over plague and calamity
The Peaches of Immortality Sovereignty over death itself — fruit that ripens once in three thousand years and frees the eater from mortality
Mount Kūnlún The western paradise at the edge of the world, where her palace and orchards stand
The tallies The tokens her Han-era devotees carried from commandery to commandery as pledges of her protection and long life

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

西王母

The name in its original Taoist form. Xīwángmǔ (西王母) is attested in the source tradition — “Western queen mother”. Its macron-length vowels and acute stress marks carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

xiwangmu

Reduced to plain xiwangmu, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels and acute stress marks. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Xīwángmǔ

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Xīwángmǔ restores macron-length vowels and acute stress marks, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Xīwángmǔ.com → xn--xwngm-yqa03cy7b.com

The non-ASCII characters in Xīwángmǔ are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Xīwángmǔ.

Pronunciation

How Xīwángmǔ was spoken

/ɕi˥ waŋ˧˥ mu˨˩˦/ Modern Standard Mandarin (Pinyin)
xī Voiceless alveolo-palatal fricative [ɕ] followed by high front vowel [i], Tone 1 (high level, ˥). Pinyin 'x' has no English equivalent; it is the 'sh' of 'she' pushed forward toward the teeth.
wáng Labial-velar approximant [w] with the final [aŋ] (open central vowel + velar nasal), Tone 2 (rising, ˧˥).
mǔ Bilabial nasal [m] with high back rounded vowel [u], Tone 3 (dipping, ˨˩˦) — the low, dipping contour that falls before it rises.

Modern Approximation

shee-WAHNG-moo — Xī held high and level like a sustained note, wáng rising from mid to high as in a question, mǔ dipping low and turning up again.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

西王母 means 'Queen Mother of the West' — xī (west), wáng (king, sovereign), mǔ (mother). The Modern Standard Mandarin reading is Xīwángmǔ (Tones 1-2-3), as recorded in the Unihan Database (Unicode Consortium, kMandarin) and standard Mandarin dictionaries. The tone-marked Pinyin restoration Xīwángmǔ preserves the citation tones of all three syllables; in connected speech the third tone on mǔ is often realized as a low half-third tone, but the lexical tone remains Tone 3.

Extended Lore

Xīwángmǔ teaches that civilization does not destroy its wilderness; it enthrones it. The tiger-toothed power of the Shān Hǎi Jīng was not replaced by the serene hostess of the Peach Banquet — she was carried forward, wildness and all, into the highest seat of the Taoist heavens. The goddess who once sent plague became the goddess who grants endless life, and the two offices are not as far apart as they seem: both are sovereignty over the boundary between life and death, held by the same unanswerable hand.
