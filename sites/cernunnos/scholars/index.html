01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Cernunnos (cernunnos) ​— Wilderness, Animals, ‌Fertility · The ‌Horned One — ​is the antlered ​god of ​Gaul: named exactly ​once in the ​surviving record, on ‌the Pillar of ​the Boatmen raised ​by the boatmen's ‌guild of the ​Parisii in the ‌reign of Tiberius, ​and pictured across ‌the Celtic ​world as the ​cross-legged lord who ​holds the torc ​of rank in ​one hand and ‌the ram-horned serpent ​of the underworld ‌in the other, ​with the beasts ‌of the wild ​gathered at ‌his back.[1]

The ​Gaulish name is ‌built on the ​pan-Celtic word for ​horn — Proto-Celtic ‌karnon, Old Irish ‌corn, Welsh corn ‌— and the ​standard gloss is ​'the horned one'; ‌the Paris ​inscription illustrates its ​own etymology, showing ‌a balding, bearded ​head wearing antlers ​with a torc ‌hanging from each.[2] ​The most famous ‌image of the ​god, the cross-legged ​antlered figure of ​the Gundestrup ​cauldron, carries no ‌inscription at all: ‌the identification is ​argued from the ‌horns, the torc, ​and the serpent, ​and honest scholarship ‌keeps the word ‌'Cernunnos' in quotation ‌marks wherever the ‌stone does ​not speak.[3]

PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Cernunnos. The ​attested Gaulish form ​is already plain ‌Latin letters — ​ERNVNNOS in the ​capitals of the ​Paris stone — ‌with no diacritic ​and no ​distinctive letter for ​the ASCII alphabet ‌to lose, which ​places the name ‌in Tier 2 ​by the mechanical ‌rule. The temple ​is domain-less: cernunnos.com ‌is registered to ‌a third party ​outside the ​PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for ​release, and no ‌form of the ‌name is claimed ​as owned.[4]

Sources

  1. Pillar of the Boatmen (CIL XIII 3026), Paris, early 1st c. CE; Musée national du Moyen Âge, Paris.
  2. Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise, 2nd ed. (Paris: Errance, 2003), s.v. carno- 'horn', Cernunnos.
  3. Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (Gloucester: Alan Sutton, 1986), on the iconography of the antlered god.
  4. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia, 5 vols. (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Cernunnos.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested once, ‌in Roman capitals, as ‌[C]ERNVNNOS on the ​Pillar of the ​Boatmen. The initial ​letter is lost at ​the broken edge ​of the stone ‌and restored as ​C by convention — ​a restoration three ‌centuries of scholarship ​have upheld, since the ‌Gaulish horn-root karno- ​predicts exactly this ‌word.[1] The double ​NN and the final ​-OS are certain; ​the name is ​a Gaulish nominative ​in full.

The etymology ‌is the standard ​one: Proto-Celtic karnon ‌'horn' (Matasović, EDPC) ​with the suffix that ‌forms theonyms and ​titles, hence 'the ‌horned one'. Delamarre's ​Dictionnaire de la langue ‌gauloise registers both ​the root and ​the name.[2] The ‌details remain honestly debated: ‌whether the word ‌functioned as a ​personal theonym or as ​an epithet of ‌a god otherwise ​named, and how ​the suffix formation is ‌to be analysed. ​The long-proposed connection ​with the Old ‌Irish heroic epithet cernach ​(Conall Cernach) is ‌philologically possible but ​unproven; Vendryes registers ​the Irish material and ​Mac Cana counsels ​caution.[3]

Because the ‌Gaulish form is ‌plain ASCII, the Unicode ​restoration Cernunnos differs ‌from the lowercase ​entry only by capitalization ​— Tier 2 ‌by the mechanical ‌rule, a statement ‌about orthography and never ‌about the god.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ‌runs:

  • c ​→ C — Same

​- e → ​e — Same
‌- r → ​r — Same

  • ​n → n ​— Same
  • ‌u → u ​— Same
  • n ​→ n — ​Same
  • n ‌→ n — ​Same
  • o → ‌o — Same

​- s → ‌s — Same

The ​project holds no ‌domain for this ‌name: cernunnos.com is ​registered to a third ​party outside the ​PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for ‌release. The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

  2. Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise, 2nd ed. (Paris: Errance, 2003); Matasović, Ranko, Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Celtic (Leiden: Brill, 2009), s.v. *karnon 'horn'.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /kerˈnun.nos/ — ​Gaulish Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Cer- — Voiceless ​velar stop ‌[k] (Gaulish ​c is always ​hard), short ‌open [e], ​trilled [r]; ‌the stressed syllable, ​and the ‌worn descendant ​of the horn-root ​karno-
  • ​-nun- — ​Voiced alveolar nasal ​[n], short ‌back [u], ​and the ‌doubled nasal across ​the syllable ‌boundary, written ​out in full ‌in the ​inscription's ERNVNNOS

‌- -nos — ​Short [o] ​and final ‌voiceless [s]: ‌the Gaulish nominative ‌ending, never ​to be ​anglicized into '-us'

‌For the ​modern speaker, ​the closest ‌approximation is: 'ker-NOON-nos' ​— every ​c hard, ‌the double n ​fully sounded, ‌the final ​syllable crisp.

Kindred ​and historical ​forms of ​the name:

‌- Gaulish — ‌[C]ERNVNNOS, the ​single epigraphic ‌attestation, Pillar of ​the Boatmen ​(1st c. ‌CE)

  • ‌Proto-Celtic — karnon, ‌'horn', the ‌root beneath ​the name (Matasović)

‌- Insular ​cognates — ​Old Irish corn, ​Welsh corn, ‌'horn, drinking-horn'[2]
​- Proposed ​echo — Conall ​Cernach of ‌the Ulster ​Cycle, whose epithet ​has been ​linked to ‌the theonym — ​proposed, never ‌proven[3]

Cernunnos ​is Tier ‌2 because the ​attested form ‌preserves nothing ‌the ASCII alphabet ​loses: the ​Paris stone ​writes plain ​Latin letters, and ‌the restoration ‌changes only ​the capital.

  2. Matasović, Ranko, Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Celtic (Leiden: Brill, 2009), s.v. *karnon.
  3. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on Conall Cernach.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in the Latin ‌alphabet as ​[C]ERNVNNOS — Gaulish, ​attested in ​the early 1st ​c. CE ​on the Pillar ‌of the ​Boatmen at Paris, ​the name's only ‌inscription.[1] Gaulish ​elsewhere in Gaul ‌was also ​written in the ‌Greek alphabet, ​but this name ​survives in ​the Latin-letter tradition ​alone; no ​native Celtic script ‌attests it, ​and the manuscript ‌cultures of ​Ireland and Wales, ‌which preserve ​so much else, ‌never wrote ​it down.

The ‌scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ​step:

  • ‌The inscription is ‌Roman capitals; the ‌initial letter ​is lost at ​the stone's ‌broken edge and ​restored as ​C, a reading ‌upheld by ​the horn-root karno- ​that predicts ‌the word.[2]
  • ​The doubled ‌NN of ERNVNNOS ​is certain ​and is kept ​in the ​normalized form Cernunnos.

‌- The ‌final -OS is ​the Gaulish ‌nominative singular ending; ​modern English habitually ​flattens it ‌to '-us', which ‌the restoration ‌resists.

  • The ‌attested form ​is plain ASCII ‌— no ​diacritic, no distinctive ​letter — ​so the Unicode ‌restoration differs ​from the lowercase ​entry only ​by the capital, ‌fixing the ​name in Tier ​2.[3]

Because ​the name is ‌pure ASCII, ​its domain was ‌registered in ​the ordinary way ‌long ago: cernunnos.com ​is held ‌by a third ‌party outside ​the PuniCodex collection ​and is ​watched for release. ​This temple ‌claims no domain ‌it does ​not hold.

  2. Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise, 2nd ed. (Paris: Errance, 2003), s.v. Cernunnos.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Cernunnos is ​the horned ‌lord of ‌the wild: ​the god ​the Gauls ​pictured enthroned ​among the ​animals, holding ‌the wealth ​of the ​torc and ‌the chthonic ​power of ‌the serpent, ​at the ‌meeting-point of ​wilderness, fertility, ​and the ​world below.[1]

​### Wilderness

​On the ‌Gundestrup plate the ​antlered figure ‌sits in ​the midst ‌of the ​beasts — ‌stag, boar, ​and the ‌wolfish creature ​that pads ​toward him ‌— the ‌still centre ‌of the ​untamed wood.

​### Animals

‌His antlers ​match the ​stag's beside ‌him; the ​images make ​him not ‌a hunter of ​beasts but ‌their sovereign, ​the one ​the animals ​approach without ​fear.

‌Fertility

At ‌Reims the ​antlered god ‌empties a ​sack of ​grain or ‌coin over ‌a bull ‌and a ‌stag: the ​horned one ‌as the ​source of ​abundance, dispensing ​plenty between ‌Apollo and Mercury.

​### The ​Underworld

The ​ram-horned serpent ‌in his ​hand is ​the chthonic ​animal of ‌Gaulish art, ​and Caesar's ‌report that ​the Gauls ‌traced their ​descent from ‌Dis Pater ‌has kept ​the underworld ​reading of ​the horned ​lord alive ‌— as ‌hypothesis, never ​as proof.[2]

Sources

  1. Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (Gloucester: Alan Sutton, 1986); eadem, Animals in Celtic Life and Myth (London: Routledge, 1992).
  2. Caesar, De Bello Gallico 6.18; Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The iconography ​of Cernunnos ‌is the ‌whole of ​his dossier ​— Gaul ​wrote no ​myths down, ​so the ‌attributes carry ​the meaning ​directly:[1]

  • ‌The antlers ​— The ‌stag's crown ​that illustrates ‌the name ​itself: sovereignty ​over the ​wild made ​visible, on ​the Paris ‌pillar and every ​image that ‌follows it

​- The ‌torc — ​The gold ‌neck-ring of ​rank in ‌the Celtic ​world; on ​Gundestrup he ‌grips it, ‌on the ‌Paris pillar ​it hangs ​from his ‌antlers, at ​Reims it ​sits with ‌the sack ​of plenty: ​wealth held, ‌worn, and dispensed
​- The ‌ram-horned serpent ​— The ​hybrid creature ​of Gaulish ​art, serpent's ‌body and ‌ram's horns, ​a chthonic ‌being found ​in the ​company of ‌high gods; ‌Cernunnos holds ‌it bare-handed
‌- The ​cross-legged seat ‌— The ​seated posture ​of Gundestrup, ​Reims, and ‌Saintes: a stillness ​the scholarship ​has long ​remarked, the ‌repose of ​a lord ​who does ​not chase ‌what already ​answers to ‌him

  • ​The gathered ‌beasts — ​Stag, boar, ‌bull, and ‌the padding ​wolf: the ​retinue of ​the wild ​that closes ‌round him ‌on the ​cauldron plate[2]

Sources

  1. Green, Miranda, Symbol and Image in Celtic Religious Art (London: Routledge, 1989).
  2. The Gundestrup Cauldron, National Museum of Denmark, Copenhagen; Mac Cana, Celtic Mythology (1970).

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Cernunnos has no ​mythology in the literary ‌sense — no tale, no ‌genealogy, no surviving word ​of his deeds. The Gauls ​entrusted their tradition to ​memory, and what memory would ​not carry is gone. ​What survives is a dossier ‌of stone, silver, and ​bronze, and the whole tradition ​must be read from ‌those.[1]

The Name on ​Stone (Pillar of the ‌Boatmen)

Under the choir of ​Notre-Dame in 1711, workmen ‌uncovered the monument the nautae ​Parisiaci — the boatmen's ​guild of the Parisii — ​had raised to Jupiter ​in the reign of Tiberius, ​early in the first ‌century CE. Among its carved ​gods, one block shows a ‌balding, bearded head wearing ​antlers, a torc hanging from ‌each antler, and above ​the figure runs [C]ERNVNNOS. The ‌first letter is lost ​and restored by convention, but ‌the reading has held: ​the only text in the ​world that names the ‌horned god.[1]

The Cross-Legged ‌Lord (Gundestrup)

In 1891 ‌a peat-cutter's spade struck silver ​in the Rævemosen bog ​at Gundestrup in Jutland: a ‌gilded vessel of thirteen ​plates, the masterpiece of Celtic ​metalwork. On one interior ‌plate sits the figure the ​world now pictures when ​it hears the name — ‌cross-legged, antlered, the torc ​in the right hand and ‌the ram-horned serpent in ​the left, a stag with ​matching antlers beside him ​and the animals of the ​wild closing round. No ‌inscription names him; the identification ‌is inference from the ​Paris pillar's evidence, and the ‌scholarship says so plainly.[2]

​### The Antlered Lords of ​Gaul

The figure recurs ‌across Roman Gaul unnamed. At ‌Reims an altar seats ‌him cross-legged between Apollo and ‌Mercury, emptying a sack ​of grain or coin over ‌a bull and a ​stag. At Saintes the cross-legged ​figure keeps his serpents. ​In the rock carvings of ‌Val Camonica in northern Italy, ​a standing antlered figure ​with torcs on his arms ​was cut perhaps four ‌centuries before the Paris pillar ​— the oldest of ​the horned images, and proof ​that the type long ‌predates the name's one attestation.[3]

​### The Insular Question

‌Irish literature preserves no Cernunnos. ​The proposed echo is ‌Conall Cernach, the Ulster Cycle ​hero who stands beside ‌Cú Chulainn as the second ‌champion of Ulster: his ​epithet cernach has been linked ​to the Gaulish horn-name ​from Vendryes to Mac Cana, ​and the honest verdict ‌is suspension — the philology ‌permits it, the sources ​never confirm it.[4]

Sources

  1. Pillar of the Boatmen (CIL XIII 3026), Musée national du Moyen Âge, Paris.
  2. The Gundestrup Cauldron, National Museum of Denmark; Koch, Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (2006), s.v. Gundestrup cauldron.
  3. Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (1986), on Reims, Saintes, and Val Camonica.
  4. Vendryes, Lexique étymologique de l'irlandais ancien; Mac Cana, Celtic Mythology (1970).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Rome met ​the horned ‌god the ‌way Rome ​met every ​god: by ​pairing him ​with her ​own. The ‌Pillar of ​the Boatmen ​is a ‌monument to ​Jupiter, with the ‌Gaulish gods ​— the ‌antlered one ​among them ​— carved ​in attendance; ​the Reims ​altar seats ‌the horned ​figure between ‌Apollo and ​Mercury, a Roman ‌frame built ​round a ‌native centre.[1]

​The deepest ‌Roman comparison ​is inferential. ​Caesar reports ‌that all ‌the Gauls ‌claim descent ​from Dis ​Pater, father ‌of the underworld, ​and since ​the nineteenth ‌century scholars ​have wondered ​whether the ‌antlered lord ​with his ‌serpent and ​his stillness ​is the ​god Caesar's ​informants meant. The ‌identification is ‌suggestive and ​unprovable; the ‌standard handbooks ​— Mac ​Cana, Green, ‌Koch — ‌file it ‌under hypothesis.[2]

‌His closest ​kin inside ‌the lexicon ​are the other ​lords of ​the Celtic ‌otherworld: Arawn ​of Annwn, ​the Welsh ​otherworld king ‌who hunts ​with white, ​red-eared hounds, ​and the ‌Dagda, the ​good god of ‌the Irish ​Tuatha Dé ‌Danann whose ​cauldron never ‌empties — ‌figures of ​plenty and ​of the ​far country, ​whom the ‌texts at ‌least allow ​to speak.[3]

Sources

  1. Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (1986), on the Gallo-Roman framing of native gods.
  2. Caesar, De Bello Gallico 6.18; Mac Cana, Celtic Mythology (1970); Koch, Celtic Culture (2006), s.v. Cernunnos.
  3. Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed (First Branch of the Mabinogi), trans. Sioned Davies, The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Cernunnos ​has the ‌strangest afterlife ‌in the ​lexicon: ​a god ​named once ​in ​antiquity who ‌became, two ​millennia later, ​the ‌most famous ​horned figure ‌in modern ​religion. ‌Margaret Murray's ​The God ​of ​the Witches ​(1931) made ​him the ‌prime ​exhibit of ‌her pan-European ​witch-cult — ‌a ​thesis academic ‌history has ​since ‌dismantled, Ronald ​Hutton's The ​Triumph of ‌the ‌Moon (1999) ‌being the ​standard autopsy ​— ‌but the ​image outlived ​the ‌theory.[1]

Gerald ​Gardner placed ​the Horned ‌God ​at the ‌centre of ​modern Wicca, ​and ​'Cernunnos' became ​the figure's ‌conventional ‌name across ​contemporary pagan ‌practice worldwide. ​The ​Gundestrup plate ‌did the ‌visual work: ‌the ‌cross-legged, antlered ​silhouette is ‌now ​among the ​most reproduced ​images of ‌the ​ancient world, ​carried into ​fiction, film, ‌games, ​and the ​devotional art ​of ‌traditions that ​did not ‌exist a ​century ‌ago. A ​god with ‌no surviving ‌myth ​became the ​emblem of ​every ​wild thing ‌that refuses ‌to be ​tamed.[2]

Sources

  1. Murray, Margaret A., The God of the Witches (London: Sampson Low, 1931); Hutton, Ronald, The Triumph of the Moon: A History of Modern Pagan Witchcraft (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1999).
  2. Gardner, Gerald B., Witchcraft Today (London: Rider, 1954); Hutton, The Triumph of the Moon (1999).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Cernunnos is ​the most ‌materially grounded ‌name in ​the Celtic ​lexicon and ​the most textually ​impoverished: his ​entire attestation ‌is archaeological.[1]

​The Pillar ​of the Boatmen, ‌raised by ​the boatmen's ‌guild of ​the Parisii ‌under Tiberius ​(14–37 CE) and ​rediscovered beneath ​Notre-Dame in ​1711, carries ​the name's ‌only inscription — ​[C]ERNVNNOS, above ‌the antlered ​head with ‌torcs — ​and stands ‌today in the ​Musée national ‌du Moyen ​Âge, Paris. ​The Gundestrup ‌cauldron, cut from ‌the peat ‌at Rævemosen, ​Jutland, in ​1891 and ‌dated to ​around the first ​century BCE ‌or CE, ​shows the ​cross-legged antlered ‌figure with torc ​and ram-horned ‌serpent on ​its most ​famous interior ​plate; it ​is the treasure ‌of the ‌National Museum ​of Denmark, ‌Copenhagen.[2]

The ​supporting gallery is ​wide: the ‌Reims altar ‌with its ‌antlered figure ‌between Apollo ​and Mercury above ‌the bull ​and stag; ​the cross-legged ​relief from ‌Saintes; the horned ​figure with ​serpents from ​Cirencester in ‌Roman Britain; ​and the ​fourth-century BCE antlered ​carving of ‌Val Camonica ​— the ‌oldest horned ​image in the ‌Celtic world. ​No temple, ‌votive deposit, ‌or cult ​site can ​be securely assigned ​to the ​god; the ‌images are ‌the cult's ​surviving footprint.[1]

Sources

  1. Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (Gloucester: Alan Sutton, 1986), catalogue of the antlered-god monuments.
  2. Pillar of the Boatmen (CIL XIII 3026); the Gundestrup Cauldron, National Museum of Denmark.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Irish cycles ‌preserve no ‌Cernunnos — ​that silence ​is ​the starting ​point of ​every honest ‌comparison. ​What they ​preserve is ‌a candidate ​and a ‌question. ​The candidate ‌is Conall ​Cernach, the ​Ulster Cycle ​hero ​second only ​to Cú ‌Chulainn among ​the ‌champions of ​the Red ‌Branch: his ​epithet cernach ‌has ​been connected ‌with the ​Gaulish horn-name ​since ‌the nineteenth ‌century, and ‌Vendryes registers ​the Irish ​material ‌in the ​etymological record.[1]

​The question ‌is whether ​the ​connection is ‌real. Mac ​Cana, who ‌gives ​the proposal ​its fullest ​fair hearing, ​ends in ‌caution: ‌the philology ​permits the ‌link, but ​no Irish ​text ‌gives Conall ‌horns, antlers, ‌or a ‌serpent, ​and the ‌epithet admits ​other explanations. ​Elsewhere the ​cycles ‌offer only ​atmosphere — ​the divine ​plenty ‌of the ​Dagda's cauldron, ​the otherworld ​herds and ‌swine ​of the ‌Tuatha Dé ​Danann, the ‌wild places ​that ‌answer to ‌figures like ​Danu's children ​— ​resonances of ​the horned ‌god's world, ‌never his ​name.[2]

Sources

  1. Vendryes, Joseph, Lexique étymologique de l'irlandais ancien (Dublin: DIAS, 1959–).
  2. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on Conall Cernach and the horned god.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Welsh ​literature knows ‌no Cernunnos ‌by name, ​but the ​Mabinogion keeps ​the clearest ​insular portrait ​of his ‌office: Arawn, ​king of ​Annwn, the ‌otherworld lord ​who rides ‌out with ​his pack ‌of white, ​red-eared hounds ​and exchanges ​places with ​Pwyll for ​a year ‌and a ​day.[1] The ‌First Branch ​never gives ‌Arawn antlers ​— the ‌Welsh otherworld ​king ‌is a ​huntsman and ​a sovereign, ‌not a ‌horned figure ‌— but ​the shape ​of the ‌role is ​the one ​the Gaulish ‌images imply: ​the still, ​wealthy lord ‌of a ​country where ‌the beasts ​run, holding ​court at ​the boundary ​between the ‌world and ‌what lies ​under it.

‌The comparison ​must be ​kept in ‌its lane. ‌No Welsh ‌text ‌connects Arawn ​to the ‌Gaulish theonym, ​and the ​horned god's ​one attested ‌name never ​crosses the ​Channel into ​literature at ‌all. What ​the Welsh ​sources contribute ​is the ‌native vocabulary ​for the ‌office the ​monuments illustrate ‌— otherworld, ​sovereignty, the ‌hunt, the ‌untamed herd ​— in ​the words ​of a ​tradition that ‌did write ‌its gods ​down.[2]

Sources

  1. Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed (First Branch of the Mabinogi), trans. Sioned Davies, The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Annwn, Cernunnos.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The epigraphic ​record of ‌Cernunnos is ‌a single ​line: [C]ERNVNNOS, ​carved in ​Roman capitals ​on the ​Pillar of ‌the Boatmen, ​the monument ​the nautae ‌Parisiaci raised ​to Jupiter ‌under Tiberius ​in the ‌early first ​century CE.[1] ​The initial ​letter stood ​at the ​broken edge ‌of the block ​and is ‌restored by ​convention; the ‌remaining letters ​ERNVNNOS are ‌secure, and ​the antlered ‌head with ​its pendant ​torcs carved ‌beneath the ‌word is ‌the inscription's ​own commentary ​on the ‌name — ​'the horned ​one', illustrated.

‌The wider ​Gaulish epigraphic ​habit matters ‌for the reading: ​Gaulish was ‌written in ​both the ​Greek alphabet ​(in the ​south, from ‌Massalia's orbit) ‌and the ​Latin, and ‌divine names ​in that ​corpus are ‌normally spelled ‌out in ‌full — ‌which makes ​the solitary ‌attestation of ​this one ​all the ​more striking. ‌Ogham, the insular ​script of ​early Ireland, ​postdates the ‌Gaulish material ​and contains ​no form ​of the ‌name. Everything ​beyond the ‌Paris line ​— Gundestrup, ‌Reims, Saintes, ​Val Camonica, ‌Cirencester — ‌is iconography ​without a ​caption, and ​the discipline ​of the ‌field is ‌to say ​exactly that.[2]

Sources

  1. Pillar of the Boatmen (CIL XIII 3026), Musée national du Moyen Âge, Paris.
  2. Delamarre, Xavier, Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise, 2nd ed. (Paris: Errance, 2003), introduction on the Gaulish epigraphic corpus.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Cernunnos ​is the ‌god built ‌from ​evidence alone. ​No ​hymn, no ​saga, no ​genealogy ‌— one ​word ​on a ‌Paris stone, ​one ‌silver plate ​from a ‌Danish ​bog, and ​a ​scattered congregation ​of images ​that ‌agree with ​each ‌other across ​six hundred ‌miles ​and four ‌hundred ​years.[1]

He ‌asks a ​different ​kind of ‌attention than ‌the ‌storied gods: ​not ​'what did ‌he do?' ​but ​'what did ‌they ​see, that ​they carved ‌him ​so carefully ‌and wrote ​his ​name so ​rarely?' ​The answer ‌the monuments ‌give ​is always ‌the ​same — ​the still ‌one ‌in the ‌middle of ‌the ​animals, holding ‌rank ​in one ​hand and ​the ‌underworld in ​the ​other, entirely ​at peace. ‌Some ​gods are ​known ​by their ‌stories. This ​one ‌is known ​by his ‌posture, ​and the ‌posture ‌has proved ​enough: two ​thousand ​years later, ​the ‌world still ‌recognizes the ​silhouette.

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970); Green, Miranda, The Gods of the Celts (1986).
