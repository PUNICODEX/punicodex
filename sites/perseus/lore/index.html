Mythology

Stories of Perseús

Perseus is the Argive hero of the Gorgon-slaying, the rescuer of Andromeda, and the founder of the dynasty that produces Heracles. His cycle, told most fully by Apollodorus and Ovid, turns on oracles, divine equipment, and the terrible double gift of the Gorgon's head.

The Birth

The Shower of Gold

Akrisios, king of Argos, went to Delphi to ask the oracle for a son and received instead a death sentence: his daughter Danaë would bear a child who would kill him. He locked Danaë in a brazen chamber sunk underground, but Zeus came upon her as a shower of gold — 'she took the gold into her lap,' as Simonides sang (PMG 543) — passing through bronze with an immortal's ease. When Perseus was born, Akrisios would not kill mother and child outright; he had them sealed in a chest and cast into the sea, which carried them gently to the island of Seriphos. There the fisherman Diktys drew them ashore and gave them his house (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.1; cf. Pindar, Pythian 12.12–16). The oracle has already framed the whole of the hero's life: his greatness and his grandfather's death are the same event, waiting years away at a games-ground for a discus to close the circle.

The Quest

The Graiai and the Gear

Polydectes, king of Seriphos, desired Danaë and wanted Perseus gone. Feigning a bride-price for Hippodameia, he demanded horses of his whole court; Perseus rashly promised the Gorgon's head instead — and Polydectes held him to it (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.3). Athena and Hermes took charge of the impossible errand and led him first to the Graiai, the 'grey ones' who from birth were old women, sisters of the Gorgons, sharing a single eye and a single tooth (Hesiod, Theogony 270–273). Perseus snatched the eye as it passed from face to face and ransomed it for the road to the nymphs; from them he received the kibisis that safely holds the head, the cap of Hades that makes its wearer unseen, and winged sandals; from Hermes the adamantine harpe, from Athena the polished bronze shield (Apollodorus 2.2.3–4; Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.610ff). The quest's real lesson is assembled before the monster is met: this hero wins not by native strength but by holding hostage what others cannot do without — an eye, a tooth, and the world's best equipment.

The Slaying

The Head of Medousa

Beyond Ocean lay the Gorgons, winged women with brazen claws and serpent hair; of the three sisters, only Medousa was mortal (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.4). Perseus came upon them sleeping and, watching Medousa's image in the mirror-bright shield rather than the living face, struck once with the harpe. The neck opened and from it sprang Pegasus and the warrior Chrysaor — 'when Perseus cut off her head, great Chrysaor and the horse Pegasus sprang forth' (Hesiod, Theogony 280–286). The sleeping sisters woke and took the wing in pursuit, but the cap of Hades hid him, and he was gone into the air. The gods had not sent him to punish Medousa so much as to harvest her: from the monster's death leap the winged horse that will carry Zeus's thunderbolts and the father of the monster Geryon — terror and poetry born from a single stroke of the sickle-sword.

The Rescue

The Sea-Monster of Ethiopia

Returning with the head, Perseus flew over Ethiopia, where the sea was swallowing the land. Kassiepeia had boasted that she was fairer than the Nereids, and Poseidon answered with a flood and a ketos, a ravening sea-beast; the oracle of Ammon declared that only the chaining of Kassiepeia's daughter Andromeda would appease it. Perseus saw her on the rock 'as a marble statue gains a soul' (Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.675–678) and bargained with her parents: her hand in marriage, or let the beast have its due. He slew the ketos and freed her — and at the wedding feast met Phineus, who claimed Andromeda for himself. Perseus had borne the Gorgon's head this far wrapped in the kibisis; now he unveiled it, and Phineus with his whole company hardened into stone, swords half-drawn (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.3; Ovid, Met. 4.663–739). The head that saved a princess then massacres a banquet: divine tools do not ask whether the hand that wields them is rescuing or revenging.

The Return

Stone upon Seriphos

On Seriphos, Polydectes had spent Perseus' long absence persecuting Danaë, who with Diktys had fled to the temples for sanctuary. The king was feasting with his assembled islanders when the hero walked in, still dusty from the road, and held up the head: court, tyrant, and dinner-tables took the grey of stone together (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.4.2–3). Perseus made Diktys king and gave the Gorgoneion to Athena, who fixed it on her aegis, where the apotropaic face guards the city of the gods ever after (Apollodorus 2.4.3). One thread of the oracle remained: at Larisa, competing in the funeral games of Teutamides, Perseus threw the discus and it veered — striking and killing a spectator who was his grandfather Akrisios (Apollodorus 2.4.4; Hyginus, Fabulae 273). Ashamed to inherit Argos by such blood, he exchanged it for Tiryns and founded Mycenae. Even the founder of dynasties cannot aim his own fate; the discus of the games closes the circle the golden shower began.

02

Perseus

Hero

Perseus is the Greek hero who slew the Gorgon Medousa and founded the royal line of Mycenae and Argos. Son of Zeus and Danaë, he accomplishes what no god would attempt, armed only with borrowed wonders — mirrored shield, adamantine harpe, cap of Hades, winged sandals — and his name may itself be eastern: "Destroyer, possibly Persian."

The Gorgon-Slayer

With Athena's mirrored shield and Hermes' adamantine harpe he took the one head no god dared face — Medousa's.

Hero

Central domain: hero, slayer of medousa — sacker of cities, ancestor of Heracles through his son Electryon.

Divine Favor

Athena and Hermes equip him; the kibisis holds the head; winged sandals and the cap of Hades carry him beyond Ocean.

Founder of Mycenae

From his dynasty — Electryon, Sthenelus, Eurystheus — come Heracles and the Argive royal line.

Sacred Symbols

Harpe (adamantine sickle-sword) The curved blade Hermes gave him for the one stroke at Medousa's neck — a weapon older than the gods' order
Mirrored shield Athena's polished bronze, in which Perseus watched the Gorgon safely — knowledge taken only by reflection
Cap of Hades The helmet of invisibility that hid him from the waking Gorgon sisters and the giants of Seriphos
Kibisis The wallet that holds the severed head without harm — the first container built for a monster
Gorgoneion The apotropaic face itself, set on Athena's aegis, turning enemies to stone at the city's threshold
Winged sandals (talaria) Flight borrowed from Hermes' own feet, carrying the hero beyond Ocean to the world's edge

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

Περσεύς

The name in its original Greek form. Perseús (Περσεύς) is attested in the source tradition — “Destroyer, possibly Persian”. Its diphthongs and acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

perseus

Reduced to plain perseus, the name loses everything that made it specific: diphthongs and acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Perseús

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Perseús restores diphthongs and acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Perseús.com → xn--perses-tya.com

The non-ASCII characters in Perseús are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Perseús.

Pronunciation

How Perseús was spoken

/perˈseu̯s/ Ancient Greek Reconstruction
Per- Unaspirated [p], open [e], and a trilled [r]; a light first syllable, as in 'per'.
-seús Voiceless [s] followed by the falling diphthong [eu̯] — 'eh-oo' in one beat — closing in final [s]. Greek Περσεύς carries its acute accent on the last syllable.

Modern Approximation

'per-SEUS' — stress the final syllable, and let the eu glide like 'eh-oo' said in one motion.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Perseús is Tier 1: the restored acute accent of Greek Περσεύς is a preserved diacritic that the ASCII form perseus flattens entirely. The accent marks the name's true Greek stress — Per-SEUS, not PER-seus — and the lexicon's canonical restoration keeps it.

Extended Lore

Perseus is the hero who wins by not looking. Every other Greek hero faces his monster; Perseus can only succeed by watching a reflection, taking the truth of the Gorgon secondhand, obliquely. It is the myth's central intelligence: some realities cannot be met head-on — only approached in a mirror, at an angle, by indirection. Even his equipment is borrowed, none of it his own; the self that triumphs is assembled from others' gifts, as a scholar's knowledge is assembled from books. And his name is itself a reflection — possibly Persian, possibly 'Destroyer,' seen dimly through two languages. Perseus suggests that heroism is not the native strength of Achilles but the managed gaze: know the monster's true shape well enough to cut its neck, and never meet its eyes.
