The Shower of Gold (The Birth)

Akrisios, king of Argos, went to Delphi to ask the oracle for a ​son and received instead a death sentence: his daughter Danaë would bear a child who would kill him. He locked Danaë in a brazen chamber sunk underground, but Zeus came upon her as ‌a shower of gold — 'she took the gold into her lap,' as Simonides sang (PMG 543) — passing through bronze with an immortal's ease. When Perseus was born, Akrisios would not ​kill mother and child outright; he had them sealed in a chest and cast into the sea, which carried them gently to the island of Seriphos. There the fisherman Diktys drew them ashore ‌and gave them his house (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.1; cf. Pindar, Pythian 12.12–16). The oracle has already framed the whole of the hero's life: his greatness and his grandfather's death are the same ​event, waiting years away at a games-ground for a discus to close the circle.

The Graiai and the Gear (The Quest)

Polydectes, king of Seriphos, desired Danaë and wanted Perseus gone. Feigning a ‌bride-price for Hippodameia, he demanded horses of his whole court; Perseus rashly promised the Gorgon's head instead — and Polydectes held him to it (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.3). Athena and Hermes took charge of ​the impossible errand and led him first to the Graiai, the 'grey ones' who from birth were old women, sisters of the Gorgons, sharing a single eye and a single tooth (Hesiod, ‌Theogony 270–273). Perseus snatched the eye as it passed from face to face and ransomed it for the road to the nymphs; from them he received the kibisis that safely holds the head, ​the cap of Hades that makes its wearer unseen, and winged sandals; from Hermes the adamantine harpe, from Athena the polished bronze shield (Apollodorus 2.2.3–4; Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.610ff). The quest's real lesson ‌is assembled before the monster is met: this hero wins not by native strength but by holding hostage what others cannot do without — an eye, a tooth, and the world's best equipment.

​The Head of Medousa (The Slaying)

Beyond Ocean lay the Gorgons, winged women with brazen claws and serpent hair; of the three sisters, only Medousa was mortal (Apollodorus, Bibliotheca 2.2.4). Perseus came ​upon them sleeping and, watching Medousa's image in the mirror-bright shield rather than the living face, struck once with the harpe. The neck opened and from it sprang Pegasus and the warrior Chrysaor ‌— 'when Perseus cut off her head, great Chrysaor and the horse Pegasus sprang forth' (Hesiod, Theogony 280–286). The sleeping sisters woke and took the wing in pursuit, but the cap of Hades ‌hid him, and he was gone into the air. The gods had not sent him to punish Medousa so much as to harvest her: from the monster's death leap the winged horse ‌that will carry Zeus's thunderbolts and the father of the monster Geryon — terror and poetry born from a single stroke of the sickle-sword.
