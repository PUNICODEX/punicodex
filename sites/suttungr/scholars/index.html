01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Suttungr (suttungr) ​— Giant, ‌Guardian of Mead ‌· Heavy ​with foundation — ​is the ​jötunn son of ​Gillingr, brother ​of Baugi, and ‌father of ​Gunnlöð: the giant ​who came ‌to own the ​mead of ‌poetry, took it ​as blood-ransom ‌from the dwarfs ​who murdered ​his father, sealed ​it in ​the mountain Hnitbjörg, ​and lost ‌it when ​Óðinn, disguised as ‌the wanderer ​Bölverkr, drilled through ‌the rock, ​won three nights ‌and three ​drinks from Gunnlöð, ‌and flew ​home as an ​eagle with ‌Suttungr's eagle one ‌beat behind.[1]

‌The name's etymology ​is not ​settled. The handbooks ‌register the ​theonym without a ​confident derivation, ‌and the PuniCodex ​gloss 'Heavy ​with foundation' keeps ‌the traditional ​reading in view ‌without pretending ​the question is ​closed.[2] Snorri ​gives Suttungr the ​continuous narrative ‌of the mead's ‌making, hoarding, ​and theft; Hávamál ‌gives Óðinn's ​own first-person memory ​of the ‌same events — ‌seduction, perjury, ‌and grand larceny ‌confessed between ​counsels.[3]

PuniCodex restores ‌the name ​as Suttungr — ​identical to ​the ASCII input, ‌because normalized ​Old Norse ​writes no diacritic ​in this ‌name. The mechanical ​rule therefore ​places the name ​in Tier ‌2: nothing distinctive ​is lost ‌in transit, a ​statement about ‌orthography and never ​about the ‌giant. The temple ‌is domain-less: ​the plain-ASCII suttungr.com ​is registered ​to a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 3-6, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. Suttungr.
  3. Hávamál 104-110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Suttungr ​— the giant's ​name and ​nothing else; no ​human personal name ​and no ‌place-name descends from ​it. Its ​etymology is one ‌of the ​honest open questions ‌of the ​northern onomasticon: de ‌Vries records ​the name without ​a settled derivation, ​Cleasby–Vigfusson and ​Zoëga gloss it ​simply 'name ‌of a giant', ​and neither ‌Simek nor Orchard ​offers more.[1] ‌The PuniCodex gloss ​'Heavy with ‌foundation' renders the ​traditional reading of ‌the name's ​elements; the lexica's ​uncertainty stands ‌beside it, unedited.

‌The ASCII ‌form is not ​a fallback ​here but the ‌form itself: ​normalized Old Norse ​writes eight plain ‌Latin letters ​and a capital. ​The Unicode ‌restoration Suttungr therefore ​preserves nothing ‌the ASCII alphabet ​loses, and ​the mechanical rule ​classifies the ​name Tier 2 ‌— an orthographic ‌fact, not ​a judgment on ‌the giant.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • ‌s → S ‌— Same, ‌capitalized
  • u ‌→ u ​— Same
  • ‌t → t ​— Same

​- t → ​t — ‌Same

  • u ​→ u ​— Same
  • ​n → ‌n — Same

​- g ​→ g — ​Same

  • r ‌→ r ​— Same

The ‌project holds ​no domain for ‌this name. ​The plain-ASCII suttungr.com ‌is registered ‌to a third ​party outside ​the collection and ​is watched for ​release; this ‌temple claims nothing ‌it does ​not hold.[2]

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈsut.tuŋɡr/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

  • ​Su- — ​Voiceless alveolar ‌sibilant [s] and ​short high ​back rounded ‌[u], stressed ​on the first ‌syllable as ​in every ‌Old Norse word

​- -ttu- ​— The ​geminate stop [tː] ​held audibly ​across the ‌syllable boundary, then ​the second ‌short [u], ​identical to the ‌first

  • ​-ngr — ‌Velar nasal plus ​stop [ŋɡ] ‌closing on ​a syllabic trill ​[r̩] — ‌the nominative ‌ending -r ‌sounded as a ​true r, ​not swallowed

‌For the modern ​speaker, the ​closest approximation ‌is: 'SOOT-toongr' — ​two beats, ​the first ‌stressed, the double ​t held ‌a fraction ​long in the ​middle, as ​in Icelandic ​Suttungur.

Kindred and ‌historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • Old ‌Norse — ​Suttungr, son ​of Gillingr, ‌brother of Baugi, ‌father of ‌Gunnlöð
  • ‌Younger Futhark — ​ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ (normalized ‌suttunkr), the ​Viking-Age runic rendering[2]

​- Manuscript ​Latin — ‌Suttungr, unchanged; the ​Icelandic codices ​write the ​name in plain ‌Latin bookhand
​- Modern ​Icelandic — Suttungur, ​with the ‌regular modern ​nominative ending -ur

‌Suttungr is ​Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves nothing ‌the ASCII ‌form loses: ​no diacritic, no ​distinctive letter, ​no vowel ​length. English and ‌Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Sources

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the Latin ​alphabet as ​Suttungr — ​Old Norse ​(North Germanic), attested ‌13th c. ​CE in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the Poetic Edda ‌(Hávamál) and ​the codices ‌of Snorri's ​Edda (Skáldskaparmál), recording ​material centuries ​older.[1] Before ​the Latin ​bookhand, Old Norse ‌names were ​carved in ‌the Younger ​Futhark; no runic ‌attestation of ​this theonym ‌survives, so ​the manuscript form ‌is the ​primary witness. ​The runic ‌rendering is ‌ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ (normalized suttunkr).

‌The scholarly ​rendering proceeds ​step by ‌step:

  • ᛋ ​(sól) writes ​/s/.
  • ‌ᚢ (úr) ​writes the rounded ​back vowels ‌/u, o, ​ø, ǫ, ‌y/ and /w/ ​— here ​short /u/ ​twice, on ​manuscript evidence.
  • ‌ᛏ (Týr) ‌writes both ​/t/ and ‌/d/ — the ​geminate /tː/ ​is fixed ‌by the ‌manuscript double ‌tt, not by ‌the runes.

​- ᚾ ‌(nauðr) writes ​/n/; ᚴ (kaun) ​writes both ​/k/ and ‌/g/ — ​here the voiced ​cluster /ŋɡ/, ​fixed by ‌the manuscript ​ng.

  • ᚱ ​(reið) writes ​final syllabic ‌/r̩/, the ​nominative ending.
  • ‌The spelling ​suttunkr is ‌a normalized ​phonetic reconstruction; ‌Younger Futhark does ‌not distinguish ​voiced/voiceless stops ​or separate ​short and long ​vowels, so ‌the Eddas' ‌bookhand arbitrates ​every ambiguity.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. suttungr; PuniCodex original-scripts record for suttungr (provenance and steps).
05

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Suttungr is ​catalogued under ‌Giant, Guardian of ‌Mead — and ​the pairing ​is exact: the ​sources give him ​no cult, ​no genealogy beyond ‌his father ​and brother, and ​no story outside ‌the mead; ​his whole mythological ‌identity is the ​hoard and ‌its loss.[1]

​Giant

He ​belongs to the ​jötnar of the ​mountain world, ​kin to the ‌giants of Jötunheimr ​— yet ‌the tradition calls ​him wise, ‌gives him a ​hall the god ‌himself praises, ​and lets him ‌act lawfully at ​every turn: ​a giant cast ‌as proprietor, ‌not as brute.[1]

‌### Guardian of ​Mead

Suttungr ​took the poetry-mead ‌as blood-ransom for ​his murdered ​father Gillingr, stored ‌it in ​Hnitbjörg in the ​vats Óðrerir, Boðn, ‌and Són, ​and set his ‌daughter Gunnlöð to ​guard it. ​Poetry's oldest kenning ​— 'Suttungr's ​mead' — names ‌the guard, not ‌the thief.[1]

​### Avenger

He ‌is the one ​figure in ​the mead myth ‌who acts ‌from justice: the ‌dwarfs Fjalar and ‌Galar drowned ​his father and ‌brained his mother, ​and Suttungr's ​ransom — the ​skerry at ‌flood, exchanged for ​the mead — ​is the ​lex talionis of ‌his world.[1]

​The Loser ​Who Keeps the ​Name

Every ‌kenning for verse ​in the skaldic ‌corpus — ​the mead of ‌Suttungr, the find ​of Óðinn, ‌the liquid of ‌Óðrerir — ​keeps his title ​on the treasure ​he lost: ​the tradition's own ‌accounting of whose ‌loss made ​poetry possible.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 3-6, trans. Faulkes (1998).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 1 (kennings for poetry), trans. Faulkes (1998); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. mead.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Suttungr ‌is narrative ​rather ​than cultic ​— the ​corpus ​gives him ‌no ​temple regalia, ​but ‌fixed around ​his ‌one story ​a ‌set of ​objects no ​retelling ​can omit:[1]

​- ​The vats ‌Óðrerir, ​Boðn, and ‌Són ​— the ‌three ​vessels of ‌the poetry-mead, ​'mind-stirrer', ‌'offering', and ​'atonement', ​emptied in ‌three ‌drinks

  • ‌Hnitbjörg ​— the ​mountain ‌vault of ​the mead: ​treasure ‌sealed in ​stone, ​poetry kept ‌from ​the world

‌- ​The auger ​Rati ​— the ​drill that ‌gnawed ‌the snake-road ​into ‌the rock: ​the ​slender breach ‌through ‌which all ‌poetry ‌escaped[2]

  • ​The eagle ‌form ​— Óðinn's ​flight ​with the ‌mead, ​Suttungr's eagle ​at ​his tail: ‌the ​chase every ​kenning remembers

​- ‌The skerry ​at ‌flood — ​the ‌tide-washed rock ​where ‌he stranded ‌the ​dwarf-murderers until ​they paid: ​the ​ransom that ‌made ‌him poetry's ​gaoler[1]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Suttungr's mythology is ​one continuous story told in two ‌keys: Snorri's Skáldskaparmál narrates the mead's ‌making, hoarding, and theft as prose ​history, while Hávamál lets Óðinn remember ​it in verse — a god ​confessing, between counsels, to seduction, perjury, ​and grand larceny.

How Suttungr ​Won the Mead (Skáldskaparmál 3-4)

When ‌the Æsir and the Vanir made ​peace, they sealed the truce by ​spitting into a vat, and from ‌the spittle they fashioned Kvasir, wisest ​of all beings. The dwarfs ‌Fjalar and Galar killed him, drained ​his blood into the vats Óðrerir, ‌Boðn, and Són, and mixed it ​with honey — the mead of ​poetry. The same dwarfs then drowned ​the giant Gillingr at sea and ​dropped a millstone on his weeping ​wife's head. Suttungr, Gillingr's son, came ‌for the reckoning: he seized the ​dwarfs and stranded them on a ‌skerry the tide would cover, and ​they ransomed their lives with ‌the mead. Suttungr carried it home, ​stored it in Hnitbjörg, and set ‌his daughter Gunnlöð to watch over ​it. This is why poetry is ‌called Kvasir's blood, the dwarfs' drink ​— and Suttungr's mead.[1]

Bölverkr ​at the Mountain (Skáldskaparmál 5-6)

Óðinn ‌came disguised as the wanderer Bölverkr ‌— 'evil-doer', the one honest name ‌in the affair. He engineered the ​deaths of the nine slaves of ​Baugi, Suttungr's brother, then hired himself ‌to do nine men's work ​for the summer at one wage: ​a drink of Suttungr's mead. When ‌Suttungr refused the bargain, Bölverkr produced ​the auger Rati and had Baugi ​bore through the mountain — the ‌first tunnel a cheat, the second ​true — then turned to a ‌snake, slipped through the hole, lay ​three nights with Gunnlöð, and at ​three drinks emptied Óðrerir, Boðn, and ​Són. Then he took eagle shape ​and flew, and Suttungr, in ‌eagle shape, flew after him.[1]

‌The Eagle Chase and the Rhymester's ​Share (Skáldskaparmál 6; Hávamál 104-110)

The ‌Æsir saw the flight coming and ​set out vats in the court ​of Ásgarðr; Óðinn spewed the mead ‌into them with Suttungr's eagle at ‌his tail-feathers — but the pursuit ‌came so close that, in his ‌fear, he sent some of the ​mead out backwards, and that ungathered ‌portion is called the rhymester's share, ​the bad poet's portion.[1] Hávamál ​lets the god tell his own ​version, and it is less heroic: ‌he boasts that he spoke many ​words to his advantage in Suttungr's ​hall; he records that Gunnlöð gave ​him the precious mead from her ‌golden seat and that the reward ​he paid her was poor; and ​he confesses the perjury — the ​frost-giants came asking after Bölverkr, and ‌Óðinn had sworn a ring-oath, and ​who now can trust his ‌oaths? He had robbed Suttungr of ​his mead and made Gunnlöð weep.[2]

‌### The Giant Poetry Is Named ​After (kennings and þulur)

Suttungr lost ‌the treasure and kept the name. ‌Skáldskaparmál's kennings for poetry run through ​his story at every turn — ​the mead of Suttungr, the find ​of Óðinn, the drink of the ​dwarfs, Gunnlöð's lost wealth — and ‌the þulur hold him among the ‌jǫtna heiti, catalogued with the giants ​he belongs to.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 3-6, trans. Faulkes (1998).
  2. Hávamál 104-110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 1 (kennings) and þulur (jǫtna heiti), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).
08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Suttungr's closest ​kin are ‌inside the ‌corpus itself. ​The mead ​myth belongs ​to the ​great northern pattern ​of the ‌coveted treasure ​prised from ​giant-keepers: Þórr ‌winning the ​mile-wide brewing ‌cauldron from ​the giant Hymir ‌so that ​Ægir can ​brew for ​the gods ​is the ​same plot ‌in beer rather ​than poetry, ‌and Lindow ​reads the ‌two tales ​as sibling ‌statements about ​how culture's goods ‌are obtained ​— never ​made at ‌home, always ‌taken across ‌the boundary.[1]

​Cross-culturally, the ​comparativists' dossier is ‌older and ​larger: the ​Indo-European school ‌long paired ​Óðinn's mead-theft ​with the ‌Indic myths of ​soma won ‌by the ​eagle — ​the bird ​that carries ​the intoxicating ‌draught of inspiration ‌down to ​the gods ‌— and ​the parallel ​is genuinely ‌striking: a ‌divine bird, ‌a guarded liquor, ‌a theft ​that founds ‌an art. ​Current handbooks ​keep the ​comparison but ‌drop the certainty: ​Simek and ​Orchard treat ​Suttungr as ‌a figure ​wholly of ​the mead ​complex, with no ‌cult, no ​place-name, and ‌no etymology ​solid enough ‌to build ​on.[2] Within ‌the corpus, ‌his related figures ​are the ​actors his ​story binds ​together: Baugi, ‌Óðinn, and ‌the giant-kind ​of Jötunheimr.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.vv. mead, Hymir.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Suttung; Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Suttung.
09

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Suttungr's ​afterlife is ‌the afterlife ‌of the ​mead ​itself: every ​retelling of ​Norse ​poetry's origin, ‌from the ​Kearys' The ​Heroes ‌of Asgard ​(1857) onward, ‌replays the ​auger-hole, ‌the golden ​seat, and ​the ​two eagles ​over the ​wall of ‌Ásgarðr.[1] ​The romantics ‌loved Gunnlöð ​and made ‌Suttungr ​a brooding ‌proprietor; the ​skaldic ‌scholars made ​him something ​rarer — ‌a ‌mnemonic fixed ‌point, because ​'Suttungr's mead' ​stayed ‌a living ​kenning and ​his ‌name travelled ​inside every ​stanza that ‌called ​verse by ‌its owner's ​title.

In ​modern ​Heathen and ​Ásatrú practice ‌he ‌is remembered, ​with some ‌sympathy, at ​the ​edge of ‌sumbel and ‌skaldic rites ‌as ‌the giant ​from whom ‌inspiration ​was taken, ​and the ​'rhymester's share' ‌— ​the mead ​spilt backwards ​in the ‌chase ​— survives ​as the ​tradition's ‌own dry ​name for ‌bad verse.[2] ​As ‌a personal ​name he ‌never entered ‌the ​living Scandinavian ​stock; his ​immortality ​is entirely ‌literary, and ‌it is ​complete.

Sources

  1. Keary, Annie, and Eliza Keary, The Heroes of Asgard: Tales from Scandinavian Mythology (London: Macmillan, 1857).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. mead.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Suttungr ​is a ‌wholly textual ‌giant: no ​runestone invokes ​him, no ​votive deposit ​names him, ​and no ‌Viking-Age image ​can be ​securely identified ‌with his ​story.[1] ‌His attestation ​hangs on ‌the manuscript ​witnesses of ​the mead ​myth — ​the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Poetic ​Edda for ‌Hávamál's account, ​and ‌the Codex ​Regius of ‌Snorri's Edda ​with its ‌fellow codices ​for Skáldskaparmál's ​continuous narrative.

‌The story's ‌pictorial life ‌begins only ​in the ​early modern ‌North: ​the eighteenth-century ​Icelandic paper ‌manuscripts of ​the Edda ​— SÁM ‌66 and ​the Melsteð ‌Edda — ​illustrate the ​myth with ​scenes of ​Óðinn ‌as an ‌eagle above ​the Æsir's ‌waiting vats, ​Suttungr's eagle ​behind him: ‌proof of ‌the tale's ‌hold on ‌post-medieval readers, ​not of ‌older iconography.[2] ​Claims ​that figures ​on the ‌Gotland picture ​stones depict ​the mead ​of poetry ‌remain interpretive ​and are ​not accepted ​as secure ‌identifications; the ​stone ‌record is ​silent, and ‌the word-hoard ​of the ‌skalds — ‌the kennings ​that kept ​'Suttungr's mead' ​current for ​centuries — ‌is the ‌only true ​monument.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda gives ‌Suttungr one sustained ‌appearance, and ​it is ​Óðinn's own voice ​that gives ​it. Hávamál 104-110 ​interrupts the ‌god's counsel-poem with ​a first-person ​memory: the visit ‌to the ​old giant, ‌the many words ​spoken to ‌advantage in Suttungr's ​hall, Gunnlöð's ​golden seat and ​the drink ​of the precious ​mead, the ‌poor reward ​paid for her ‌whole heart, ​Rati's mouth gnawing ‌the rock, ​the paths of ‌the giants ​standing over and ‌under. It ​closes on ​the record's one ‌moment of ‌shame: the frost-giants ‌came to ​Hár's hall asking ​after Bölverkr ‌— whether he ​had reached ​the gods ‌or Suttungr had ​slain him ​— and Óðinn, ‌the poem ​says, had sworn ‌a ring-oath, ​and who now ​can trust ​his oaths? ​He had robbed ‌Suttungr of ‌his mead and ​made Gunnlöð ‌weep.[1]

The frame ​verses deepen ​the ambivalence. Hávamál ‌13-14 names ‌the heron ‌of forgetfulness that ‌broods over ​the drink of ‌mortals and ​steals their wits, ​and confesses ​'drunk I was, ‌very drunk, ​at wise ​Fjalar's' — the ​mead-hall remembered ‌as a night ​of dangerous ​wisdom, from which ​the best ‌drink is the ​one a ‌man comes ​home sober from. ‌The poem ​grants the giant's ‌household the ‌epithet 'wise' even ​as it ​celebrates the robbery: ​the verse ​tradition, like ‌the prose, never ‌makes Suttungr ​a fool.[2]

Sources

  1. Hávamál 104-110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Hávamál 13-14 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Suttungr's continuous ‌witness, and it ​is a ​whole story. ​Skáldskaparmál 3-4 ​gives him his ​origin in ‌the mead: ​Kvasir brewed from ​the truce-spittle ‌of Æsir ​and Vanir, murdered ‌by the ​dwarfs Fjalar ‌and Galar, whose ​blood-honey mead ​becomes Suttungr's ​when he ​exacts it as ​ransom for ‌his drowned ​father Gillingr and ‌his brained ​mother — ‌the skerry at ​flood traded ‌for the ​vats Óðrerir, Boðn, ‌and Són, ​sealed in ​Hnitbjörg under ‌Gunnlöð's guard. Skáldskaparmál ‌5-6 gives ‌him his ​loss: Bölverkr's nine ​dead slaves ‌and summer's ​labour for Baugi, ​the refused ‌bargain, the ​auger Rati and ​the two ‌borings, the ​snake in ‌the hole, the ​three nights ​and three ​drinks, and the ​eagle chase ‌to the ‌waiting vats of ​Ásgarðr with ‌the rhymester's ​share spilt backwards ​along the ‌way.[1]

Snorri's ‌craft deserves ‌note: he frames ‌the whole ​tale as ‌the answer to ​Ægir's question ​— why ​is poetry called ‌what it ​is? — ​so that Suttungr's ​name survives ‌as a ​definition, not ​merely a character. ​The kennings ‌chapter keeps ​him inside the ‌poets' shorthand ​— the ‌mead of Suttungr, ​Gunnlöð's lost ‌wealth — ‌and the þulur ​hold him ​among the ​jǫtna heiti, ​a giant catalogued ‌with bureaucratic ‌fidelity among ​his mountain-kin.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 3-6, trans. Faulkes (1998).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 1 (kennings for poetry) and þulur (jǫtna heiti), trans. Faulkes (1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains no ‌invocation ​of Suttungr. ​No runestone ​asks ​his favour, ​no amulet ‌bears ​his name, ​and ‌no inscription ​yet found ‌links ​a dedicant ‌to any ​cult ​of the ​mead's guardian ​— ​a silence ‌he shares ​with ‌nearly every ​figure of ‌the ​mead myth ‌and ​one that ‌marks him ​as ​a literary-mythological ‌figure rather ‌than ‌an independently ​culted power.[1]

​What ‌can be ​said runologically ​is ‌the rendering ​itself: in ​the ‌Younger Futhark ​the ‌name would ​have been ​carved ​ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ, and ​here the ‌sixteen-rune ‌row genuinely ​underspecifies — ‌ᛏ ​writes both ​/t/ and ‌/d/, ‌ᚴ covers ‌both /k/ ‌and ​/g/, and ‌the ​row does ​not separate ​short ‌vowels from ​long, so ​the ​normalized transliteration ‌suttunkr is ​a ​reconstruction from ​the manuscript ‌spelling, ​not a ‌reading of ​stone. ‌No Viking-Age ​carving ‌of the ‌theonym is ​known; ​the manuscript ​spelling of ​the ‌Eddas remains ‌the primary ​witness.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.vv. Suttung, mead.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix; PuniCodex original-scripts record for suttungr.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Suttungr's ‌medieval presence ‌runs through ​the skalds ​rather than ​the saga ​prose-writers. His ​name survives in ‌the kennings ​of the ​court poets ‌— verse ​called the ‌mead of ​Suttungr, the ‌treasure of the ​giants, Gunnlöð's ​lost wealth ​— so ​that every ​stanza that ‌named poetry ​named him; ‌the corpus of ​skaldic verse ‌treats his ​story as ‌common property, ​the shared ‌myth behind ​the craft's ​own vocabulary.[1] The ‌saga tradition ‌knows him ‌only inside ​that frame: ​when kings' ‌and family ​sagas quote ​praise-poetry, the kenning ‌machinery of ​the mead ​myth is ‌the technical ​foundation, and ‌Suttungr stands ​silently inside ​it.

The myth's ​older strata ​surface obliquely. ‌Hávamál's 'wise ‌Fjalar' of ​the drinking-verses ‌and the ​dwarf Fjalar ​of Snorri's narrative ‌have long ‌been read ‌as a ‌trace of ​variant tellings ‌— the ​mead-hall's host ​variously giant or ​dwarf — ‌a reminder ​that the ​story Snorri ​smooths into ‌one plot ​circulated in ​competing shapes before ​him.[2] What ‌the medieval ​record never ‌does is ​moralize him ‌away: from ​Hávamál's ring-oath ‌to the kennings' ‌accounting, the ​tradition keeps ​the theft's ​cost visible, ​and keeps ‌the giant's ‌name on ​the treasure.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 1 (kennings for poetry), trans. Faulkes (1998); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. mead.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.vv. Fjalar, mead of poetry.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Suttungr ​is the ‌rare giant ‌who loses ​nothing through ​his own ​fault. He ​avenged ​his father ‌lawfully, took ​ransom in ​the manner ‌of his ​world, stored ‌his ​treasure in ‌stone, set ​his own ​child as ​its guard, ​and ​refused a ‌bargain he ​had every ‌right to ​refuse. Then ‌a god ​lied ‌to his ​brother, seduced ‌his daughter, ​and emptied ​his mountain ‌— and ‌the ‌tradition tells ​the story ​as a ‌triumph, and ​names the ​product after ‌the ​victim.[1]

The ​myth knows ‌what it ​is doing: ‌Hávamál makes ​Óðinn ​himself recite ​the perjury, ​and the ‌kennings keep ‌Suttungr's name ​on the ‌mead ​as if ​to balance ‌the account. ‌Poetry, the ‌North seems ‌to say, ​is ‌not a ​thing that ​can be ​owned — ‌but it ​can be ​hoarded, ​and the ‌difference between ​the giant's ​vault and ​the god's ‌gift ​is only ‌the auger-hole ​between them. ‌Every verse ​ever drunk ‌from the ‌vats ​of Óðrerir, ​Boðn, and ​Són is ​poured, in ‌the end, ‌from Suttungr's ​loss.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál 3-6, trans. Faulkes (1998).
  2. Hávamál 104-110 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
