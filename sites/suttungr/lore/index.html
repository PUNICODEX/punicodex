The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ

The name in its original Norse form. Suttungr (ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ) is attested in the source tradition — “Heavy with foundation”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

suttungr

The plain suttungr form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Suttungr

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Suttungr. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Suttungr.com → suttungr.com

The non-ASCII characters in Suttungr are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Suttungr.

Pronunciation

How Suttungr was spoken

/ˈsut.tuŋɡr/ Old Norse Reconstruction
S- Voiceless alveolar sibilant [s], as in English 'stone'; Old Norse initial s is plain, never voiced.
-u- Short high back rounded [u], as in English 'put', held brief — Old Norse short u, not the long ú.
-tt- The long (geminate) stop [tː] straddling the two syllables, held audibly as in modern Icelandic and Italian 'notte'.
-u- Second short high back rounded [u], identical to the first — the name keeps its vowel uniform.
-ngr Velar nasal plus stop [ŋɡ] closing on a syllabic trill [r̩] — the nominative ending -r, sounded as a true r, not swallowed.

Modern Approximation

'SOOT-toongr' — two beats, the first stressed, the double t held a fraction long in the middle, and the final -r trilled as a syllable of its own, as in Icelandic Suttungur.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse Suttungr, son of the giant Gillingr, brother of Baugi and father of Gunnlöð; the name is attested only for this giant.
  • Younger Futhark ᛋᚢᛏᛏᚢᚾᚴᚱ (normalized suttunkr), the Viking-Age runic rendering — the short-twig row writes t and d alike, and ᚴ covers both k and g.
  • Manuscript Latin Suttungr, unchanged — the Icelandic codices of the Poetic and Prose Edda write the name in plain Latin bookhand.
  • Modern Icelandic Suttungur, with the regular modern nominative ending -ur replacing medieval -r.

The Accent / Script Rule

Suttungr is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — no diacritic, no distinctive letter for the ASCII alphabet to lose. The figure's centrality to the mead myth vastly outruns his name's orthography; only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input.

05

Giant, Guardian of Mead

The Jötunn Who Hoarded Poetry in the Mountain

Suttungr, son of Gillingr and brother of Baugi, is the giant who came to own the mead of poetry — the blood of wise Kvasir, brewed by the dwarfs Fjalar and Galar and yielded up as ransom for their lives. He stored it in the mountain Hnitbjörg, in the three vats Óðrerir, Boðn, and Són, and set his own daughter Gunnlöð to guard it. His whole mythology is the story of that hoard and its loss: Óðinn came disguised as the wanderer Bölverkr, drilled through the rock, lay three nights with Gunnlöð, drank the vats dry, and flew home as an eagle with Suttungr's wings one beat behind him. From that flight, every poet drinks.

The Mead of Poetry

Kvasir's blood mixed with honey — the drink that makes a man a poet or a scholar, hoarded by Suttungr in three vats deep inside the mountain.

Hnitbjörg

The 'clashing rocks' or beating-rocks where Suttungr set his treasure — the mountain stronghold no hand could enter until an auger made a door.

Gunnlöð on the Golden Seat

The daughter set to guard the vats, who gave Óðinn three nights and three drinks — and wept when the price of the mead was paid in a false oath.

The Auger Rati

The drill-bit whose mouth gnawed through the mountain — Óðinn holding the shaft, Baugi boring, and a snake slipping through the hole at the end.

Sacred Symbols

The vats Óðrerir, Boðn, and Són The three vessels of the poetry-mead — 'mind-stirrer', 'offering', and 'atonement' — emptied in three drinks
Hnitbjörg The mountain vault of the mead — treasure sealed in stone, poetry kept from the world
The auger Rati The boring tool that made the snake-road into the rock — the slender breach through which all poetry escaped
The eagle form Óðinn's flight with the mead, pursued by Suttungr in eagle shape — the chase every kenning remembers
The skerry at flood The tide-washed rock where Suttungr stranded the dwarf-murderers until they paid him the mead — the ransom that made him poetry's gaoler
06

Mythology

Stories of Suttungr

Suttungr's dossier is one continuous story told in two keys: Snorri's Skáldskaparmál narrates the mead's making, hoarding, and theft as prose history, while Hávamál lets Óðinn himself remember it in verse — a man confessing, between counsels, to seduction, perjury, and grand larceny. Between them stands the corpus's fullest portrait of a giant who was never the fool of the story.

Skáldskaparmál 3-4

How Suttungr Won the Mead

When the Æsir and the Vanir made peace, they sealed the truce by spitting into a vat, and from the spittle they fashioned a man, Kvasir, wisest of all beings — no question was asked him he could not answer. He travelled the world teaching wisdom, until the dwarfs Fjalar and Galar killed him in private, drained his blood into two vats and a kettle named Óðrerir, Boðn, and Són, and mixed it with honey: that is the mead of poetry, of which whoever drinks becomes a skald or a sage.

The dwarfs' violence did not end there. They invited the giant Gillingr to row with them and capsized the boat — Gillingr, who could not swim, drowned — and when his wife wept at the news, Galar dropped a millstone on her head as she passed under the lintel. Then Suttungr, Gillingr's son, came for the reckoning. He seized the dwarfs, carried them out to sea, and stranded them on a skerry that the tide would cover. They begged for their lives and offered the precious mead as ransom, and Suttungr accepted: blood-guilt paid in blood-brew. He carried the mead home, stored it in the mountain called Hnitbjörg, and set his daughter Gunnlöð to watch over it. This is why poetry is called Kvasir's blood, the dwarfs' drink — and Suttungr's mead.

Skáldskaparmál 5-6

Bölverkr at the Mountain

Óðinn set out to take the mead, and he did not take it by force. Disguised as a wanderer who named himself Bölverkr — 'evil-doer', the one honest name in the whole affair — he came upon nine slaves mowing hay for Baugi, Suttungr's brother, sharpened their scythes on a whetstone, and let them kill each other in the quarrel over it. Then he offered Baugi to do nine men's work for the summer, for one wage: a drink of Suttungr's mead. Baugi warned that he had no say over his brother's treasure but agreed to try.

When the summer was done, Suttungr flatly refused the bargain. Bölverkr then produced the auger called Rati and had Baugi bore through the mountain to the mead-chamber; when Baugi claimed the hole went through, Bölverkr blew into it and chips flew back in his face — the first tunnel was a cheat — and at the second boring the chips flew inward. Then Bölverkr turned himself into a snake, wriggled into the auger-hole, and Baugi stabbed after him with the drill and missed. Inside, he came to Gunnlöð, lay with her for three nights, and she granted him three drinks of the mead: at the first he emptied Óðrerir, at the second Boðn, at the third Són — the whole hoard. Then he took eagle shape and flew, and Suttungr, taking eagle shape himself, flew after him.

Skáldskaparmál 6; Hávamál 104-110

The Eagle Chase and the Rhymester's Share

The Æsir saw Óðinn's flight coming and set out vats in the court of Ásgarðr, and when he came in over the wall with Suttungr's eagle at his tail-feathers, he spewed the mead up into the vessels. But Suttungr had come so close that Óðinn, in his fear, sent some of the mead out backwards — and that portion no one bothered to gather: it was left for anyone who wanted it, and it is called the rhymester's share, the bad poet's portion. The good mead Óðinn gives to the Æsir and to those men who know how to compose.

Hávamál lets the god tell his own version, and it is less heroic. He boasts that he got little by silence in Suttungr's hall and spoke many words to his advantage; he records that Gunnlöð gave him a drink of the precious mead from her golden seat, and that the reward he paid her was poor — 'her whole heart, her sorrowful spirit'; he remembers letting Rati's mouth gnaw the rock and the paths of the giants standing over and under him. And he confesses the cost to others: the frost-giants came to Hár's hall asking after Bölverkr, whether he had reached the gods or Suttungr had slain him — and Óðinn, the poem says, had sworn a ring-oath, and who now can trust his oaths? He had robbed Suttungr of his mead and made Gunnlöð weep. The verses that frame the memory call the whole episode a drunken night at wise Fjalar's, from which a man comes home wiser — and the forgetfulness-heron broods over the drink of every mortal.

Skáldskaparmál, þulur and kennings

The Giant Poetry Is Named After

Suttungr lost the treasure and kept the name. Skáldskaparmál's catalogue of kennings for poetry runs through his story at every turn: verse is the mead of Suttungr, the find of Óðinn, the drink of the dwarfs, the liquid of Óðrerir, the freight of the eagle, Gunnlöð's lost wealth. No other jötunn is so woven into the language of the skalds' own craft — every poet who named his art named its former owner.

The þulur, the metrical name-lists preserved with the Edda, hold him among the jǫtna heiti, the giants' names, in the company of his mountain-kin — a gaoler catalogued with the figures he belongs to. And the handbooks note the strangeness of his standing: Suttungr is called wise, keeps a hall the god himself praises, and bargains in good faith at every step of the story — a giant whose only crime was to own what a god decided to steal.

Extended Lore

Suttungr is the rare giant who loses nothing through his own fault. He avenged his father lawfully, took ransom in the manner of his world, stored his treasure in stone, set his own child as its guard, and refused a bargain he had every right to refuse. Then a god lied to his brother, seduced his daughter, and emptied his mountain — and the tradition tells the story as a triumph, and names the product after the victim. The myth knows what it is doing: Hávamál makes Óðinn himself recite the perjury, and the kennings keep Suttungr's name on the mead as if to balance the account. Poetry, the North seems to say, is not a thing that can be owned — but it can be hoarded, and the difference between the giant's vault and the god's gift is only the auger-hole between them. Every verse ever drunk from the vats of Óðrerir, Boðn, and Són is poured, in the end, from Suttungr's loss.
