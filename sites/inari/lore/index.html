The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

稲荷

The name in its original Japanese form. Inari (稲荷) is attested in the source tradition — “Rice load”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

inari

The plain inari form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Inari

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Inari. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding

The non-ASCII characters in Inari are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Inari.

05

Kami of Rice, Fertility, and Foxes

Guardian of the Harvest and Merchant Prosperity

Inari is one of the most widely worshipped kami in Japan. Originally a deity of rice and agriculture, Inari became over the centuries a patron of fertility, sake, swordsmiths, merchants, and worldly success. The vermillion torii gates and fox statues of Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyōto are among the most iconic images of Japanese religion.

Rice and Harvest

Inari governs the rice plant, the foundation of the Japanese diet and economy.

Fox Messengers

Kitsune serve Inari, acting as omens, guardians, and intermediaries.

Merchant Success

Traders and craftsmen pray to Inari for prosperity and protection.

Fushimi Inari

The head shrine in Kyōto is famous for its thousands of vermillion torii gates.

Sacred Symbols

Vermillion torii gates The thousands of gates donated by worshippers at Fushimi Inari and other Inari shrines
Fox (kitsune) Inari's messenger, often depicted holding a key to the rice granary or a jewel in its mouth
Rice sheaf The agricultural basis of Inari's cult
Jewel A magical gem associated with the fox and with prosperity
06

Mythology

Stories of Inari

Inari has no single canonical myth. The kami's identity is distributed across agricultural rites, local shrine legends, and the fox lore of the Edo period.

Agricultural cult

Deity of the Rice Plant

Inari was first worshipped as the spirit of the rice plant itself. The Nihon Shoki records that an Inari shrine was established in 711 CE at the request of the clan head Hata no Irogu, making it one of the oldest documented shrines in Japan. Rice, sake, and fertility remained at the center of Inari devotion.

Fox lore

The Fox as Messenger

By the medieval period, the fox (kitsune) had become Inari's principal messenger. Foxes were believed to possess magical powers, to shape-shift into human form, and to act as omens. Shrine statues often show a pair of foxes, one with a rice sheaf and one with a jewel, guarding the entrance.

Edo period

Patron of Commerce

As Japan's cities grew, merchants and artisans adopted Inari as a patron of worldly success. Small Inari shrines appeared in shops, factories, and entertainment districts. The kami who had once protected the rice field now protected the ledger and the forge.

Extended Lore

Inari teaches that the sacred is not separate from the everyday. The rice in the bowl, the fox at the shrine gate, and the merchant's account book all belong to the same divine economy. Prosperity, in Inari's world, is not greed but the abundance that allows life to continue.
