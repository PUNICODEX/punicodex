The Two Spirits at the First Dawn (Yasna 30)

The oldest stratum of Zoroastrian scripture is not a ​battle story but a choice. In Yasna 30.3–5 Zarathustra hymns 'the two spirits in the beginning, the twins renowned as the Better and the Bad in thought, word, and deed... when the ‌two spirits met, they created life and not-life, and settled that at the last the worst existence would be for the followers of the Lie, but the Best Mind for the followers of ​Truth.' The Bad Spirit — aŋrō mainiiuš — 'chose not-being' in the deed he performed. Later tradition hardened the twinhood into two eternities, but the Gāthās themselves insist on what scandalized later ‌theologians: evil is primordial as a choice, not as a substance. The myth means that good and evil are first of all directions of the will, and every human soul stands where ​the two spirits once stood.

The Assault on the Sky (Bundahišn 1)

The Sasanian Greater Bundahišn (ch. 1) narrates the attack. Ahriman, in his darkness below, glimpses Ohrmazd's luminous creation and rises, ‌envious, to destroy it. Ohrmazd — knowing the end belongs to him — offers peace: let the two realms keep to themselves for 9,000 years. Ahriman refuses; but at the first recitation ​of the Ahuna Vairya prayer he is thrown into stupor for three thousand years, from which the demoness Jahi the Whore wakes him with a shout. He smashes through the sky — ‌egg-like boundary of the ordered world — from the north, and his demons pour in after him. Only then does Ohrmazd set creation in motion against him. The myth means that evil's ​first act is always an invasion of someone else's order: the Lie owns nothing it does not break into.

Counter-Creation (Bundahišn 23)

Ahriman's characteristic act in the Bundahišn is not creation but ​counterfeiting. Against Ohrmazd's sky he set the demon of winter; against water, drought and salt; against the earth, the burrowing demons; against plants, poison and the hundredfold diseases; against the sacred cattle, wolves ‌and hunger; against man, the lie, lust, and untimely death. The text counts the diseases he fashioned at 99,999. Nothing in his inventory is original — everything is a wound answering a ‌good thing, exactly as later doctrine says: he could not make a world of his own, and so made war on the one he found. The myth gives Zoroastrianism its sharpest answer ‌to the problem of evil: the Devil has no substance of his own, only a talent for damage — and what can only destroy is already defeated in principle.

A Meditation

Ahriman is the rarest thing in mythology: a god of evil whom his own tradition insists is not a god. The Gāthās will ‌not let him be a second creator or an equal power; they make him a choice — the will that looked on truth and chose otherwise. What later centuries built into a ‌towering demon-king began as something more unsettling: the recognition that evil needs no substance, only a decision, and that the decision is available to everyone, always.

This is why Zoroastrian eschatology is ‌so strange to readers raised on Norse or Christian endings. There is no last battle whose outcome is uncertain, no twilight in which evil wins the field. Ahriman's defeat is announced at ‌his first appearance; his entire mythology is a working-out of a foregone conclusion — which means the drama was never really about him. It is about us, standing where he stood, asked ​the question he was asked, and answering.
