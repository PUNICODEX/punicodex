Why Metztli belongs in your address bar

The ASCII form metztli looks like a word, but it is only a shadow of the name. Metztli is the moon — not merely the satellite that governs the night, but a luminous presence at the heart of Nahuatl cosmology, calendar, and ritual. In the classical Nahuatl of the Aztec and their neighbors, the moon was personified, watched, and addressed. To type metztli in lowercase is to treat that presence as raw data; to restore Metztli with its proper capital is to recognize a named power. This post tells the longer story: where the word comes from, how it sounded in the mouths of Nahuatl speakers, what myths and ceremonies gathered around the moon, and why the restored form now resolves as a working domain. The argument throughout is simple — the original spelling is not an upgrade; it is the name.

Overview

Metztli (metztli) — Moon — belongs to the Nahuatl tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Moon". The name means "Moon". For the Nahuatl-speaking peoples of central Mexico, including the Mexica (Aztecs) of Tenochtitlan, the moon was no mere astronomical body. It was a deity or personified force with its own history, its own wounds, and its own role in the great calendar that ordered agriculture, warfare, and festival life. The moon measured time, governed certain rituals, and appeared in origin stories that explained why it shone less brightly than the sun.

PuniCodex restores the name as Metztli and serves its temple at its temple. The restoration needs no distinctive letters or diacritics its ASCII form would lose, which places the name in Tier 2. The plain ASCII form metztli survives as a modern convenience imposed by the early domain-name system; the restoration, not the fallback, is the form the project defends as philologically complete. The capital M and the full alphabetic spelling signal that this is a proper noun, a sacred name, and a scholarly citation form, not a lowercase keyword or a registry hack.

The Name

The name is attested in colonial Nahuatl sources as Metztli. Etymologically it means "Moon".

The spelling metztli follows the conventions of classical Nahuatl orthography established by Franciscan friars in the sixteenth century and refined by modern linguists. The initial M is capitalized in the restoration because the word functions as a proper noun — the name of a celestial power. The consonant cluster tz represents an alveolar affricate, [ts], a single sound in Nahuatl that English speakers tend to split. The final tl is a lateral affricate, [tɬ], one of the most distinctive phonemes of the language and notoriously difficult for speakers of European languages. The vowel i closes the word with a bright, short sound. In Nahuatl, every syllable is pronounced, and stress usually falls on the penultimate syllable, making the spoken name something like METZ-tli.

The ASCII form metztli preserves all of these letters but strips away the capitalization that marks the word as a divine name. It also flattens the name into the visual register of a password or a username. The Unicode restoration Metztli recovers the dignity of the scholarly citation form. It says, in effect: this is not a string; this is a name.

Etymology and Linguistic Roots

The classical Nahuatl word metztli means "moon" or "month". The same root underlies the count of months and the observation of lunar phases. In Nahuatl thought, the moon and the month were not two separate ideas held together by a pun; they were a single continuum. The moon was the visible face of the temporal cycle that the word named.

The etymology is internal to the Uto-Aztecan family, to which Nahuatl belongs. Related words appear across Uto-Aztecan languages with meanings connected to moon, month, or night. Within Nahuatl, the root is primary and underived: metztli is not a transparent compound but a lexical staple, one of the basic nouns that every speaker would know. That very simplicity gives the word its power. Where Greek names the moon Selene and derives a host of poetic compounds, Nahuatl uses a single, compact term that is also the everyday word for the thing itself. The sacred name and the common noun are one.

Some colonial sources distinguish between Metztli the moon and other lunar or night-related powers, including Tecciztecatl, a figure associated with the moon in the cosmogony of the fifth sun. The relationship between Metztli and Tecciztecatl is debated among scholars: some treat them as overlapping names for the same celestial body, while others see Tecciztecatl as a more specific mythic actor in the story of the sun's birth. What is clear is that metztli remained the ordinary and ritual word for the moon throughout the colonial period and into modern Nahuatl.

The Moon in Nahuatl Cosmology

Nahuatl cosmology divided the universe into vertical and horizontal registers: the thirteen heavens above, the nine levels of the underworld below, and the four cardinal directions around the living earth. The moon moved across these boundaries, appearing in the sky, disappearing, and reappearing in ways that invited interpretation. Its phases marked the passage of the months; its eclipses were moments of cosmic danger; its monthly renewal was a small image of the larger cycles of creation and destruction.

The Nahuatl calendar was a dual system: the xiuhpohualli, a 365-day solar year divided into eighteen months of twenty days, and the tonalpohualli, a 260-day ritual count. The moon did not govern either count directly in the way that, for instance, the sun governed the solar year, but lunar observation shaped the timing of certain rites and the interpretation of days. The word metztli named not only the body in the sky but also the interval it marked. A metztli was a month, and the completion of a month was a small ritual event.

In the cosmogony recorded in sources such as the Florentine Codex, the present era — the Fifth Sun — began when the gods gathered at Teotihuacan to create a new sun. Two figures volunteered to become the sun: the proud Nanahuatzin and the wealthy Tecciztecatl. When the moment came, Nanahuatzin leaped into the fire and rose as the sun, while Tecciztecatl hesitated, followed, and rose as the moon. The gods, seeing the new moon shining as brightly as the sun, threw a rabbit at it to dim its face. That rabbit, in Nahuatl and Mesoamerican tradition, is visible on the moon's surface still. This story explains both the moon's lesser light and the markings on its face.

Mythology

The myths surrounding Metztli are less a single coherent biography than a cluster of stories about the moon's nature, origin, and role. The moon is sibling or rival to the sun, guardian of the night, and a body marked by violence or sacrifice.

The Rabbit in the Moon

The most widespread Nahuatl explanation for the moon's markings is the rabbit. When the moon rose too bright after the creation of the Fifth Sun, the gods hurled a rabbit at it to reduce its radiance. The rabbit stuck, and its shape can still be seen in the lunar surface. This myth transforms an astronomical observation — the dark patches on the moon — into a narrative of cosmic regulation. The moon is not allowed to rival the sun; its light is deliberately diminished so that night remains night.

The Sacrifice of Tecciztecatl

In the Florentine Codex account of the Fifth Sun, Tecciztecatl is the deity who becomes the moon through a failed bid for the sun. His story is one of hesitation and second place. Where Nanahuatzin offers himself humbly and is transformed into the glorious sun, Tecciztecatl clings to his jade, feathers, and coral, and must be shamed into the fire. His lunar afterlife is a caution against pride and a reminder that cosmic order depends on each body accepting its proper station. The moon, in this telling, is the deity who almost became the sun.

The Moon as Calendar-Keeper

Beyond origin myths, Metztli governs the smaller rhythms of life. The monthly cycle determines planting and harvesting cues, menstrual observance, and the timing of certain offerings. Farmers watched the moon; midwives and healers noted its phases; priests aligned some rites with its appearance and disappearance. The moon was not a distant god but a practical presence, present in the calendar, the field, and the body.

Calendar and Ritual

The Nahuatl month, metztli, gave its name to the lunar interval and to the agricultural rhythm. Each of the eighteen months of the solar year had its own ceremonies, and while these were not strictly lunar, the visible waxing and waning of the moon provided a natural frame for observing time. Priests and calendar specialists, the tonalpouhque, tracked both counts and interpreted the significance of days.

Specific rites addressed the moon and the night. Offerings might include incense, food, flowers, and bloodletting. The moon, like the sun, was a source of light and heat, if a lesser one; it deserved respect and propitiation. Colonial sources describe Nahuatl speakers addressing the moon at its rising, asking it to carry prayers or to grant good fortune. The moon was also associated with dreams, divination, and the boundary between waking and sleeping.

In some accounts, the moon shares qualities with water, fertility, and the feminine. These associations are not as sharply defined in Nahuatl sources as they are in Greek or Roman tradition, but they appear in ritual contexts, in the agricultural calendar, and in the moon's pairing with the sun as a complementary cosmic pair.

Symbols and Iconography

The iconography of Metztli centers on a small set of images that recur in codices, sculpture, and oral tradition:

  • The crescent moon — The simplest and most direct symbol, marking the waxing and waning body.
  • The rabbit — The figure hurled at the moon in the creation story, visible on the lunar surface.
  • Tecciztecatl's paraphernalia — Jade, quetzal feathers, and coral, the wealth the lunar deity could not leave behind.
  • Night and water — Associated elements that cluster around the moon in cosmological diagrams and ritual texts.

Colonial-era images often show the moon as a crescent or disk, sometimes with a rabbit inside it. These images carry forward pre-Hispanic conventions while adapting them to European representational styles.

Archaeology and Evidence

The evidence for Metztli comes from several kinds of sources. The Florentine Codex, compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún and his indigenous informants in the sixteenth century, is the richest textual witness; its accounts of the Fifth Sun and the calendar preserve the myths and rites associated with the moon. The Codex Borgia and other pre-Hispanic painted books include lunar and celestial imagery. Stone monuments, calendar wheels, and temple alignments at sites such as Tenochtitlan and Teotihuacan reflect the importance of celestial observation in Nahua religion.

Modern Nahuatl communities continue to use words descended from metztli for moon and month, preserving a continuous thread from the colonial lexicon to the present. The name has never stopped being spoken; it has only been waiting for a writing system and an internet infrastructure that would treat it as a name rather than a string.

Cultural Legacy

Metztli's legacy extends through Mesoamerican history into contemporary culture. In Mexico and among Nahuatl-speaking communities, the moon remains metztli or its modern reflex. The rabbit in the moon is still a familiar image, told to children and reproduced in art. The story of the Fifth Sun, with Tecciztecatl's failed ascent, appears in schoolbooks, murals, and national mythology.

For scholars, Metztli is a window into Nahuatl cosmology. The name's dual meaning — moon and month — reveals how tightly astronomy, timekeeping, and religion were woven together. For artists and writers, the moon in Nahuatl tradition offers an alternative to European lunar iconography: not a pale, romantic body, but a marked, diminished, and still powerful light. For indigenous-language advocates, restoring the spelling and pronunciation of Metztli is part of a larger effort to keep Nahuatl visible and audible in digital spaces.

Selected Sources

The account of Metztli given here rests on a small set of authoritative witnesses. The Florentine Codex preserves the Nahuatl texts, calendar lore, and the story of the Fifth Sun in the words of Sahagún's indigenous informants. Frances Karttunen's Analytical Dictionary of Nahuatl secures the form and meaning of metztli. Miguel León-Portilla's studies of Aztec thought provide the philosophical framework, while Mary Miller and Karl Taube's Gods and Symbols of Ancient Mexico and the Maya anchor the iconographic evidence. Kay Almere Read and Jason J. González's Handbook of Mesoamerican Mythology offers the wider comparative setting.
