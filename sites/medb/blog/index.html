The hidden history behind Medb

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Medb is the opposite case, and the more instructive one: a queen so famous that her war is the national epic of Ireland, attached to a name so plain that Old Irish itself wrote it in unbroken ASCII. There is nothing to restore, and everything to explain. This post walks the full record — name, script, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the greatest queen of the Celtic world has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII medb.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Medb (medb) — Sovereignty, War, Intoxication · 'The intoxicating one' — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Sovereignty, War, Intoxication".

Medb of Crúachan is the ruling queen of Connacht and the driving will of the Táin Bó Cúailnge, the longest and greatest tale of the Ulster Cycle: the woman who audited fortunes with her husband Ailill in the pillow-talk, found herself one bull short of equality, and marched the hosting of four provinces against Ulster to take the Donn of Cúailnge. The tradition never decides whether she is a goddess remembered as a queen or a queen worshipped as a goddess — and the scholarship has argued the point for seventy years.

PuniCodex restores the name as Medb — identical to the ASCII input, because normalized Old Irish writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the queen.

The Name

The name is attested in Old and Middle Irish as Medb (genitive Medba; Modern Irish Meadhbh, Anglicized Maeve), and it doubles as a common noun: medb means both 'mead, the intoxicating drink' and 'intoxicating, she who intoxicates'. The Dictionary of the Irish Language prints both senses, and the etymology is not in dispute: the word continues Proto-Indo-European médhu, 'sweet drink, honey-drink' — the same word as English mead, Greek méthu ('wine'), and Sanskrit mádu. Welsh meddw, 'drunk', is the Brythonic cognate of the adjective.

The name's meaning is its interpretation. Irish inauguration ideology bound the kingship to a drink and a marriage — the banais ríghe, the 'wedding-feast of kingship', in which the goddess of the territory offered the king the cup. A queen named 'the intoxicating one' reads as that ideology incarnate, and Proinsias Mac Cana's foundational studies of the king-and-goddess theme made Medb its type-case. The tradition itself kept a namesake to confirm the pattern: Medb Lethderg of Tara, of whom the Book of Leinster records that she slept with nine kings of Ireland and that no king ruled at Tara who had not slept with her.

The Original Script

Old Irish writes the name Medb in the Insular Latin script of the great codices — the Táin survives in Lebor na hUidre, the Book of the Dun Cow (written at Clonmacnoise around 1106), and in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster. The spelling requires no diacritic: plain Latin letters, exactly the letters of the keyboard.

The earlier monumental alphabet, ogham, attests the name-type exactly once — and in the most suggestive place in Ireland. Within Oweynagat, the Cave of the Cats beneath the Rathcroghan complex that the tales make Medb's royal seat, stands an ogham stone reading VRAICCI MAQI MEDVVI, '(the stone of) Fráech, son of Medb'. Fráech is himself an Ulster Cycle figure, the hero of Táin Bó Fraích and Medb's nephew in the genealogies — though honest epigraphy notes that medb is also a man's name in the ogham record, and the coincidence cannot be pressed into identification. Still: the queen's name-type, carved in Ireland's oldest script, in the cave under her own rath.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed Old Irish pronunciation is /ˈmʲeðv/ — one short syllable:

  • M- — slender (palatalized) m, the m of 'muse', not 'mud'
  • -e- — short stressed e, the 'e' of 'bed', held brief
  • -d- — lenited d, the soft voiced 'th' of English 'this', written plain in the manuscripts
  • -b — final lenited b, [v] — the ancestor of the -bh of Modern Irish Meadhbh and the v of 'Maeve'

The closest approximation for a modern speaker is 'MYETHV'. The living Irish form Meadhbh has collapsed the middle consonant entirely and sounds simply 'mayv' — which is why the Anglicized Maeve is one of the few Irish names English speakers already pronounce correctly.

Mythology

Medb's mythology is the Táin Bó Cúailnge, with the death-tale Aided Meidbe as its coda.

The Pillow-Talk. One night at Crúachan, Medb and Ailill fell into a bolster-conversation about whose fortune was greater, and to settle it they had every treasure counted — cups, rings, cattle, sheep, horses. The tally came out exactly even, save one item: the great bull Finnbennach, born in Medb's herd, had scorned to be counted a woman's chattel and walked over into Ailill's. To restore the equality she would not live without, Medb sought the Donn of Cúailnge from Dáire mac Fiachna on a year's loan at princely terms. Dáire agreed — until her drunken messengers boasted they would have taken the bull by force anyway. The loan died of the boast, and Medb resolved on war.

The Bride-Gift Speech. The Táin lets Medb state her own terms. Daughter of Eochu Feidlech, high king of Ireland, wooed by the kings of Ireland, she refused them all: 'for I asked a strange bride-gift such as no woman before me had asked of a man of the men of Ireland — a husband without meanness, without jealousy, without fear.' Such a man she found in Ailill mac Máta. She brought him the bride-gift, and it is into her province, not his, that he came. The Táin is blunt about the arithmetic: he is king of Connacht because he is married to Medb of Crúachan.

The Debility and the Boy at the Ford. Medb timed the raid for the pangs of the Ulstermen, the debility Macha laid on them for forcing her to race the king's horses while great with child. With Ulster abed, one boy held the fords: Cú Chulainn, untouched by the curse. Medb spent everything on him — terms, ambushes, satirists, her daughter Findabair promised to a dozen champions at once — and the hardest price fell on Ferdia, Cú Chulainn's foster-brother, plied with drink and promises until he took the ford and died there on the gae bolga.

The Battle of the Bulls. When the pangs lifted and Ulster rose, Medb's army broke at Gáirech and Irgáirech — but the Donn was already driven south, and she covered the retreat herself, in her gush of blood, asking Cú Chulainn's protection for her army's rear. The bulls settled the account themselves: they fought through the night across Ireland, the Donn trampled Finnbennach to pieces, carried the carcass through Connacht, and died of his wounds on his return to Cúailnge. Medb had her equality, and the tale's dry arithmetic: the two most valuable beasts in Ireland, both dead, and nothing left to count.

The Cheese of Inis Cloithrinn. Medb outlived Ailill and kept her sovereignty into age at her island on Lough Ree, where she bathed every morning at sunrise. She had killed her sister Clothru, and Clothru's son Furbaide — cut from his mother's womb, whence his name Ferbend, 'of the cutting' — measured her bathing place with practice casts until his sling flew true by habit. One sunrise his stone, a ball of hard cheese, struck her dead at the water's edge.

Symbols & Iconography

Medb's regalia are the furniture of her rath and the terms of her marriages — the most domestic and the most political attributes in the Irish corpus:

  • The marriage-bed of Crúachan — the site of the pillow-talk: sovereignty as an audit, equality counted treasure for treasure in the dark
  • The two bulls — Finnbennach and the Donn: the balance of power on four legs, dead on each other's horns by the tale's end
  • The bride-gift terms — no meanness, no jealousy, no fear: the queen's charter, demanded where a dowry was expected
  • The march of the four provinces — Connacht, the Leinster Galeóin, and the Ulster exiles under Fergus mac Róich: armies moved by a single insistence
  • Oweynagat, the Cave of the Cats — the Otherworld gate beneath her royal complex, whence the things of Samain come up in the tales

Archaeology & Evidence

Medb's mythic address is the best-documented royal landscape in Ireland: Crúachan Aí, the Rathcroghan complex in County Roscommon, a five-kilometre spread of more than two hundred and forty recorded monuments dominated by the great flat-topped Rathcroghan Mound. Beneath it opens Oweynagat, the natural cave with its two ogham stones — one of them the VRAICCI MAQI MEDVVI inscription. Her death-place, Inis Cloithrinn (Inchcleraun), survives as the largest island of Lough Ree, crowned by early monastic ruins. Her reputed grave is the great unexcavated Neolithic cairn (c. 3200 BCE) on the summit of Knocknarea above Sligo — Miosgán Meadhbha, 'Medb's heap' — which local tradition insists she fills standing upright, spear in hand, facing Ulster.

The textual witnesses are exact: Recension I of the Táin in Lebor na hUidre (c. 1106), the fullest recension in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster, and Cecile O'Rahilly's editions as the modern bases. J. P. Mallory's In Search of the Irish Dreamtime (2016) is the standard archaeological cross-examination of the whole epic — and its verdict, that the Táin tells us more about its medieval tellers than about the Iron Age, is the standing caution on every claim above.

Realm & Domain

Medb is catalogued under the domain 'Sovereignty, War, Intoxication' — three words that map onto her office, her deed, and her name. Sovereignty: she is the ruler whose consent makes kings, the one who chooses rather than is chosen. War: the Táin is her war, raised on a point of equality and waged without hesitation. Intoxication: her name is the drink and the state at once — the mead of the king-making feast and the recklessness of the raid are the same word working at two scales.

Across Cultures

The Welsh corpus preserves no Medb — but it preserves her word, meddw, 'drunk', and the Mabinogi stages the same sovereignty complex from the Welsh side: Rhiannon, the mounted marvel no rider can overtake, and Goewin, the footholder whose office is bound to Math's life, belong to the pattern Mac Cana documented for Ireland. Medb's deepest kin are lexical and ritual at once: the drink by which kings were made, and the goddess who offered it. The tradition kept a second Medb to prove the point — Medb Lethderg of Tara, who slept with nine kings of Ireland and let no man rule Tara without her. Her closest kin inside the lexicon are Macha, whose curse opened Ulster to her army, and the Mórrígan, who rides the same war-paths from the Otherworld side.

Cultural Legacy

The Táin's modern reception made Medb the most argued-over woman in Irish literature. Lady Gregory's Cuchulain of Muirthemne (1902) carried the epic into English; Thomas Kinsella's translation (1969), with Louis le Brocquy's ink drawings, fixed the Táin as the Irish national epic for the modern reader — and Medb at its centre as its engine rather than its ornament. Scholarship split over her nature: Mac Cana's euhemerized sovereignty goddess against the skeptics' medieval literary queen. Popular culture settled the argument in its own way: the Anglicized Maeve has become one of the most fashionable Irish girls' names in the English-speaking world, and 'Queen Maeve' — the woman who wrote her own terms, chose her own husbands, and out-argued every man in the tale — stands as a feminist icon far beyond the universities. The cairn on Knocknarea draws climbers every summer; the visitor centre at Rathcroghan interprets her rath; and in Irish itself Meadhbh never left.

The Scholarly Record

The primary witnesses are O'Rahilly's two editions of the Táin — Recension I (1976) and the Book of Leinster text (1967) — with Kinsella's translation as the standard English road in. The death-tale Aided Meidbe and the debility tale Noínden Ulad (edited by Vernam Hull, Celtica 8, 1968) complete the narrative dossier. The name is secured by the Dictionary of the Irish Language (s.v. medb) and Matasović's Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Celtic (*medu-). The sovereignty reading rests on Mac Cana's 'Aspects of the Theme of King and Goddess in Irish Literature' (Études Celtiques 7–8, 1955–59) and his Celtic Mythology (1970), with Sjoestedt's Gods and Heroes of the Celts (1949) as the older frame and Mallory (2016) as the standing cross-examination. Thurneysen's Die irische Helden- und Königsage (1921) remains the classic study of the Táin's composition; McManus's Guide to Ogam (1991) covers the Oweynagat inscription; Koch's Celtic Culture encyclopedia (2006) is the reference frame throughout.

A Meditation

Medb is the only great figure in the Celtic corpus who insists, in her own voice, on being counted equal — and then proves the point by going to war over the one item on which she was not. Read her as a goddess and she is sovereignty itself, the land auditing its king; read her as a woman and she is something rarer in medieval literature: a ruler whose motives are entirely legible and entirely her own. The tradition punishes her and cannot dismiss her. The raid ends in ruin, the bulls destroy each other, the covering of the retreat is hers to do in the tale's most humiliating passage — and afterward she goes home and keeps the throne, keeps her terms, keeps choosing, until a nephew's measured patience and a piece of cheese end her at her morning bath. Medb asks the question the audit poses: what would you move an army to keep equal — and what, when the counting is done, was ever actually at stake?

The Unicode Restoration

The restoration Medb is the honest minimum: normalized Old Irish writes the name in plain Latin letters, with no diacritic and no distinctive letter for ASCII to lose, so the restored form differs from the lowercase input only by the capital. That places the name in Tier 2 by the project's mechanical rule — a statement about orthography, not about standing. What the restoration does is canonical rather than orthographic: it fixes the Old Irish manuscript form, Medb, against the tidal pull of the Anglicized Maeve and the modern Meadhbh, and anchors the queen to the language the Táin was written in.

Four letters, no losses. The simplest transformation in the Celtic lexicon, and the one that carries the most argument.

The Domain Name

Because the name is pure ASCII, its domain was registered in the ordinary way long ago: medb.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection and is watched for release. No form of the name — medb.com, maeve.com, meadhbh.com — is currently owned, and this temple claims none. The temple lives at its canonical lexicon address, and if the plain-ASCII form ever drops, the collection will be watching. That is the whole of it: the restoration is a scholarly act, not a land-grab, and the queen who would not live with an unbalanced account would perhaps appreciate the patience of a project content to wait.

The Celtic Pantheon

Medb stands at the meeting point of the Celtic lexicon's two worlds. On the human side she belongs to the Ulster Cycle, the court epic of Conchobar's Ulster and its enemies: Cú Chulainn is her adversary, Fergus mac Róich her guide, and the fate of Deirdre and the sons of Uisliu the reason her army is full of Ulster exiles. On the divine side the sovereignty scholarship places her beside the great figures of the Mythological Cycle — Macha, who cursed the Ulstermen and cleared her road; the Mórrígan, the war-goddess whose paths cross hers; and behind them the elder pantheon of the Dagda and Lugh, the gods the Táin's medieval tellers had already demoted to ancestors. Whether Medb herself stands on the human or the divine side of that line is precisely the seventy-year argument her name provokes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Medb a goddess or a queen?

The Táin presents her as a queen and never says otherwise. The goddess reading is scholarly inference: her name means 'the intoxicating one' — the mead of the king-making feast — her consent makes and unmakes kings, and Irish inauguration ideology knew exactly such a figure. Mac Cana made the case; Mallory counsels caution; the debate is live.

How do you pronounce Medb?

Old Irish: /ˈmʲeðv/ — 'MYETHV', one syllable. Modern Irish Meadhbh and the Anglicized Maeve are simply 'mayv'.

Did Medb really exist?

No evidence says so. The Táin is set in a legendary pre-Christian age, and the archaeology of Rathcroghan — however magnificent — cannot confirm a single episode of the epic. What is real is the landscape: Crúachan, Oweynagat with its ogham stones, Inis Cloithrinn, and the Knocknarea cairn are all visitable places.

What is the ogham connection?

One stone in the Oweynagat cave at Rathcroghan reads VRAICCI MAQI MEDVVI, 'Fráech, son of Medb' — the name-type's only epigraphic attestation, at the queen's own mythic seat. It may commemorate a man named Medb rather than the queen's family; the coincidence is famous either way.

Why does PuniCodex claim no domain?

Because medb.com was registered by someone else long ago, in the ordinary way, before the collection existed. It is watched for release; nothing is claimed.

Typing Medb

Nothing could be simpler: m-e-d-b, four unmodified keys, and shift for the capital. The name is one of the very few in the lexicon that the ASCII keyboard types perfectly on the first try — which is precisely why it sits in Tier 2, and precisely why its domain was gone before the project began.

Sister Temples

Medb's temple keeps company with the other Celtic names whose plain-ASCII forms are held outside the collection and watched: the great fathers and lords of the Mythological Cycle, and the war-goddesses and sovereignty figures whose paths cross hers in the Táin and beyond.

Why This Restoration Matters

Medb proves the restoration doctrine from the negative side. Not every name needs rescuing from the address bar; some need rescuing from translation. 'Maeve' is a beautiful anglicization and a complete erasure — it drops the medieval manuscript form, the genitive Medba, the ogham MEDVVI, and above all the etymology that makes the queen legible: medb, the intoxicating one, the mead of the king-making feast. The restoration Medb costs nothing to type and returns everything that matters. That is what a canonical reference is for.
