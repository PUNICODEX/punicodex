Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ugayafukiaezu ​(ugayafukiaezu) — ‌Ancestor of ‌Jimmu ​· Cormorant ​grass-thatching ​unfinished — ​belongs to ​the ‌Japanese tradition, ​where it ​is ‌catalogued under ​the domain ‌"Ancestor ​of Jimmu". ‌The ​name means ​"Cormorant grass-thatching ​unfinished"[1].

​Ugayafukiaezu is ​one of ‌the ​most strangely ‌named figures ​in ‌Japanese myth. ​His ‌name describes ​the unfinished ‌thatched ​hut, covered ​with cormorant ‌feathers, ‌in which ‌he was ​born. ​As the ‌son ​of Hoori ​and the ‌sea ​princess Toyotama, ​and the ‌father ​of Emperor ‌Jimmu, he ​is ​the final ​link ​between the ‌ocean depths ‌and ​the imperial ‌line.[2]

PuniCodex ​restores ​the name ‌as Ugayafukiaezu ‌and ‌serves its ‌temple ​at its ‌temple. The ​restoration ​aSCII-equivalent form ​— a ‌description ​computed from ​the displayed ​form ‌itself. The ​plain ​ASCII form ​ugayafukiaezu survives ‌as ​a modern ‌convenience imposed ​by ‌the early ​domain-name system; ‌the ‌restoration, not ​the ​fallback, is ​the form ​the ‌project defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

Sources

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed pronunciation ‌of the ‌name is ​/ɯ.ɡa.ja.fɯ.ki.a.e.zɯ/ — ​Modern Standard ​Japanese (Hepburn).[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • U- ‌— Close ​back unrounded ​[ɯ]
  • -ga- ‌— Voiced ​velar stop ‌[ɡ] plus ​open central ‌[a]; from ​鵜 'cormorant'
  • ​-ya- — ​Palatal approximant ​[j] plus ​open central ‌[a]; from ​草 'grass'
  • ‌-fuki- — ​Voiceless bilabial ‌fricative [ɸ], ​close back ‌[ɯ], voiceless ​velar [k], ‌close front ​[i]; from ​葺 'thatch'
  • ‌-aezu — ‌Open central ‌[a], close-mid ​[e], voiced ​alveolar fricative ‌[z], close ​back [ɯ]; ​from 不合 ‌'unfinished/not joined'

​For the ​modern speaker, ‌the closest ​approximation is: ‌'OO-gah-yah-foo-kee-ah-eh-zoo' — ​many light ​syllables; the ​'f' is ​soft, made ‌with both ‌lips.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of ​the name:

  • ​Japanese — ‌鵜草葺不合命 (Ugayafukiaezu-no-mikoto), ‌'Cormorant Thatching ‌Unfinished' — ‌Kojiki, age-of-gods ​genealogy
  • Parents ‌— Hoori ​(Hikohohodemi) and ​Toyotama-bime — ​Kojiki I; ‌Nihon Shoki, ​book 1
  • ​Son — ​Emperor Jimmu ‌(Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko) — ​Kojiki I; ​Nihon Shoki, ​book 3

‌Ugayafukiaezu keeps ​its restoration ‌plain: plain ​Hepburn. His ‌strange name ​refers to ‌a birth ‌hut thatched ​with cormorant ​feathers and ​left unfinished. ​He is ‌the immediate ‌father of ​Emperor Jimmu.

Sources

  1. Kojiki, age-of-gods chapters (Hoori, Toyotama, Ugayafukiaezu, and Jimmu cycles), trans. Donald L. Philippi (University of Tokyo Press, 1968).

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name ‌is written ‌in ​Japanese ​characters as ​鵜草葺不合命. ​This ​original script ‌is ​the ​form against ‌which ​the ‌ASCII fallback ​ugayafukiaezu ‌and ​the PuniCodex ​restoration ​Ugayafukiaezu ​are measured: ​the ‌restoration ​preserves its ‌full ​diacritic ‌detail of ​the ‌written ​form, so ‌that ​a ​reader typing ‌the ‌modern ‌address still ​speaks ​the ‌ancient name.[1]

​The ​age-of-gods ‌names are ​written ​in ‌kanji that ​gloss ‌rather ​than transcribe. ​The ​full ​ceremonial form ‌is ‌鵜草葺不合命 ​(Ugayafukiaezu-no-mikoto), with ‌the ​honorific ​命 (mikoto): ‌鵜 ‌“cormorant,” ‌草 “grass, ‌reed,” ​葺 ‌“to thatch,” ​不合 ​“not ​joined, unfinished.” ‌The ​characters ​record the ​Kojiki's ‌own ​explanation of ​the ​name, ‌not a ​phonetic ‌spelling; ​medieval manuscripts ‌abbreviate ​the ‌compound variously, ‌and ​its ​reading has ​been ​debated ‌since the ‌earliest ​commentaries.

Sources

  1. Kojiki, age-of-gods chapters (Hoori, Toyotama, Ugayafukiaezu, and Jimmu cycles), trans. Donald L. Philippi (University of Tokyo Press, 1968).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ugayafukiaezu ​is one ‌of the ‌most ​strangely named ​figures ​in Japanese ​myth. His ​name ‌describes the ​unfinished thatched ​hut, ‌covered with ​cormorant ‌feathers, in ​which he ‌was ​born. As ​the son ​of ​Hoori and ​the ‌sea princess ​Toyotama, and ‌the ​father of ‌Emperor Jimmu, ​he ‌is the ​final ‌link between ​the ocean ​depths ‌and the ‌imperial line.[1]

‌Father ​of Jimmu

​His ‌fourth son, ​Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko, won ​the ‌succession and ​founded the ​Yamato ‌line as ​Emperor ‌Jimmu (Kojiki ​I).

Sea-Land ​Bridge

​Son of ​Hoori the ‌land-hunter ‌and Toyotama-bime, ​daughter ‌of the ​sea god ​Watatsumi ‌— the ‌marriage that ‌joined ‌coast and ​deep.

‌Unfinished Birth ​Hut

His ​name ​glosses the ‌cormorant-feather thatch ​of ​the Kojiki's ​birth ‌hut — ​and the ​roof ​never finished ‌(鵜草葺不合).

Imperial ​Ancestor

‌The last ​link ‌of the ​divine genealogy: ‌through ‌him the ​regalia line ​descends ​to the ​historic ‌emperors (Nihon ‌Shoki, books ​1–3).

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ugayafukiaezu's mythology is the ​final chapter of the Hoori–Toyotama cycle and ‌the prologue to the reign of Jimmu: one ‌night on a seashore, one unfinished roof, ​one farewell poem, and a lineage that ​steps out of the hut into history.[1]

The ​Cormorant-Feather Hut (Kojiki I)

The Kojiki (age ​of gods) tells how the sea princess ​Toyotama-bime, daughter of Watatsumi the sea god, came ‌up from the deep to bear the ​child of the land-hunter Hoori. On the ​seashore she built a hut and thatched ‌its roof with cormorant feathers, and as her ​hour came she warned her husband: do ‌not look inside. Hoori waited, but when ​the cry came he could not hold back; ‌he peered through the wall and saw ​his wife transformed into a great sea-creature ​— the Kojiki says an eight-fathom shark ​— coiled in labor. Toyotama knew herself shamed. ​She left the newborn where he lay, ​in the hut whose cormorant thatch had ‌never been finished, and the compilers give the ​child his name from the shelter itself: ‌Ugayafukiaezu, 'Cormorant-Thatching-Unfinished'. The scene survives because a ​roof was never completed: a goddess of the ‌sea delivering on land, and a lineage ​beginning in the gap between the elements.[2]

‌The Farewell Song (Kojiki I)

Before she ​returned to the sea, Toyotama spoke the poem ‌the Kojiki preserves as one of its ​most celebrated archaic songs: had you not ​shamed me, I would have come and gone ‌by the sea-road, and for ever eased ‌the way of the ships; but now ‌that you have looked, the crossing will ​be hard — the precise wording varies in ​translation, but Philippi's rendering keeps its reproach ‌intact. She left the child in the ​care of her younger sister Tamayori — 'Good ​Jewel' — who came up out of ‌the water to suckle him. In a ​text crowded with genealogies, the poem is the ​rare moment the myth turns elegy: a ‌mother who could have made the sea ​a road for her son instead leaves ‌him a song — and the imperial line ​inherits, with its treasures, a story about ​the price of looking.

The Aunt Who ​Stayed (Kojiki I)

Tamayori raised the boy of ​the unfinished hut, and when he was ‌grown the Kojiki says she became his ‌wife; the compilers record the union without comment, ​the sea line marrying back into itself. ‌Four sons were born: Itsuse, Inahi, Mikeiri, ​and the youngest, Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko. The Nihon Shoki's ​parallel account of the succession tells how the ‌three elder brothers marched east against the ‌defiant chiefs of the land and failed ‌— Itsuse fell at Kusaka, his army repulsed ‌— while the youngest, guided by the ​three-legged crow Yatagarasu sent down from heaven, ‌found the road through the mountains, defeated ​Nagasunehiko, and planted the Yamato line. The child ​of the roofless hut becomes the hinge ​on which the narrative turns from gods ‌to kings: from this point the chronicles stop ​telling of the divine age and begin, ​almost, to tell of a realm.

The ​Treasures Cross the Water (Nihon Shoki)

The Kojiki ‌and Nihon Shoki agree on the cargo ​of the line Ugayafukiaezu completes: the imperial ​regalia — the mirror, the sword Kusanagi, ​and the jewel — descended from Amaterasu with ‌Ninigi, passed down through Hoori's house, and ​went east with Jimmu on the expedition, ‌the divine mirror and sword carried against the ​rebels of the land. Later tradition placed ‌the unfinished hut's son at the exact ​point where the age of the gods ‌closes: everything celestial has now crossed into a ‌single human lineage. The Nihon Shoki's more ​sober parallel strips away wonders and keeps ​the genealogy, which is testimony of its own ​kind — whether read as myth or ​as charter, the story exists to make ‌one claim: that the family of the unfinished ‌roof was entrusted with heaven's treasures, and ​did not drop them.

Sources

  1. Nihon Shoki (Nihongi), books 1–3, trans. W. G. Aston (1896).
  2. Philippi, Donald L., Kojiki (Princeton University Press, 1969).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Ugayafukiaezu has ​no cult ‌and no ‌shrine — ​his whole ​existence is ​genealogical, and ​his meaning ​is that ‌of the ​custom his ​name preserves. ‌Japanese ethnographers ​documented birth ‌huts, ubuya, ​in which ‌mothers delivered ​and secluded ​themselves, persisting ​in parts ​of the ​country into ‌the twentieth ​century; the ‌Kojiki's cormorant-feather ​hut is ‌read against ​this custom ‌as its ​mythic charter. Medieval ‌Buddhist exegetes ​folded the ​age-of-gods narratives ‌into the ‌honji suijaku ‌framework, treating ​the kami's ​doings as ‌local manifestations ​of cosmic ​Buddha-activity, while ‌Edo-period nativists ​of the ​Kokugaku school ‌— Motoori ​Norinaga above ‌all — ​pored over ​the strange ​compounds of ​the divine ‌genealogy as ‌evidence of ​an authentically ‌Japanese antiquity. ​Each tradition ​found in ‌the unfinished ‌roof what ‌it needed: ritualists ‌a birth ​custom, monks ‌a parable ​of manifestation, ​nativists a ​pure native ‌word.[1]

Kindred ​figures in ​the PuniCodex ​cross-tradition index ‌include Hoori ​(father and ​ancestor of ​Jimmu), Toyotama ‌(mother and ​ancestor of ‌Jimmu), Anāhitā ​(earth / ‌mother / ​fertility), Auðhumla ‌(earth / ‌mother / ​fertility), Ọbalúayé ​(earth / ​mother / ​fertility), and ‌Bꜣstt (earth ‌/ mother ​/ fertility).

Sources

  1. Kojiki, age-of-gods chapters (Hoori, Toyotama, Ugayafukiaezu, and Jimmu cycles), trans. Donald L. Philippi (University of Tokyo Press, 1968).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

His ​legacy runs ‌through ‌the one ​institution ​he ​made possible. ​As ​Jimmu's father, ‌Ugayafukiaezu ​stands in ​the ‌imperial genealogy, ​and ‌Japan's National ​Foundation ‌Day ​(11 February) ​marks ​the legendary ​accession ​of the ‌son ​born in ‌the ​unfinished hut. ‌The ​birth-hut ‌custom behind ​his ‌name outlived ​the ​myth in ‌practice: ‌ethnographers recorded ‌ubuya ​seclusion into ​the ‌modern ​period, especially ​in ‌the Izu ​Islands ​and the ‌northeast. ​In scholarship ‌his ​name is ​a ​permanent ​puzzle — ‌every ‌handbook of ​Japanese ‌myth pauses ​over ​the cormorant ‌feathers ‌— and ‌in ‌popular ​culture the ‌Jimmu ​cycle, Yatagarasu ​and ​all, returns ‌in ​anime, manga, ​and ​school texts ‌as ​the ​nation's prologue. ​The ‌strange name ​does ‌the work: ​a ‌lineage that ​begins ‌under an ‌unfinished ​roof ​can never ​be ​accused of ‌having ‌begun too ​comfortably.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Ugayafukiaezu ​has no ‌independent ‌material ​cult; the ​evidence ​is textual ​and ​funerary. ‌The Kojiki ​survives ​through the ‌Shinpukuji ​manuscript ‌line (supplemented ​by ‌the 1371–72 ​date-bearing ​copies), ​and the ​Nihon ​Shoki through ‌Heian-period ​manuscript ‌traditions. The ​imperial ‌mausoleum his ​line ‌supposedly ​founded is ‌real ​as a ​site: ‌at ‌Kashihara in ‌Nara ​prefecture the ​Imperial ‌Household ​Agency tends ​Unebi-yama ‌no ushitora ​no ​sumi ‌no misasagi, ​the ‌kofun-style ​tomb attributed ​to ​Jimmu, the ​son ‌whose ‌birth the ​hut ‌story explains ​— ​a ‌large early-period ‌enclosure, ‌undisturbed by ‌archaeology ​and ‌unprovenanced to ​any ​historical person. ​Birth ‌huts ​(ubuya) themselves, ​the ​custom behind ‌the ​myth, ​persisted in ​parts ‌of Japan ​into ‌the ​twentieth century ‌and ​were documented ‌by ‌ethnographers ​— the ​closest ​the earth ​comes ‌to ‌the cormorant-thatched ​roof.[1]

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

He ​is named ‌for what ‌was never ​finished. Every ​other ​figure of ​the Kojiki's ​first books ‌wins a ​name from ​a ‌deed, a ​place, a ‌father; Ugayafukiaezu ​wins his ‌from ​defective carpentry ​— a ​roof of ​cormorant feathers, ​begun in ‌haste ​and never ‌completed. Scholars ​have tried ‌to make ​the ‌name less ​odd, reading ‌the feathers ​as ritual ​paraphernalia, the ‌incompleteness ‌as birth ‌taboo, the ​whole hut ​as a ‌lodge ​built for ​a single ‌night and ​deliberately left ​open to ‌the ​sky. All ‌may be ​true, and ​the text ​does ​not care. ‌What matters ‌is that ​the imperial ‌line — ​the ​oldest continuously ‌claimed succession ‌on earth ‌— passes ‌through ​a child ‌born in ​a shelter ​that was ​never made ‌weatherproof. ​The unfinished ​roof is ​the tradition's ‌quiet joke ​and ​its deepest ​claim at ‌once: origins ​are not ‌supposed to ​be ‌comfortable, complete, ​or safe. ‌Beginnings, like ‌thatch, are ​provisional; ​what matters ​is who ​carries the ‌story out ‌of the ​hut.[1]

Sources
