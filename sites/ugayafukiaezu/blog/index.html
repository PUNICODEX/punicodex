Overview

Ugayafukiaezu (ugayafukiaezu) — Ancestor of Jimmu — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "Cormorant grass-thatching unfinished".

Ugayafukiaezu is the son of Hoori and Toyotama and the father of Emperor Jimmu, the last divine generation before the first human emperor.

PuniCodex restores the name as Ugayafukiaezu. The plain ASCII form ugayafukiaezu is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 鵜草葺不合命. Etymologically it means "Cormorant grass-thatching unfinished".

Ugayafukiaezu means "cormorant grass-thatching unfinished," from u (cormorant), kaya (grass used for thatching), fuki (thatch), and aezu (unfinished/not meeting). The name describes the circumstances of his birth or upbringing: he was raised in a hut thatched with cormorant feathers or grass before it was completed.

The ASCII form ugayafukiaezu survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Ugayafukiaezu gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is Tier 2 because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 鵜草葺不合命 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Ugayafukiaezu (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //ɯ.ɡa.ja.ɸɯ.ki.a.e.zɯ//.

The characters 鵜草葺不合命 are a semantic-phonetic rendering: 鵜 (cormorant), 草 (grass), 葺 (thatch), 不 (not), 合 (meet/finish), 命 (honorific divine suffix). The name records a specific mythic episode rather than describing a general attribute.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /ɯ.ɡa.ja.ɸɯ.ki.a.e.zɯ/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'UGAYAFUKIAEZU' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Ugayafukiaezu is Tier 2 because the ASCII form ugayafukiaezu already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short syllable receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as separate morae. The name Ugayafukiaezu therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English speaker stresses one syllable heavily. The Hepburn romanization used here records the modern standard pronunciation; it does not preserve the historical pitch accent of the word, which differs across dialects. When reading the name aloud, keep the vowels pure and avoid English diphthongs: the restoration Ugayafukiaezu is the conventional scholarly citation form, not a phonetic transcription with added diacritics. Because the ASCII string ugayafukiaezu already encodes the same phonemic sequence, the name remains Tier 2 in the PuniCodex system.

Mythology

Ugayafukiaezu was raised by his aunt Tamayori after his mother Toyotama returned to the sea. He married Tamayori, and their four sons included Jimmu, who would lead the eastern campaign to establish the imperial line at Yamato. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki thus make Ugayafukiaezu the crucial link between the divine generations and the human imperial house.

The narrative of Ugayafukiaezu participates in the larger mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of ugayafukiaezu illustrates how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

Birth in the Cormorant Hut (Kojiki)

After Toyotama returned to the sea, her son Ugayafukiaezu was born in a thatched hut. The name describes the hut's covering of cormorant feathers and its unfinished state. Toyotama's sister Tamayori came from the sea to raise him.

Marriage to Tamayori (Kojiki)

Ugayafukiaezu grew up and married his aunt Tamayori, who had cared for him. Their union produced four sons, the youngest of whom was Kamuyamato-Iwarebiko, later known as Emperor Jimmu.

Ancestor of the Imperial Line (Imperial genealogy)

Through Ugayafukiaezu, the imperial line inherited both the maritime heritage of Toyotama and the terrestrial heritage of Hoori. His son Jimmu's eastern expedition founded the Yamato state.

Archaeology & Evidence

The textual record in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki is the primary source. Sites in Kyūshū and the Kinki plain associated with the Jimmu migration tradition preserve archaeological and ritual evidence of the imperial foundation myths.

The material record associated with Ugayafukiaezu must be read alongside the textual traditions; shrines, ritual objects, and landscape features preserve practices that the chronicles only summarize. Excavations and architectural history therefore offer independent evidence for how devotion to ugayafukiaezu developed across periods, even when written sources present a single mythic moment as eternal.

Realm & Domain

Ugayafukiaezu is the domain of imperial ancestry and the transition from mythic to human time. As the son of Hoori and Toyotama and the father of Jimmu, he stands at the last generation before the first historical emperor. His unusual name marks the liminal nature of this transition.

The domain of Ugayafukiaezu is understood within the broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, buddhas, and local spirits. Shrines, temples, and household rites all invoke the figure in ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Ugayafukiaezu therefore names not only a mythic person but a living field of ritual activity: pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. Cultural Legacy

Ugayafukiaezu is venerated in imperial genealogy and at shrines associated with the Jimmu tradition. His story marks the moment when the divine line assumes human form, making him essential to the ideology of the Japanese state.

Today Ugayafukiaezu continues to appear in shrine pilgrimage, festival observance, anime, manga, video games, and scholarly discourse. The name functions as both a religious referent and a cultural symbol, a flexibility that has allowed it to survive the formal separation of Shinto and Buddhism and the secularisation of modern Japan.
