01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Gwydion (gwydion) ​— Magic, ‌Poetry, Trickery · ‌Enchanter of ​the Fourth Branch ​— is ​the great magician ​of medieval Welsh ​literature: son ‌of Dôn, brother ​of Gilfaethwy, ​nephew of King ‌Math fab ​Mathonwy of Gwynedd, ‌and the ​animating intelligence of ‌'Math uab ​Mathonwy', the Fourth ​Branch of the ​Mabinogi.[1] Poet, ​trickster, shape-shifter, foster-father, ​and avenger, ‌he engineers the ​war that ‌costs him three ​years in ‌animal skins, and ​he ends ‌the tale as ​the careful craftsman ‌who sings ​his foster-son Lleu ​Llaw Gyffes ‌down out of ‌an oak ‌and nurses him ​back into ​human shape.[1]

The ‌name's etymology ​is honestly debated. ​A derivation from ‌the Celtic ​root widu- 'wood, ​forest' has ‌often been proposed ​— compare ‌Welsh gwydd 'trees', ​Old Irish ​fid — but ​the connection ​is not proven, ‌and the standard ‌scholarship treats ​it as uncertain.[2] ‌Because Welsh ​is written in ​the Latin ‌alphabet and the ‌Middle Welsh ‌manuscripts already spell ‌the name ​Gwydion, the ASCII ‌form loses nothing: ​the name ​is Tier 2 ​by the ‌mechanical rule, preserving ​no diacritic ​or distinctive letter ​that ASCII ‌drops.

PuniCodex records ​the name ​as Gwydion — ​the attested manuscript ‌spelling, identical ​in ASCII and ‌Unicode. The ​plain-ASCII domain gwydion.com ‌is registered ​to a third ‌party and ‌held outside the ​collection; it ​is watched for ​release, and this ​temple meanwhile ‌stands without a ‌domain of ​its own.[3]

  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia, 5 vols. (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Gwydion.
  3. Ifor Williams, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1951).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Middle Welsh ‌as Gwydion ​— Gwydion uab ​Dôn, 'Gwydion son ​of Dôn' ​— in the ​White Book ‌of Rhydderch and ​the Red ​Book of Hergest.[1] ‌Its etymology is ​debated. The ‌most-discussed proposal derives ​it from ‌the Celtic root ​widu- 'wood, ​forest' (Welsh gwydd ​'trees', Old Irish ​fid, Gaulish ​vidu-), which would ‌make the ​enchanter's name a ‌tree-name — ​fitting for the ‌magician of Kat ​Godeu, the ‌Battle of the ​Trees — ‌but the phonological ​and semantic ​steps are not ‌secure, and the ‌handbooks mark ‌the etymology uncertain.[2] ​The older ​popular gloss 'born ‌of trees' ​reflects this same ​gwydd association rather ‌than a ​settled derivation.

Because ​the manuscript ‌spelling is plain ​Latin letters, ‌the ASCII form ​gwydion is ​not a flattening ​but the attested ​form itself: ‌there is no ‌diacritic to ​restore and no ‌distinctive letter ​ASCII loses, which ​places the name ‌in Tier ‌2. The Unicode ‌form Gwydion ‌differs from the ​ASCII only ‌by capitalisation.

The ​letter-by-letter transformation runs:

​- g ​→ G — ‌Same, capitalized
​- w → ​w — ​Same

  • y ‌→ y — ​Same
  • ​d → d ​— Same

‌- i → ​i — ‌Same

  • o ​→ o — ‌Same
  • ​n → n ‌— Same

‌The plain-ASCII domain ​gwydion.com is ​registered to a ​third party and ​held outside ‌the collection; it ‌is watched ​for release.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The modern ​Welsh pronunciation ‌of the ‌name is /ˈɡwɪdjɔn/ ​— Modern ​Welsh standard.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Gwy- ​— Voiced velar ‌stop [ɡ] ​immediately rounded ​into [w] as ‌a single ​onset; the ‌vowel is Welsh ​y in ‌a stressed ​monosyllabic element, the ​close central ​vowel conventionally written ​[ɪ], like ​a tighter ‌version of the ​i in ‌English 'kin'
​- -dion — ‌Voiced dental ​stop [d], ‌palatal glide [j] ​written i ‌before the vowel, ​short open ​[ɔ], final ‌dental nasal [n]

‌For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ​is: 'GWID-yon' ‌— two ​syllables, stress on ​the first, ‌the gw sounded ​together as ​in 'Gwen', ‌never 'guh-WID-ee-on'.

Kindred ​and historical ‌forms of ​the name:

  • ​Middle Welsh ​— Gwydion ​(uab Dôn), the ‌manuscript form

‌- Modern Welsh ​— Gwydion, ‌unchanged in ​spelling and near-unchanged ​in sound
‌- Proposed ‌cognates — Welsh ‌gwydd 'trees, ‌wood', Old ​Irish fid 'wood', ‌Gaulish vidu-, ​behind the debated ​widu- etymology[2]
​- Attested ‌form — Gwydion

​Gwydion is ​Tier 2 ​because the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​ASCII loses: ​the name arrives ​in the ‌Latin alphabet, without ​diacritics, in ‌the manuscripts ​themselves. The name's ‌interest lies ​not in ‌rescued marks but ‌in the ​honesty of ​its uncertainty — ​a famous ​magician whose own ‌name has ‌never been ​fully explained.

Sources

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

Welsh ​is written ‌in the ‌Latin alphabet, ​and the ​name is ​preserved in ​Middle Welsh ​manuscripts as ‌Gwydion ​— attested ​14th–15th c. ‌CE in ​the White ‌Book of ​Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, ‌National Library ​of Wales, ​Peniarth ​4–5, c. ​1350) and ​the Red ‌Book of ​Hergest (Oxford, ‌Jesus College ​MS 111, ‌c. ​1400), the ‌two great ​codices of ‌the Mabinogion, ​recording material ​whose oral ‌and written ‌prehistory reaches ‌centuries ​earlier.[1]

The ​scholarly rendering ‌proceeds step ​by step:

​- The ‌manuscript spelling ​Gwydion is ​already the ‌normalized ​editorial form; ‌Ifor Williams's ​standard edition ​of the ​Four Branches ​regularises nothing ‌in this ‌name.[2]

  • ​No ‌runic, ogham, ​or other ​non-Latin attestation ‌exists; Welsh ‌names of ‌this stratum ‌were not ​carved in ‌ogham, ​and the ​ogham stones ​of Wales ‌belong to ​an earlier, ​Irish-settler epigraphic ​horizon.
  • ‌The ​ASCII form ​gwydion differs ​only in ‌capitalisation; nothing ​is lost ‌or restored ​between the ‌two.
  • ​The ‌plain-ASCII domain ‌gwydion.com is ​registered to ​a third ​party, held ​outside the ‌collection, and ‌watched for ​release.[3]

Sources

  1. Huws, Daniel, Medieval Welsh Manuscripts (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 2000), on the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest.
  2. Ifor Williams, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1951).
  3. Sioned Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), introduction and textual note.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Gwydion is ​the Fourth ‌Branch's master of ‌magic, craft, and ​vengeance: the ​enchanter whose illusions ​pass for ​wealth, whose disguises ​wring names ‌and weapons out ​of an unwilling ​fate, and ‌whose final acts ​of justice ‌are as carefully ​shaped as ‌his spells.[1]

​Magic

He conjures ​stallions, hounds, ​and shields solid ​enough to ​trade for otherworld ‌swine; he raises ​a phantom ‌fleet against Caer ​Arianrhod; with ‌Math he makes ​a woman ‌out of flowers. ​In the Book ‌of Taliesin's ​Kat Godeu he ​enchants the ‌forest itself to ‌march as ‌an army.[1][2]

​Craft

His power ​is artisanal ‌rather than thunderous: ​he wins ​by making — ‌disguises, verses, strategies ​— and ​the Fourth Branch ‌repeatedly calls ​his work by ‌the vocabulary ​of skill. He ​arrives at Pryderi's ​court as ​a poet, and ‌beneath Arianrhod's ‌walls as a ​shoemaker.

Vengeance

‌His justice ​is measured and ​transformed: three ‌years in animal ‌shapes as ‌his own sentence, ‌then the owl-shape ​for Blodeuwedd ‌— 'I will ​not kill ​you' — and ​the spear-cast ‌through the stone ​at Llech Gronw. ​Punishment, in ​his hands, is ‌a kind ​of making too.[1]

​### The House ​of Dôn

‌Son of Dôn, ​brother of ‌Gilfaethwy and Arianrhod, ​nephew of ‌Math, fosterer of ​Lleu: the Fourth ‌Branch keeps ‌him inside the ​kinship of ​the Children of ​Dôn, the ​Welsh mythic kindred ‌whose quarrels and ‌reconciliations the ​tale narrates.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Kat Godeu (Book of Taliesin), ed. and trans. in Haycock, Marged, Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin (Aberystwyth: CMCS, 2007).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Gwydion's attributes ​are narrative ‌rather than ‌cultic — ​the Welsh ​corpus preserves ​no temple regalia, ​but the ​Fourth Branch ‌fixed a ​recognisable set ​of instruments around ‌him:[1]

  • ​The magic ‌wand — ​Math's wand, ‌which strikes ​the brothers into ​deer, boars, ​and wolves, ​tests Arianrhod, ​and finally ‌strikes Lleu from ​eagle back ‌into man; ​Gwydion shares ‌in its ​use at ‌every turning of ​the tale

‌- The ​illusory horses ​and hounds ‌— Twelve stallions ‌with golden ‌harness, twelve ​greyhounds with ​golden collars ‌and leashes, ​twelve golden shields: ​conjured wealth ‌real for ​a day, ​the price ‌of the first ​swine in ‌Wales

  • ​Oak, broom, ​and meadowsweet ​— The ​three flowers from ‌which he ‌and Math ​fashioned Blodeuwedd

‌- The ​sow — The ​beast he ‌followed up ‌the valley ‌of the ‌Cynfael to ​the oak where ‌the wounded ​eagle rotted ​and fed ​her

  • ‌The three englynion ​— The ​verses he ​sang to ‌bring Lleu ​down the ​tree perch by ​perch: poetry ‌doing the ​work of ‌a rope

​- The shoemaker's ‌bench — ​The disguise ‌under which ‌he tricked ​Arianrhod into ​naming her son ​Lleu Llaw ​Gyffes, 'the ‌fair one ‌with the ​skilful hand'

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Gwydion's mythology is the ​Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, 'Math uab Mathonwy' ‌— the masterpiece of medieval Welsh prose, and ‌a single continuous narrative in which he ​moves from trickster to penitent to restorer.[1]

​The War for the Swine

King Math of ​Gwynedd could live only with his feet ​in a virgin's lap, save in wartime, and ​the maiden Goewin held that office. When Gwydion's ‌brother Gilfaethwy desired her, Gwydion engineered the ​remedy: he went to Pryderi of Dyfed in ​the guise of a poet and asked for ‌the swine — the hobeu, the first ​pigs brought to Wales, a gift from Arawn ‌of Annwfn. Refused, he conjured twelve stallions, twelve ​greyhounds, and twelve shields, all of them ‌seeming-gold, and traded the apparitions for the herd. ​The glamour faded, Pryderi pursued, and Gwynedd and ​Dyfed went to war. While Math was ​with the army, Gwydion and Gilfaethwy forced Goewin. ​At the settlement Pryderi demanded single combat, and ​at Maenor Penard Gwydion killed him — ‌the tale's own formula is 'by strength and ​valour and by magic and enchantment'.[1]

‌The Punishment of the Brothers

Math's answer was ​the most distinctive judgement in the Mabinogi. He ‌struck the brothers with his wand into ​a stag and a hind for one year, ‌a boar and a sow for the second, ​a wolf and a she-wolf for the ‌third; each year's offspring — Hyddwn, Hychddwn Hir, ​Bleiddwn — was sent to court, baptised, and ​raised. After the third year Math restored ‌them: they had had punishment enough, and had ‌been greatly shamed, and he took them back ‌into honour.[1]

The Naming and Arming ​of Lleu

Proposing his sister Arianrhod as the ​new footholder, Gwydion watched her step over Math's ‌wand for the virginity test and drop ​a yellow-haired boy — Dylan, who made at ​once for the sea — and 'something small' ‌that Gwydion swept up and hid in ​a chest at the foot of his bed. ​From the chest came the boy he fostered. ‌Arianrhod, shamed, laid three tynghedau on him: ​no name unless she named him, no arms ‌unless she armed him, no wife of any ​race then on earth. Gwydion undid the ​first two by craft: disguised as a shoemaker ​beneath Caer Arianrhod, he let the boy knock ​a wren's leg askew with a stone ‌until Arianrhod cried out that the fair one ‌had struck with a skilful hand — Lleu ​Llaw Gyffes, and the name was given; ‌then he raised a phantom fleet against the ​castle until she armed her own defenders.[1]

​The Flower-Maiden, the Eagle, and the Vengeance

‌For the third tynged, Gwydion and Math made ‌a woman from the flowers of oak, broom, ‌and meadowsweet and named her Blodeuwedd. She ‌betrayed Lleu with Gronw Pebr of Penllyn, drew ​from him the impossible conditions of his death, ‌and Gronw's spear struck him into the ​shape of an eagle. Gwydion searched until he ​followed a sow to an oak in the ​valley of the Cynfael, found the eagle ‌in its top, and sang three englynion to ​bring him down; a stroke of the ​wand returned him to human shape, skin and ​bone, to be nursed back by doctors. The ‌vengeance was as crafted as the rescue: ​Blodeuwedd he would not kill but turned into ​an owl — 'you will not dare show ​your face in daylight, because of the ‌enmity of all the birds' — and Gronw, ​standing in Lleu's place, took Lleu's spear-cast through ‌the great slab at Llech Gronw and ​died.[1]

Beyond the Fourth Branch

The Book ‌of Taliesin preserves Gwydion's other fame: in Kat ​Godeu, 'The Battle of the Trees', he ‌enchants the forest to march against Arawn of ‌Annwfn, alder at the front — and in ​the tale-tradition around the poem he guesses ​an enemy champion's hidden name from the alder ​twigs in his hand, naming-by-craft as in the ​wren scene.[2] Related figures include Taliesin, the ‌poem's boasting witness, and Manawydan, whose own branch ‌of the Mabinogi shares the Fourth's otherworld economy ​of magic and debt.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch (Math uab Mathonwy), trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
  2. Kat Godeu (Book of Taliesin), ed. and trans. Marged Haycock, Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin (CMCS, 2007).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Gwydion ​belongs to ‌the European ‌family of ​trickster-enchanters, and ​comparative ​readers have ​long set ​him beside ‌Loki ​— the ​disguise-bargainer and ‌shape-shifter — ​and Hermês, ‌the ​poet-thief who ‌wins by ​craft rather ​than force. ​Mac ​Cana treats ​him as ‌one of ​the ‌great magician-figures ​of the ‌Celtic world, ​but the ‌comparisons ​hold at ‌the level ​of motif, ​not ‌borrowing: illusion ‌as currency, ‌the hidden ​name, punishment ​by ‌metamorphosis.[1]

The ​deeper kinship ​is insular ‌Celtic. Irish ​tradition ​is full ‌of druidic ​shape-shifters and ‌three-fold ​transformations, and ​Gwydion's and ​Math's wand-work ​belongs to ‌that ‌shared idiom; ​scholars have ‌also connected ​the Fourth ​Branch's ‌otherworld material ‌— Arawn's ‌swine, the ‌phantom ​fleet — ‌to the ​mythology of ​Manawydan and ​the ‌First and ​Third Branches. ​The proposed ​widu- ‌etymology, if ​it could ​be proven, ​would align ‌the ​name itself ‌with the ​insular tree-lore ‌of Welsh ​gwydd ‌and Old ‌Irish fid ​— but ​it ​remains a ​proposal, and ‌the honest ‌verdict is ​uncertainty.[2]

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, The Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1992).
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Gwydion, Mabinogi.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Gwydion's afterlife ​runs from ‌Welsh sky-lore ‌into world ​literature. Later ​Welsh tradition ​named the ​Milky Way ​Caer Gwydion, ‌'Gwydion's fortress' ​— the ​enchanter's stronghold ‌written across ​the night ‌sky — ​and the bards ‌kept his ​tree-battle in ​the poetic ​canon through ​Kat Godeu.[1]

​The twentieth ‌century carried ​him into ‌English letters ​twice over. ‌Alan Garner's ​The Owl ‌Service (1967) ​rebuilt the Blodeuwedd ‌tragedy as ​a pattern ​that repeats ‌itself down ‌the generations ‌of a ​Welsh valley ​— flowers ‌into owl, ​owl into ​flowers — ‌and remains ​the most ​powerful modern ‌reworking of the ​Fourth Branch. ‌Lloyd Alexander's ​Chronicles of ​Prydain drew ​its Prince ​Gwydion fab ‌Don, heir ‌of Math, ​directly from ‌the Mabinogi, ​giving the ​enchanter a ‌second life ‌as a ‌hero of children's ‌literature; Evangeline ​Walton's retellings ‌of the ​Mabinogion made ​his punishment ​and restoration ‌the moral ​hinge of ​her Fourth ​Branch volume. ‌In scholarship ​he stands ​as the ​standard exhibit of ‌the Welsh ​magician: Ifor ‌Williams's edition, ​Mac Cana's ‌study, and ​Koch's encyclopedia ‌all treat ‌the Fourth ​Branch as ​the masterpiece ​of medieval ​Welsh prose ‌and Gwydion ‌as its ​animating intelligence.[2]

Sources

  1. Sioned Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007), notes on Caer Gwydion and the tale's reception.
  2. Garner, Alan, The Owl Service (London: Collins, 1967); Alexander, Lloyd, The Book of Three (New York: Holt, 1964); Walton, Evangeline, The Virgin and the Swine (Chicago: Willett, Clark, 1936).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Gwydion ​has no ‌pre-Christian material ‌attestation: no ​inscription, votive ​deposit, or ​image names ​him, and ​his evidence ‌is manuscript ​evidence. ​The Fourth ‌Branch survives ​in the ‌White Book ​of Rhydderch ‌(NLW Peniarth ​4–5, c. ​1350) and ​the Red ​Book ​of Hergest ‌(Jesus College ​Oxford MS ‌111, c. ​1400), the ‌two codices ​on which ‌all modern ​editions ‌rest; Kat ​Godeu is ​preserved in ‌the Book ‌of Taliesin ‌(NLW Peniarth ​2, early ​fourteenth century).[1]

‌What the ​landscape ​contributes is ‌geography, not ​cult. The ​tale is ‌set in ​walkable terrain ‌— Gwynedd ​and Arfon, ​the moor ​of ​Maenor Penard, ‌the river ‌Cynfael in ​Ardudwy, the ‌court of ​Penllyn — ​and a ‌holed stone ‌traditionally pointed ‌out ‌as Llech ​Ronw, 'Gronw's ‌stone', the ​slab Lleu's ​spear pierced, ​stands in ‌the Cynfael ​valley. The ​identification ​is local ‌tradition rather ​than proof, ​but it ​shows how ‌early and ​how firmly ‌the story ​was mapped ‌onto ​the Welsh ‌landscape: medieval ‌audiences, like ​modern walkers, ​could be ​taken to ​the places ‌where the ‌Fourth Branch ​happens.[2]

Sources

  1. Huws, Daniel, Medieval Welsh Manuscripts (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 2000).
  2. Sioned Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007), notes on the tale's topography.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Gwydion ​belongs to ‌Welsh rather ‌than Irish ​literature, but ​the ​Irish cycles ​supply his ​closest mythic ‌company. Irish ​tradition ​is rich ‌in druidic ​shape-shifters and ‌multiple transformations ​— the ‌magicians ​of the ​Ulster and ​Mythological cycles ​who change ​their ‌own shapes ​and others' ‌by spell ​and wand ‌— ​and Gwydion's ‌and Math's ​wand-work belongs ‌to that ​shared ​insular idiom ‌of magic ‌as metamorphosis.[1]

‌The structural ​parallels ​run deep. ‌The Otherworld ​economy of ​the Fourth ‌Branch ​— Arawn ​of Annwfn's ‌gift of ​swine, the ‌phantom wealth ​that ​fades — ​matches the ​Irish pattern ‌in which ‌Otherworld ​gifts carry ‌binding conditions, ​and the ​Welsh Annwfn ‌of ‌the Mabinogi ‌is the ‌counterpart of ​the Irish ‌síd. ​The proposed ​derivation of ​Gwydion's name ‌from widu- ​'wood' ​would, if ​proven, tie ‌him etymologically ​to Old ​Irish ​fid and ‌to the ​tree-lore that ‌runs through ​the Irish ‌ogham ​tradition; but ‌the etymology ‌remains debated, ​and the ​honest ​comparative verdict ​is kinship ‌of motif, ‌not demonstrable ​descent.[2]

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on insular magic and transformation.
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Gwydion, Annwfn.

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Gwydion's ​home witness ‌is the ‌Fourth Branch ​of the ​Mabinogi, 'Math ​uab Mathonwy', ​preserved in ​the White ‌Book of ​Rhydderch (c. ​1350) and ‌the Red ​Book of ‌Hergest (c. ​1400) and ‌established in ​its standard ​form by ​Ifor Williams's ​Pedeir Keinc ​y Mabinogi.[1] ‌The branch ​gives him ‌the full ​arc: the ‌swine-trick and ​the war ‌with Dyfed, ​the three ‌years of ​animal punishment, ​the fostering ‌of Lleu, ‌the undoing ‌of Arianrhod's ​three tynghedau, ​the making ‌of Blodeuwedd, ​and the ​long vengeance ‌that ends ​at Llech ​Gronw.

The ‌second ​Welsh witness ‌is poetic. ​The Book ​of Taliesin's ​Kat Godeu, ​'The Battle ‌of the ‌Trees', remembers ​Gwydion as ‌the enchanter ​who made ​the forest ‌march against ‌Arawn of ‌Annwfn, and ‌its speaker ​boasts of ‌having been ​present at ​the battle ​— a ‌tradition Taliesin's ​persona keeps ​alive.[2] Later ​Welsh tradition ‌adds the ​sky-name: Caer ​Gwydion, 'Gwydion's ​fortress', for ‌the Milky ​Way. Together ‌the two ​witnesses give ‌the figure ​his double ‌aspect — ‌the prose ​Mabinogi's careful ​craftsman of ​justice, and ​the poetry's ‌master of ‌the enchanted ​wild.[3]

Sources

  1. Ifor Williams, ed., Pedeir Keinc y Mabinogi, 2nd ed. (University of Wales Press, 1951).
  2. Haycock, Marged, Legendary Poems from the Book of Taliesin (CMCS, 2007), Kat Godeu.
  3. Sioned Davies, trans., The Mabinogion (Oxford University Press, 2007), notes.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​inscription names ‌Gwydion. ‌The ogham ​and ​Latin-inscribed stones ​of ​early medieval ​Wales ‌— some ​four ​hundred monuments, ‌concentrated ​in the ‌south-west ​— commemorate ‌secular ​kindreds in ​an ​Irish-settler epigraphic ​tradition, ​and none ‌carries ​this name ‌or ​any secure ‌reference ​to the ‌Children ​of Dôn.[1]

‌The ​silence is ​expected ‌rather than ‌damaging. ‌The Mabinogi's ​figures ​belong to ‌a ​mythological stratum ​transmitted ‌orally and ​then ​in codex, ‌not ​to the ‌commemorative ​epigraphic habit; ​the ​name Gwydion ​does ‌not occur ‌even ​as an ‌anthroponym ​in the ​early ‌Welsh stone ‌record. ‌What the ‌epigraphic ​corpus does ‌preserve ​is the ​linguistic ​frame: the ‌same ​Brittonic naming ​conventions ​— map/mab ‌patronymics, ​compound personal ​names ​— within ‌which ​Gwydion uab ‌Dôn ​was formed, ‌and ​the Gaulish ‌and ‌Old Irish ​cognates ​(vidu-, fid) ​on ​which the ‌debated ‌widu- etymology ​rests.[2]

Sources

  1. Nash-Williams, V. E., The Early Christian Monuments of Wales (Cardiff: University of Wales Press, 1950).
  2. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Gwydion, inscriptions.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Gwydion ​is what ‌a culture ‌makes of ​cleverness when ​it refuses ​to call ​cleverness innocent. ​His finest ‌trick — ​the phantom ​gold that ‌bought the ​swine ‌— is ​also his ‌crime, and ​the tale ​answers it ​with the ​strangest sentence ​in Celtic ‌literature: three ​years in ‌the bodies ​of ‌beasts, one ​year for ‌each shape ​of the ‌debt, until ​punishment and ​shame have ‌been paid ‌in full ‌and the ​king takes ​the brothers ‌back ​into honour.[1]

​But the ‌same hands ​that tricked ​a king ‌later coax ​a dying ‌man out ​of an ​oak, one ​verse at ​a ‌time. Gwydion's ‌magic is ​never thunder; ‌it is ​craft — ​the shoemaker's ‌bench, the ‌poet's englyn, ‌the wand ‌used at ​last to ‌heal rather ​than ​to change. ​The Fourth ‌Branch's calm ​doctrine is ​that to ​know the ‌true shape ​of a ​thing is ​to be ‌able to ​change ‌it, and ​to be ‌answerable for ​the change. ‌Gwydion is ‌the enchanter ​who learns, ​and teaches, ​that power ​over forms ‌is a ‌form of ​responsibility.

Sources

  1. The Mabinogion, Fourth Branch, trans. Sioned Davies (Oxford University Press, 2007).
