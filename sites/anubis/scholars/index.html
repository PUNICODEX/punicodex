Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

ꜣnpw ​(anubis) — ‌He Who ‌Is Upon ​His Mountain ​— ​belongs to ​the Egyptian ​tradition, where ‌it is ​catalogued ​under the ‌domain "Mummification, ​Afterlife, Jackals". ‌The name's ​etymology is ‌uncertain; ​his epithet ​tpy-ḏw=f means ​"He who ​is upon ​his ‌mountain"[1].

ꜣnpw ​is the ‌oldest funeral ​god of ‌Egypt: ​lord of ‌the necropolis ​before Osiris ‌ever ruled ​it, ​master of ‌embalming, and ‌the steady ‌hand at ​the ​scales where ‌every human ​heart is ​weighed against ‌a ​feather. His ​black jackal ‌watches from ​the edge ‌of the ​desert ​where the ​dead were ​always buried.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ‌the ​name as ‌ꜣnpw and ​serves its ​temple at ‌ꜣnpw.com. ‌The restoration ‌preserves a ‌distinctive feature ​the ASCII ‌form ​loses — ​the initial ​alef of ‌the consonantal ​skeleton ​— which ​places the ‌name in ​Tier 1. ​The ​plain ASCII ‌form anubis ​survives as ‌a modern ​convenience imposed ‌by ​the early ‌domain-name system; ‌the restoration, ​not the ​fallback, ​is the ​form the ‌project defends ‌as philologically ​complete[3].

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested in ‌Hieroglyphs as ​𓃢. The ​name's etymology ​is uncertain; ​his epithet ​tpy-ḏw=f means ‌"He who ​is upon ​his mountain"[1].

‌The ASCII ​form anubis ‌survives only ​because the ‌early domain-name ​system could ​not carry ​diacritics; it ​is a ​technological compromise, ‌not an ​ancient spelling. ‌The Unicode ​restoration ꜣnpw ‌recovers the ​full diacritic ‌detail of ​the scholarly ‌transliteration directly ​in the ​address bar. ‌The restoration ‌preserves a ‌distinctive feature ​the ASCII ​form loses ‌— the ​initial alef ​of the ‌consonantal skeleton ​— which ​places the ‌name in ​Tier 1.

‌The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

​- a ​→ ꜣ ​— Alef ‌ꜣ: glottal ‌stop

  • ​n → ‌n — ​Same
  • ​u → ‌p — ‌P: bilabial ‌stop
  • ‌b → ​— — ‌Dropped: not ​in standard

​- i ​→ w ‌— W: ​bilabial glide
​- s ​→ — ‌— Dropped: ​final suffix

​Attested and ​derived spellings ‌of the ​name:

  • ‌Anúbis — ​alternate stress, ‌scholarly variant: ​Acute on ‌upsilon: alternate ‌stress position

​The project ​holds the ​domain ꜣnpw.com ​(xn--npw-lk3l.com) as ‌the canonical ‌home of ​this name[2].

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The ​reconstructed ‌pronunciation ‌of the ​name ​is ​/jaːˈnaː.paw/ ​— ​Egyptological ‌(from ꜣnpw).[1]

​For ​the ‌modern ​speaker, ‌the ​closest ‌approximation is: ​The ​name ​is ​a ​consonant ‌skeleton: j-n-p-w; ​the ‌Greeks ​vocalized ‌it ​as ‌Anubis.

Kindred ​and ‌historical ​forms ​of ‌the ‌name:

‌- Egyptian ​— ​ꜣnpw
‌- ​Greek ​— ‌Ἄνουβις

  • ​Coptic ​— ‌Anoup

​- ‌Owned ​form — ​ꜣnpw

​Egyptian ​ꜣnpw ‌is ‌conventionally ​vocalized "Anupu" ‌or ​"Anup"; ​the ‌Greek ‌rendering ‌Anoubis ‌gives English ​"Anubis." ‌The ​initial ​ꜣ ​(aleph, ‌Gardiner G1) ​is ​a ​glottal ‌consonant ​Egyptology ​once ignored ​and ‌now ​transcribes ‌— ​the ‌restoration ​ꜣnpw keeps ‌the ‌ancient ​consonantal ​skeleton ​exactly ​as the ‌hieroglyphs ‌write ​it.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved ‌in Hieroglyphs ​as ​𓃢 ​— Egyptian ​hieroglyphs, ​attested Egyptian ‌hieroglyphic, ​c. 3200 ​BCE ‌– 4th ​century ‌CE, ​in Nile ‌Valley, ​Egypt. The ​script ​is written ​right-to-left ​/ top-to-bottom ‌/ ​multidirectional.[1]

‌The scholarly ​transliteration ‌is ꜣnpw ​(Egyptological ‌transliteration), giving ​the ‌normalized reading ​Original ​vocalisation ‌unknown; Egyptological ‌/aː.ˈnuː.bɪs/..

‌The rendering ​proceeds ​step by ‌step:

​- Recumbent ​jackal ‌(𓃢) ​as ideogram ​and ‌determinative

  • ​Full ‌phonetic spelling ​can ​include ​𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱
  • ​God ‌of mummification ‌and ​guide of ‌the ​dead

The ​name ‌is ‌written ꜣ-n-p-w ‌with ‌the recumbent ​jackal ‌(Gardiner E16) ​as ​determinative, over ​the ‌hotep ​offering sign ​in ​the fuller ‌orthographies. ​The Greeks ​heard ​it as ‌Anoubis; ​Coptic ‌preserved it ​as ‌Anoup. The ​jackal ‌is not ‌decorative: ​it is ​the ​god ​himself, present ‌in ‌his own ​spelling.

ꜣnpw ​is the ‌oldest ‌funeral god ​of ​Egypt: lord ​of ​the necropolis ​before ‌Osiris ever ​ruled ​it, master ‌of ​embalming, and ‌the ​steady hand ‌at ​the scales ​where ​every human ​heart ​is weighed ‌against ​a ‌feather. Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

In the ​earliest religion ꜣnpw simply ‌is the god ‌of the dead; when ​the Osiris cycle ​rose, he was ​written into it — ​as embalmer, son, ​and guide — without ‌ever losing his ​seniority at the grave.[1]

​### The First ‌Embalming (The Wrapping)

When ​Set dismembered Osiris ‌and Isis gathered the ​pieces, it was ‌ꜣnpw who washed, ​anointed, and wrapped the ​god's body — ​the first mummy, the ​prototype of every ​burial thereafter. Egyptian priests ‌performed the rite ​wearing his jackal mask, ‌and the liturgy ​addresses the dead as ‌"Osiris," while the ​hands doing the ‌work are Anubis' own.[2]

​### The Feather ‌of Maat (The Judgment)

​The Book of ​the Dead's spell 125 ‌fixes his most ‌famous office: in the ‌Hall of the ​Two Truths, ꜣnpw leads ​the deceased before ‌the scales, steadies ​the beam as the ​heart is weighed ‌against Maat's feather of ​truth, and announces ​the reading to Thoth, ‌who records it. ​A heart heavy with ‌lies is thrown ​to Ammit, the devourer ​— his is ​the hand that ​lets that happen, or ‌prevents it.

‌Son of Nephthys (The ​Lineage)

Later tradition ‌(Plutarch's On Isis and ​the Egyptian sources ​behind it) makes him ‌the son of ‌Osiris and Nephthys, exposed ‌and raised by ‌Isis — a ​hidden child of the ‌very murder he ​would spend eternity repairing. ​Earlier texts give ​him humbler parentage, sometimes ‌the cow-goddess Hesat: ​theologies changed, but his ​place at the ​grave never did.

‌Opener of the ​Ways (The Guide)

​His title Wp-w3wt — ​Wepwawet's title too, ‌"Opener of the Ways" ​— marks his ‌oldest function: the pathfinder ​through the Duat. ‌On coffins and tomb ​walls he leans ‌over the bier, one ‌hand on the ​wrapped body, not ​as a threat but ​as the ferryman: ​no soul reaches the ‌Field of Reeds ‌without his escort through ​the dark.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The ​Greeks, ‌finding ‌a ​god ​who ​guides ​souls, fused ​him ‌with ​Hermês: ​Hermanubis ‌had ​temples ‌of ​his ‌own ​in ​Alexandria ​and ​Rome, ​jackal-headed ‌with the ​kerykeion ‌in ​hand. ‌Isis' ​Roman ‌mysteries ​kept ‌him ​as ​mystagogue ‌— ‌Apuleius ‌describes ​his ​black-and-gold mask ‌in ​the ​Isiac ‌procession ​at ​Cenchreae. ‌Early ​Coptic ‌tradition ​quietly ​absorbed ​him ​into ‌the psychopomp ‌figure ​of ‌the ​archangel ​Michael, ‌weigher ‌of ‌souls.[1]

‌Kindred ​figures ‌in ​the ​PuniCodex ​cross-tradition ‌index include ​Hádēs ​(underworld ​/ ‌death), ​Hermês ​(psychopomp), ​Kānāloa ‌(underworld ​/ ‌death), ​Kēr ‌(underworld ​/ ‌death), ‌Mōt (underworld ​/ ​death), ​and ​Persephonē ‌(underworld ‌/ ​death).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

He ​is, ‌for ‌​most ​of ​the ​world, ​‌the ​face ​of ‌ancient ​‌Egypt: ​Tutankhamun's ‌black ​jackal ​​shrine ​— ​the ‌recumbent ​‌Anubis ​atop ‌his ​portable ‌​chest ‌— ​is ​among ‌the ‌‌most ​reproduced ​objects ‌on ​​earth. ‌Mummification ​cinema, ​from ‌​Karloff ‌to ​modern ​franchises, ​​works ‌his ‌territory; ​and ‌​every ​modern ‌scale-icon ‌of ‌justice ‌​unconsciously ‌borrows ​the ​pose ​‌of ​his ​beam. ​Egyptologists ‌​note ​the ​supreme ‌irony: ​‌feared ​as ‌a ​horror-movie ‌‌monster, ​he ​was ​antiquity's ​‌most ‌trusted ​guardian.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Tutankhamun's ​tomb (KV62) ‌yielded the ‌Anubis shrine: ​a life-size ​black wooden ​jackal, gilded ​ears and ​collar, guarding ‌the canopic ​equipment — ​now in ‌Cairo. The ​Anubeion at ‌Saqqara, excavated ​over decades, ‌preserves catacombs ​of mummified ​dogs and ​jackals offered ​to him ​— estimates ‌run to ​millions of ‌animals across ​Egypt's canine ‌catacombs. Vignettes ​of spell ‌125, with ​his ‌hand on ​the scales, ​survive in ‌hundreds of ‌Books of ‌the Dead, ​from the ​papyrus of ‌Ani in ​the British ​Museum to ‌the papyrus ​of Hunefer.[1]

​His great ‌cult centre ​was Cynopolis ‌— 'Dog ​City' — ​in Middle ​Egypt, where ​the Greeks ‌noted the ‌dogs kept ​sacred in ‌his temple. ​The Anubeion ​at Saqqara, ‌excavated beside ‌the Serapeum, ‌preserves ‌his other ​world: catacombs ‌filled with ​millions of ​mummified dogs ​and jackals, ‌votive offerings ​of pilgrims ​from the ​Late Period ‌to the ​Roman era. ​In Greco-Roman ​Alexandria the ‌temple of ​Hermanubis fused ‌him with ​Hermês, and ‌Apuleius describes ​his black-and-gold ‌mask leading ‌the Isiac ​procession — ​the Egyptian ​jackal marching, ​fully at ‌home, through ‌a Greek ​city.

Sources

  1. A Concise Dictionary of Middle Egyptian, Griffith Institute, 1962. ↗
  2. The Saqqara Anubeion reports; Apuleius, Metamorphoses 11.11.

Pyramid Texts

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

In ​the ‌Pyramid ‌Texts ​— ​Egypt's ​oldest ​religious ​corpus, ‌ca. ​2350 ​BCE ‌— ​ꜣnpw ‌already ​holds ‌his ​seniority ​at ​the ​grave: ​the ‌utterances ​call ‌on ​him ‌as ​the ‌one ​"foremost ‌of ​the ​westerners" ‌and ‌invoke ‌his ​jackal ​form ‌in ​the ​king's ‌ascent ​and ​provisioning. ‌He ​appears ‌where ​the ​ritual ​needs ​a ‌master ‌of ​the ‌necropolis ​and ​a ‌guide ‌on ‌the ‌western ​roads, ‌functions ​he ​holds ​centuries ‌before ​Osiris' ​dossier ​absorbs ‌them. ​The ​texts ​establish ‌what ​never ‌changes ​afterward: ‌the ​dead ‌king's ‌first ​companion ​beyond ​the ​tomb ‌is ‌the ​jackal.

Sources

  1. Faulkner, The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts (ꜣnpw utterances).

Coffin Texts

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Coffin ‌Texts ‌democratize ​his ​offices ​for ​non-royal ​dead: ‌spells ​call ​on ‌ꜣnpw ​to ‌guide ​the ‌deceased ​through ​the ​Duat's ​gates, ​to ‌​"open ‌the ​way," ‌and ​to ‌preside ​at ‌the ​embalming ​that ‌every ‌justified ‌dead ​now ​receives. ‌The ​Book ​of ‌Two ​Ways ​— ‌the ​Middle ‌Kingdom's ​underworld ​map ​painted ​inside ‌coffins ‌— ​marks ‌the ​roads ​he ‌opens. ‌His ‌titles ‌in ​the ‌corpus ​(Imy-ut, ​Khenty-imentiu) ​are ‌​already ​fixed ​liturgy, ‌and ​the ​embalming ​spells ‌put ​the ‌priest ​in ‌his ​mask: ‌the ‌ritual ​is ​performed ​by ​the ‌god ‌in ​effigy.

  1. Faulkner, The Ancient Egyptian Coffin Texts; Book of Two Ways.

Book of the Dead

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Book ‌of ‌the ​Dead ​gives ​him ​his ​canonical ‌scene: ​spell ​125, the ‌weighing ​of ‌the ​heart ‌in ​the ​Hall ​of ​the ​Two ‌Truths. ​The ‌vignettes ​— ‌Ani, ​Hunefer, ‌and ​hundreds ‌of ​others ​— ‌show ꜣnpw ‌leading ‌the ​deceased ​in, ‌crouching ​at ​the ‌scales ​to ​read ‌the ​beam ‌against ​Maat's ​feather, ​and ​announcing ‌the ‌result ​to ‌Thoth. ​Spells for ​"not ‌dying ‌again" ‌and ‌for ​the ‌heart's ​loyalty ​invoke ​him ‌as ​guardian ​of ​the ‌verdict's ​fairness. ​It ​is ‌the ​most ‌reproduced ​judgment scene ‌in ​human ‌religious ‌history, ​and ​his ​steady ​hand ‌is ‌its ​centre.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

ꜣnpw ​‌is ‌the ​​god ​of ​​careful ‌hands. ​​At ‌​the ‌two ​‌moments ​a ​​person ​is ​‌most ​‌helpless ​— ‌​the ‌body's ​‌unmaking ​and ​‌the ‌‌heart's ​examination ​‌— ​he ​‌is ​the ​‌one ​‌who ​touches ​​gently ​and ‌‌reads ​honestly. ‌​Civilizations ​‌are ‌judged, ‌‌the ​Egyptians ‌​believed, ​by ​‌how ​​they ​treat ‌​the ​dead; ​‌he ​is ‌​the ‌​god ‌of ‌​that ​judgment, ​​in ‌both ‌​senses.[1]
