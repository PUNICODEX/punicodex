Mythology

Candra’s mythology clusters around two stories: the theft of Tārā and its curse, and the moon-churning of the ocean ‌of milk. He is the most beautiful of the gods and the most diminished — the waxing and waning written into his body as punishment and mercy at once.

​The Abduction of Tārā (Purāṇic Cycle)

Candra loved Tārā, wife of the guru Bṛhaspati, and carried her off; the gods’ war over her ended with her return — ‌and her pregnancy, which she refused to explain until the child’s radiance declared the moon his father. Budha, the child, founded the lunar dynasty through which the epic’s ​heroes descend.

The Curse and the Mercy (Śiva Cycle)

Dakṣa, Bṛhaspati’s champion, cursed the moon to waste to nothing; the pleading gods won a partial reprieve — the ‌curse would lift for half each month. The phases of the moon are the scar of the love-affair, and Śiva’s wearing of the crescent is the war-god’s mercy ​made ornament.

The Moon from the Churning (Samudra Manthana)

When the gods and demons churned the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality, the moon rose among ‌the treasures — Dhanvantari’s medicine-chest, the apsarases, and Candra, the glittering disc. Śiva took him onto his brow; the poison of the churning, Halāhala, he drank instead, turning ​his throat blue.

The Celestial Plant (Vedic Soma)

In the Veda the moon is Soma’s home: the pressed plant’s juice ascends to become the lunar disc, and the moon’s ‌quarters are the soma-cup’s refills. The identification of moon, plant, and god is the Veda’s most compact theology — three substances, one divinity.

Across Cultures

Candra fused with Soma in the Vedic period and with the astronomical moon (one of the nine grahas) in the medieval period. The Greeks of ​the Northwest compared him to Selene’s domain but found no single Olympian match; and the Buddhist lunar mansions (nakṣatras) kept his calendar alive across Asia.

Cultural Legacy
