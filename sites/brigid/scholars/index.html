01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Brigid (brigid) ​— Poetry, Healing, ‌Smithcraft, Fire · ‌The Exalted One ​— is the ​triple goddess of ​medieval Irish tradition: ​Brigit the poetess, ​daughter of the ‌Dagda, whom Cormac's Glossary ​pairs with two ​sisters of the ‌same name — ​Brigit the woman ‌of smithcraft and ​Brigit the female ‌physician — so ​that the forge, ​the well, and ​the word all ​answer to a ​single divine name.[1] ‌The Lebor Gabála ​Érenn places her ‌in the house ​of the Dagda ‌among the Túatha Dé ​Danann,[2] and Cath ‌Maige Tuired gives ​her the corpus's ‌most terrible dignity: ​when her son ​Ruadán dies on ‌the field of ‌the Second Battle ‌of Mag Tuired, ​her lament is ​the first keening ‌heard in Ireland.[3]

​Her feast is ​Imbolc, the first ‌of February — ​the season Cormac ​glosses as oimelc, ‌'ewe's milk', when the ​flocks come into ‌milk at the ​turning of the ​year[1] — and ​at Kildare a ​fire burned for ‌her that Giraldus ‌Cambrensis describes around ​1188: hedged so ‌no man might ​cross, tended by ​nineteen nuns in ‌nightly turn, and ‌on the twentieth ‌night committed to ‌Brigid herself, its ​ashes never increasing ‌though the wood is ​always consumed.[4]

PuniCodex ​keeps the temple ​for this name ‌without a domain: ​the plain-ASCII brigid.com ​is registered to ​a third party ‌outside the collection ​and is watched ​for release. The ​normalized Old Irish ‌form Brigit is ​already plain ASCII ‌— no diacritic ​or distinctive letter ‌survives for the ​restoration to recover — ‌which places the ‌name in Tier ​2 by the ​project's mechanical rule. ​The temple's claim ​is scholarly, not ‌territorial: the name ‌and its sources, ​in full.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Irish ‌as Brigit (genitive ​Brigte). It ​descends from Proto-Celtic ​Brigantī, 'the Exalted ​One', a ​feminine derivative of ‌the root brig- ​'high, lofty' ​— the same ‌element that produced ​the tribal ‌name Brigantes of ​northern Britain, the ‌Romano-British goddess ​Brigantia, the Gaulish ​divine name Brigindu, ​and place-names ​such as Brigantium ​(modern Bregenz and ‌La Coruña).[1] ​The standard comparison ‌with Sanskrit bṛhatī, ​feminine of ‌bṛhant-* 'high, exalted', ​shows the etymology's ‌Indo-European depth; ​among scholars it ‌is one of ​the least ​contested in Celtic ‌onomastics.[2]

The modern ‌English spelling ‌Brigid and the ​normalized Old Irish ​Brigit are ‌already plain ASCII: ​no diacritic, and ​no distinctive ‌letter that the ​ASCII alphabet cannot ​write. There ‌is therefore nothing ​for a ‌Unicode restoration to ​recover — only ​the capital ​distinguishes the temple's ​form from the ‌input — ‌and the name ​is classified Tier ‌2 by ​the project's mechanical ​rule. This is ‌a fact ‌about orthography, not ‌standing: the name's ‌fame vastly ​outruns its spelling.

‌The letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • ​b → B ​— Same, capitalized

‌- r ​→ r — ​Same

  • i ​→ i ‌— Same
  • ​g → g ​— Same

​- i → ‌i — Same
​- d ‌→ d — ​Same

The project ‌holds no ​domain for this ‌name: brigid.com was ‌registered by ​a third party ​decades ago in ​the ordinary ​way and is ‌watched for release. ‌This temple ​claims none.

Sources

  1. Vendryes, Joseph, Lexique étymologique de l'irlandais ancien (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1959–), s.v. Brigit.
  2. Jackson, Kenneth H., Language and History in Early Britain (Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 1953), on *brig- and the Brigantes; Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Brigit, Brigantia.
03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the Old Irish ‌name is /ˈbrʲiɣʲitʲ/ ​— Old ​Irish Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

  • ​Br- — ​Voiced bilabial stop ‌[b] and ​slender (palatalized) trill ​[rʲ]; Old Irish ‌consonants came ​in broad and ‌slender pairs, and ​the r ‌of Brigit was ​slender, as ​it remains in ​modern Irish
  • ​-i- — ​Short front [i]; ‌in the later ​language the ‌vowel lengthened, giving ​modern Irish ‌Bríd
  • -g- ​— Voiced velar ‌fricative [ɣ] ​between vowels: not ‌the hard stop ​of English ​'big' but a ‌soft continuant, ‌the regular lenited ‌value of Old ​Irish medial ​g
  • -id ‌— Short front ​[i] and ​slender final [dʲ]; ‌the modern ​form keeps exactly ​this ending, /bʲrʲiːdʲ/

‌For the ​modern speaker, the ‌closest approximation is: ​'BRIJ-id' — ​like 'bridge' split ​into two ​syllables, the g ‌softened toward a ‌gargled continuant. ​The modern Irish ‌form Bríd is ​simply pronounced ​'breed'.

Kindred and ‌historical forms ‌of the name:

‌- Old Irish ‌— Brigit ​(genitive Brigte), daughter ‌of the Dagda
​- Proto-Celtic ​— *Brigantī, 'the ​Exalted One', ‌source also of ​Brigantia and the ​Brigantes[2]

  • ​Modern Irish — ‌Bríd, the living ​form of ​goddess and saint ​alike
  • ‌Scottish Gaelic / ​Manx — Brìde ‌/ Breeshey

​- Watched form ‌— brigid.com, held ​outside the ‌collection; no form ‌owned

Brigid ​is Tier 2 ​because the restoration ​has nothing ​to restore: the ‌Old Irish spelling ‌survives into ​ASCII whole.

Sources

  1. Thurneysen, Rudolf, A Grammar of Old Irish, trans. D. A. Binchy and Osborn Bergin (Dublin: Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, 1946), phonology of broad and slender consonants and lenition.
  2. Vendryes, Lexique étymologique de l'irlandais ancien, s.v. Brigit.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in the medieval ‌Irish manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet, written ​in Insular ​script — the ​Irish bookhand ‌— as ​Brigit, attested in ​Old Irish ‌from the early ​medieval period ‌in the ​glossaries, the Mythological ‌Cycle, and ​the saints' Lives.[1] ​The script ​is written ​left-to-right; the older ​Ogham alphabet ‌would have notched ​the name ‌ᚁᚏᚔᚌᚔᚈ (B-R-I-G-I-T), but ​that rendering ‌is a ​normalized reconstruction — ‌no Ogham ​stone records the ‌theonym.[2]

The ​scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • ‌Old Irish was ‌written in ​the Latin ​alphabet in Insular ‌minuscule; spelling ​varies across manuscripts ​between Brigit, ‌Briget, and ​related forms.
  • ​The normalized ‌scholarly form is ​Brigit, genitive ‌Brigte, from Proto-Celtic ​*Brigantī 'the ​Exalted One'.[3]

​- No diacritic ​is required: ‌the Old Irish ‌spelling carries ​no long ‌vowel and no ​distinctive letter, ​so the ASCII ‌form Brigid ‌is orthographically ‌complete.

  • The ‌temple therefore ​claims no restoration ‌and no ​domain: brigid.com ​is held by ​a third ‌party outside the ​collection and ​watched for release.

​The single ‌caution that ​attaches is etymological ​honesty: the ​derivation from *brig- ‌'high' is ​the standard ‌and near-uncontested account, ​but the ‌leap from name-element ​to the ‌goddess's original ‌character — exalted ​by height, ​by rank, or ​by fire ​— is ‌inference, and the ‌texts do ​not say.[3]

Sources

  1. Thurneysen, A Grammar of Old Irish (DIAS, 1946), on Old Irish orthography.
  2. McManus, A Guide to Ogam (An Sagart, 1991).
  3. Vendryes, Lexique étymologique de l'irlandais ancien, s.v. Brigit.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Brigid ​is catalogued ‌under the ‌domain "Poetry, ​Healing, ​Smithcraft, Fire" ​— the ​triad of ​Cormac's ‌Glossary plus ​the undying ​flame ‌of her ​Kildare hearth.[1]

‌### Poetry

​Brigit ‌banfhiled, Brigit ​the woman-poet, ​is the ​Glossary's ​first sister: ​patroness of ‌filidecht, the ​learned ‌poetic craft, ​and of ‌the word ​made ‌durable — ​fittingly, her ‌own one ​great ​speech-act in ‌the texts ‌is ‌the invention ​of lamentation.[2]

​### Smithcraft

‌Brigit ​goband, the ​woman of ‌smithcraft, holds ​the ​forge-province of ‌the triad ​— the ‌divine ​smith's art ​that her ​own myth-world ​practices ‌in Goibniu ‌of the ​Túatha Dé ‌Danann.[1]

​### Healing

​Brigit légach, ‌the ‌female physician, ‌completes the ‌triad; her ​province ‌survives in ​the cure ​customs of ​the ‌hundreds of ​Irish holy ​wells that ​bear ‌the saint's ​name.[1]

​Fire

The ​perpetual ‌fire of ​Kildare, hedged ‌from men ​and ‌tended by ​nineteen keepers, ‌is ‌the visible ​form of ​all three: ​a ​craft-flame that ‌neither fails ‌nor accumulates ​ash.[3]

  2. Cath Maige Tuired, §§125–131, ed. and trans. Gray (1982).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Brigid's ​iconography is ‌drawn from ‌the texts ​and ​from living ​custom rather ​than ​from ancient ‌images — ​no pre-Christian ​representation ‌of the ​goddess survives:[1]

‌- The ​sacred ‌flame of ​Kildare — ​the ​perpetual fire ​that neither ​dies nor ‌accumulates ​ash: patronage ‌that outlasts ​the night-watch ‌of ​its keepers
‌- The ​anvil ‌and the ​forge — ​the smith-sister's ‌province: ‌skill that ‌gives raw ​metal a ​lasting ‌form, as ​poetry gives ​raw ‌speech one
​- The ​holy well ‌— ​the leech-sister's ‌province: healing ​drawn from ​the ​ground, still ​visited at ‌the ‌hundreds of ​wells of ‌St Brigit
​- ​Fea and ‌Femen, the ‌two oxen ‌— ‌the Dagda's ​daughter's beasts ‌in ​Cath Maige ​Tuired, with ​the plains ‌Mag ​Fea and ​Mag Femen ​named for ‌them: ​wealth and ​the working ​land[2]
‌- The ​rush cross ‌— St ​Brigid's ‌cross, woven ​on Imbolc ‌eve: the ‌small ​loom on ​which the ​goddess's ​feast was ‌carried into ‌the saint's ​day

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Brigid's mythology is ​a small dossier of ‌exact testimonies rather than ‌a cycle of adventures: the ​genealogists know her family, ​the battle-tale knows her ​grief, and the glossary knows ​her triad.

The ​Dagda's Daughter (Lebor Gabála ‌Érenn)

The Book of Invasions ​files her in the ​house of the Dagda: ‌Brigit the poetess, daughter ​of the good god of ‌the cauldron, among the ​Túatha Dé Danann — ‌the divine race whose eponymous ​ancestress is Danu. The ​same tradition marries her ​to Bres, the half-Fomorian king ​whose beautiful face and ​niggardly rule provoked the ‌great war, and gives her ​a son, Ruadán: the ‌Exalted One stands by ​marriage and blood on both ‌sides of the conflict.[1]

​### The First Keening ‌in Ireland (Cath Maige Tuired)

​In the Second Battle ‌of Mag Tuired — ​the tale in which Lug ​leads the Túatha Dé ‌Danann against the Fomorians ‌— Ruadán is sent ‌to spy on the divine ​smith Goibniu and hurls ​a spear at him; ‌the smith casts it back, ​and Ruadán goes to ​his father to die. ‌Brigit comes to the field ​and bewails her son, ​and the text is ‌explicit: hers was the first ​keening and the first ‌whistling for grief heard ​in Ireland. The goddess of ​the word is the ​one who taught Ireland ​what a mother says at ‌a grave.[2]

Three ‌Sisters, One Name (Sanas ​Cormaic)

Cormac's Glossary (c. 900) ‌preserves the triad on ​which her whole modern ​image rests: Brigit the ‌poetess, daughter of the Dagda, ‌had two sisters, Brigits ‌also — Brigit the ‌woman of smithcraft and Brigit ​the female physician — ‌from whose names all ​the Irish used to call ​a goddess Brigit. Whether ​three figures or one ‌goddess's three provinces, the entry ​is the witness every ​later account follows.[3]

​The Fire at Kildare (Giraldus ‌Cambrensis)

Gerald of Wales, ​c. 1188, describes the ​Kildare fire as a wonder ​of his own day: ‌hedged against men, tended ​by nineteen nuns in nightly ‌turn, and on the ​twentieth night committed to ‌Brigid with the words ​'guard your fire, it is ‌yours' — burning always, ‌its ashes never increasing. ​He records it as a ​marvel of the saint's ​church; the hedge, the ​nineteen keepers, and the unashing ‌flame have never been ‌doubted to belong to ​an older hearth.[4]

Sources

  1. Lebor Gabála Érenn, ed. and trans. Macalister (1938–56), on Brigit daughter of the Dagda.
  2. Cath Maige Tuired, §§125–131, ed. and trans. Gray (1982).
  3. Sanas Cormaic, s.v. Brigit, ed. Meyer (1912).
  4. Giraldus Cambrensis, Topographia Hiberniae II.34–36, ed. O'Meara (1949).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Brigid's syncretic ​life runs ‌in two ‌directions. Across the ​water, her ​name is ​one with Brigantia, ​the goddess ​of Roman Britain ‌honoured in ​inscriptions from ​the Wall country ‌and portrayed ​on the ‌Birrens relief with ​Minerva's globe ‌and spear ​— the interpretatio ​romana pairing ​the exalted one ​with the ​Roman goddess ‌of craft and ​war-wisdom. The ‌tribal Brigantes ​and the Gaulish ‌Brigindu carry ​the same ‌name-element, and the ​comparison with ‌Sanskrit bṛhatī shows ​how deep ​the root ‌runs.[1]

Within Ireland, ‌the identification ‌is Christian: ​Brigit of Kildare, ​the sixth-century ‌saint whose ​feast falls on ​Imbolc and ‌whose church kept ​the perpetual ​fire. Cogitosus's ‌Life of Brigit ​(c. 650), ‌our earliest ​substantial Life of ​any Irish ​saint, presents ​wonder-tales in which ‌the saint's ‌patronage of poets, ​smiths, and ‌the poor ​uncannily covers the ​goddess's provinces.[2] ‌Whether saint ‌succeeded goddess, goddess ‌was Christianized ‌into saint, ​or two figures ‌fused in ​one name, the ​sources do ​not decide ‌— and Mac ​Cana and ​Koch both ​counsel holding the ‌question open. ​What is ​certain is the ​continuity: the ‌Exalted One never ​left Ireland's ‌calendar.

Within ​the pantheon, her ‌closest kin ​are the ‌Dagda (her father), ‌Lug (the ​many-skilled master ​of all arts ​at Mag ​Tuired), and Danu ‌(ancestress of ‌the Túatha ​Dé Danann).

Sources

  1. Koch, John T., ed., Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (Santa Barbara: ABC-CLIO, 2006), s.v. Brigantia, Brigantes, Brigit.
  2. Cogitosus, Vita Sanctae Brigitae, ed. in Acta Sanctorum, Febr. I (Antwerp, 1658); trans. S. Connolly and J.-M. Picard, 'Cogitosus's Life of St Brigit', Journal of the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland 117 (1987).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

No ​Celtic deity ‌has ‌a longer ​unbroken afterlife. ​Imbolc ​is still ​celebrated ​as St ‌Brigid's Day ​— ​Lá Fhéile ‌Bríde ​— and ‌since 2023 ​it ‌has been ​a ​public holiday ​in the ​Republic ​of Ireland, ‌the ​first named ‌for ​a woman.[1] ‌Rushes are ​still ‌woven into ​Brigid's ‌crosses on ​the eve ​of ‌the feast ‌and ‌hung above ​doors; the ​holy ‌wells of ​St ​Brigit, with ‌their rag-trees ​and ​cure customs, ‌keep ​the leech-sister's ‌province ​in living ​folk practice.

​Modern ​paganism and ‌Celtic ‌Reconstructionism restored ​the goddess ‌explicitly, ​keeping Imbolc ​as ‌the feast ‌of the ‌returning ‌light and ​of ‌the three ​crafts, and ​claiming ​her patronage ‌for ​poets, smiths, ​and ​healers alike.[2] ‌And the ​name ​itself — ​Brigid, ‌Bridget, Bríd ​— never ‌ceased ​to be ‌given ​to Irish ‌daughters, carrying ‌the ​Proto-Celtic word ​for ​exaltation into ​every generation ‌since ‌the Iron ​Age.

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Proinsias, Celtic Mythology (London: Hamlyn, 1970), on Brigit and Imbolc; Ó Catháin, Séamas, The Festival of Brigit (Dublin: DBA Publications, 1995), on the feast's folk customs.
  2. Koch, Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (2006), s.v. Brigit, Imbolc.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Brigit ​herself is ‌unattested ‌in pre-Christian ​Irish ​material: no ​Ogham ​stone, votive ​deposit, ‌or image ​names ​the goddess, ‌and ​Ireland's Iron ‌Age ​left no ‌cult ​site securely ​hers.[1]

​Her name's ​material ​record lies ‌across the ​water. ‌Romano-British inscriptions ​honour ‌Brigantia from ​the ‌Wall country ​— ‌most famously ​the ​relief from ‌Birrens ‌(Dumfriesshire), where ‌the ​goddess stands ​with ‌Minerva's globe, ​spear, ​and mural ‌crown ​— and ​a ‌Gaulish inscription ​from ‌Volnay (Côte-d'Or) ​invokes ​Brigindu, the ​same ​name-element on ‌the ‌Continent.[2]

In ​Ireland ‌the evidence ​is ​ecclesiastical and ‌customary: ‌the monastic ‌site ‌of Kildare ​(Cill ‌Dara, 'church ​of ​the oak'), ​with ‌its round ​tower and ​its ​memory of ‌the ​perpetual fire ​Giraldus ​describes, and ‌the ​hundreds of ‌holy ​wells dedicated ‌to ​St Brigit, ‌whose ‌rag-trees and ​cure ​customs are ​the ​living deposit ‌of ‌the leech-sister's ​province.[3]

Sources

  1. Mac Cana, Celtic Mythology (1970), s.v. Brigit.
  2. Koch, Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (2006), s.v. Brigantia (the Birrens relief and the epigraphic record).
  3. Ó Catháin, Séamas, The Festival of Brigit (Dublin: DBA Publications, 1995), on wells and folk practice.

Irish Mythological Cycles

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Brigit ​belongs to ‌the Mythological ‌Cycle, ​the cycle ​of ​the Túatha ​Dé Danann. ​Her ‌genealogy is ​filed by ​the ‌Lebor Gabála ​Érenn, ‌the great ​pseudohistory that ‌orders ​the gods ​into an ​ancestry ​of invasions: ​Brigit ‌the poetess, ​daughter of ‌the ​Dagda, wife ‌of Bres, ​mother ‌of Ruadán.[1] ​Her ‌one extended ​scene belongs ​to ‌the cycle's ‌central battle-tale, ‌Cath ​Maige Tuired, ​where ‌the Morrígan ​prophesies and ​ravens ‌over the ​same field ​on ‌which Brigit ​invents ‌keening — ​the two ​great ​female voices ​of the ‌war, ‌one of ​doom ‌and one ​of grief.[2]

​She ‌scarcely figures ‌in the ‌later ‌Ulster and ​Fenian ‌cycles; her ​afterlife runs ​instead ​through the ‌third great ​stratum ​of Irish ​text, ‌the saints' ​Lives, where ​Cogitosus's ​Brigit of ‌Kildare inherits ​her ‌name, her ​feast, ‌and — ​in the ‌wonder-tales ‌— her ​provinces. The ​cycle ​that continued ​her ‌story was ‌the hagiographical ​one.[3]

Sources

  1. Lebor Gabála Érenn, ed. and trans. Macalister (1938–56).
  2. Cath Maige Tuired, ed. and trans. Gray (1982).
  3. Cogitosus, Vita Sanctae Brigitae, trans. Connolly & Picard (1987).

Welsh Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Welsh ​literature has ‌no ‌narrative of ​Brigid: ​the Mabinogion ​knows ​nothing of ​her, ‌and no ​Welsh ​goddess-tale attaches ‌to ​her name.[1] ‌The ​Welsh record ‌is ​onomastic and ​hagiographic ​rather than ​mythological. ​The name-element ‌*brig- ​is ‌genuine Brittonic ​inheritance ‌— the ​Brigantes ‌were a ​Brittonic-speaking ‌people — ​and ​the River ‌Braint ‌in Anglesey ‌is ​standardly derived ​from ‌Brigantia, a ​survival ​of the ‌goddess's ​name in ​Welsh ‌water.[2]

The ​saint ‌travelled further ​than ​the goddess: ​Brigit ​of Kildare ‌was ‌naturalized in ​Welsh ‌as Ffraid, ​and ​Welsh churches ‌and ‌holy wells ‌of ‌St Ffraid ​— ‌above all ​at ​Treffraid (Llansantffraid) ​sites ‌across ​Wales — ​carry ​the Kildare ‌tradition ​into the ​Welsh ​landscape. The ‌Welsh ​evidence thus ‌confirms ​the pattern ‌of ​the Irish: ‌the ‌exalted one ​survives ​most durably ​in ​names of ‌water, ‌church, and ​daughter.[1]

Sources

  1. Koch, Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (2006), s.v. Brigit, Ffraid.
  2. Jackson, Language and History in Early Britain (1953), on Brittonic *brig-, Brigantia, and river-names.

Inscriptions & Seals

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

No ​Ogham inscription ‌records ‌the theonym ​Brigit. Ireland's ​earliest ​writing — ​the ​Ogham stones ‌of ​the fourth ​to ‌sixth centuries ​— preserves ‌personal ​names in ‌abundance, ​but the ​goddess's ​name is ​not ​among the ‌attested dedications; ​a ‌rendering such ​as ‌ᚁᚏᚔᚌᚔᚈ (B-R-I-G-I-T) ​is ‌a normalized ​modern ‌reconstruction of ​how the ​name ‌would have ‌been ‌notched, not ​a ​citation.[1]

The ‌epigraphic ​record of ​the name ‌belongs ​to her ​cognate: ‌Romano-British altars ​and ‌inscriptions to ​Brigantia ​from the ​Wall country ​— ‌the relief ‌from ​Birrens pairing ‌her ​with Minerva's ​attributes ‌is the ‌most famous ‌— ‌and the ​Gaulish ‌dedication to ​Brigindu ​at Volnay ​(Côte-d'Or).[2] ‌The pattern ​is instructive: ​the ​name of ‌the ​Exalted One ​enters ​the epigraphic ‌record ​wherever Roman ‌lettering met ​the ‌Celtic north, ​while ‌in Ireland ‌itself ​her name ​waited ​for the ​manuscript, the ‌well, ‌and the ​calendar.

Sources

  1. McManus, Damian, A Guide to Ogam (Maynooth: An Sagart, 1991), on the Ogham corpus and its personal-name content.
  2. Koch, Celtic Culture: A Historical Encyclopedia (2006), s.v. Brigantia (the Birrens relief; Volnay's Brigindu).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Brigit ​is the ‌rare ‌goddess whose ​three ​provinces ​are one ​act ​seen from ‌three ​sides. The ​smith ‌and the ​poet ‌do ​the same ‌thing: ​they take ​what ​is raw ​— ​ore, breath ‌— ​and ‌by heat ​and ‌pressure give ​it ‌a form ​that ‌outlasts them. ​The ​healer ‌does it ‌too, ‌coaxing the ​broken ​body back ‌toward ​the shape ​it ‌was ​made for. ​Cormac's ‌three sisters ​are ‌really a ​single ​lesson: ​making is ​sacred ‌wherever it ‌happens, ​at the ‌anvil, ​on the ​tongue, ‌or ‌at the ‌sickbed.[1]

‌Her fire ​at ‌Kildare, hedged ​and ​watched and ​never ‌consumed, ​is the ​emblem ​of that ‌lesson ​— a ​flame ​tended by ‌nineteen ​keepers ‌that still ​belongs, ‌on the ​twentieth ‌night, to ‌the ​name itself. ​Craft, ​kept ​in common, ‌does ‌not go ​out.

Sources

  1. Sanas Cormaic, s.v. Brigit, ed. Meyer (1912); Giraldus Cambrensis, Topographia Hiberniae II.34–36, ed. O'Meara (1949).
