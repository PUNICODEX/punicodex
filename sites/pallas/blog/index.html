Mythology

Pállas belongs to the older generation of gods, the Titans. His myth ​is less narrative than genealogical and thematic: he is the source from which the personifications of war's consequences descend.

Hesiod names Pállas as the son of ‌the Titan Kreios (Crius) and the sea-power Eurybia. From this union of sky-lineage and maritime force comes a deity whose domain is the practical mastery of conflict.

The Children of Pállas (Children)

By ​the Oceanid Styx, Pállas fathers Nike (Victory), Kratos (Strength), Bia (Force), and Zelos (Rivalry). These are not companions chosen at random; they are the necessary entourage of any war. Where Pállas is, they ‌follow.

War in Heaven (Titanomachy)

As a Titan, Pállas belongs to the older order overthrown by Zeus and the Olympians. His children, however, side with Zeus — a sign that the forces ​of warcraft can be transferred from one regime to the next.

Across Cultures

Pállas is ​often conflated in the modern imagination with Pallas Athena, whose cult title 'Pallas' may originally have been separate. Some ancient sources suggest Athena slew a giant or Titan named Pallas and took his name ​as a trophy. The overlap has made 'Pallas' ambiguous: it can mean the Titan of warcraft, or it can be an epithet of Athena. PUNICODEX treats them as distinct entries, each with its ‌own temple and domain.

Within the Greek tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Achérōn, Achilleús, Adámas, Ádōnis, Aḗr, and Aígyptos.

​A Meditation

Pállas is war as a technology. He is not the bloodlust of Árēs nor the strategic wisdom of Athēnâ; he is the craft itself — the forging of weapons, the drilling ​of formations, the transmission of technique from one generation of fighters to the next. His children are not emotions but outcomes: victory, strength, force, rivalry.

To think about Pállas is to ask whether ‌war can ever be pure craft, divorced from the reasons for which it is fought. The Greeks suspected not; even a craftsman of battle must eventually choose a side. His children followed Zeus, and ‌so, in the end, did the craft.
