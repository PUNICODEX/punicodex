The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

大阪

The name in its original Japanese form. Ōsaka (大阪) is attested in the source tradition — “Large hill or slope”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

osaka

Reduced to plain osaka, the name loses everything that made it specific: original diacritics and script distinctions. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Ōsaka

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Ōsaka restores original diacritics and script distinctions, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Ōsaka.com → xn--saka-k3a.com

The non-ASCII characters in Ōsaka are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Ōsaka.

Etymology

Japanese 大阪 Ōsaka; from 大 ō “great" + 阪 saka “slope, hill"; the mercantile hub of western Japan.

Meaning

Merchant City, Kansai

Pronunciation

How Ōsaka was spoken

/oːsaka/ Hepburn Japanese Reconstruction
Ō- Long open back vowel [oː], marked by the macron; the two-mora length makes the name Tier 1.
-sa- Voiceless alveolar fricative [s] plus short open front vowel [a], the middle syllable of the old name Naniwa.
-ka Voiceless velar stop [k] plus short open front vowel [a], a common place-name ending meaning 'place, slope'.

Modern Approximation

'OH-sah-kah' — the first vowel is long; the following syllables are short and level in pitch.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Japanese Naniwa (難波), the ancient port and first imperial capital on the site of modern Ōsaka
  • Japanese 大阪 (ō-saka), 'large hill' or 'large slope', the name that replaced Naniwa in the medieval period
  • Japanese 天下の台所 (tenka no daidokoro), 'the nation's kitchen', the Edo-period nickname for the merchant city

The Accent / Script Rule

Ōsaka is Tier 1 because the Hepburn restoration preserves the long initial vowel ō. As with other Japanese entries, the macron records length rather than pitch accent, which is not registrable at the DNS root.

05

Mythology

Stories of Ōsaka

Ōsaka began as the ancient port of Naniwa, gateway to the Asian continent and briefly an imperial capital. It later became the merchant city par excellence — the 'kitchen of the nation' — and the fortress from which Toyotomi Hideyoshi sought to rule a unified Japan.

Origins

Naniwa, the First Imperial Port

Long before it was called Ōsaka, the area was known as Naniwa. The Nihon Shoki records that Emperor Nintoku's great tomb rose on the Osaka plain, and in 645 Emperor Kōtoku moved the capital to Naniwa Nagara Toyosaki-no-miya, making it Japan's first official capital. The city of Ōsaka notes that Naniwazu port served as the arrival point for envoys, Buddhism, ceramics, and metallurgy from Korea and China, technologies that then spread across the archipelago.

Religion

Shitennō-ji and Prince Shōtoku

In 593 Prince Shōtoku founded Shitennō-ji, one of Japan's oldest Buddhist temples, in what is now Ōsaka. The temple was both a religious centre and a diplomatic statement: it marked the region's role as a point of contact with the continental mainland. Shitennō-ji's pagoda and gate still stand, a reminder that Ōsaka's identity was shaped by international exchange as much as by trade.

Unification

Hideyoshi's Castle Town

In 1583 Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had completed the military unification of Japan, began construction of Ōsaka Castle on the site of the former Ishiyama Hongan-ji fortress. He intended the castle to be the centre of a new political order. After his death the Toyotomi were destroyed by Tokugawa Ieyasu in the sieges of 1614–1615, but the castle kept its symbolic place as the heart of the Kansai merchant capital.

Commerce

The Nation's Kitchen

During the Edo period (1603–1868) Ōsaka became Japan's principal distribution centre for rice, sake, and luxury goods. The city's network of canals earned it the nickname tenka no daidokoro, 'the nation's kitchen'. Merchant culture flourished here: bunraku puppet theatre, kabuki, and the money markets of Dōjima made Ōsaka an urban culture distinct from the aristocracy of Kyōto and the samurai of Edo.
