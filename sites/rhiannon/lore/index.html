The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Rhiannon

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Rhiannon is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Great queen”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

rhiannon

The plain rhiannon form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Rhiannon

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Rhiannon. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding

The non-ASCII characters in Rhiannon are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Rhiannon.

Pronunciation

How Rhiannon was spoken

/r̥iˈa.nɔn/ Modern Welsh
Rh- Voiceless alveolar trill [r̥] — the Welsh digraph rh, an r sounded with breath; English has no equivalent, so 'hr' is the usual crutch.
-i- Short close front [i], the light first syllable; Welsh stress has not yet fallen.
-an- Open [a] in the stressed penult — Welsh words stress the next-to-last syllable, so the weight of the name lands here.
-non Alveolar nasal [n], open-mid [ɔ] (the Welsh o), final [n]; the unstressed ultima closes quietly.

Modern Approximation

'hree-AH-non' — begin with a breathy, whispered r, and put the stress on the middle syllable, the Welsh penult.

Etymological Kin

  • Middle Welsh Rhiannon, daughter of Hefaidd Hên, queen of Dyfed in the First and Third Branches of the Mabinogi.
  • Common Celtic (reconstructed) *Rīgantonā, 'great queen, divine queen' — the form Celticists derive from the name; a reconstruction, not an attestation.
  • Old Irish cognate rígan, 'queen', the exact cognate of the name's first element.
  • Modern Welsh rhiain, 'maiden, noble lady, queen' — the name's living relative in the modern language.

The Accent / Script Rule

Rhiannon is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the Middle Welsh form is already plain ASCII — there is no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the restoration to recover, and only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input. The etymology is a scholarly reconstruction rather than an attested form: Celticists derive the name from Common Celtic *rīgan- 'queen' plus a divine suffix, 'Great Queen', a reading that fits the figure precisely but must be labelled as what it is.

05

The Rider No Horse Can Catch

Horses, Sovereignty

Rhiannon is the Great Queen of the Mabinogi: the lady of the Otherworld who rides past Gorsedd Arberth on a pale horse at a slow, even pace that the fastest mounts of Dyfed cannot close — the goddess who chooses her own king, bears a false charge with unbroken dignity, and endures the penance of the horse-collar without bending.

Gorsedd Arberth

The mound above Narberth where wonders are seen: whoever sits there receives blows — or sees a marvel. Pwyll saw her ride past.

The Uncatchable Pace

She rides a pale horse at a slow, steady walk, and the swiftest riders in Dyfed cannot close the distance — until she is asked to stop.

The Birds of Rhiannon

Adar Rhiannon: three birds whose song, though heard far over the sea, is as clear as if they sang beside you — sweeter than any earthly song.

The Horse-Block

The marble mounting-block at the gate of Arberth, where she sits out her penance and offers to carry every guest to court on her back.

Sacred Symbols

The pale horse Sovereignty arriving at its own pace — the mount no pursuit can overtake, because the rider alone decides when to stop
The three birds Adar Rhiannon, whose song crosses the boundary between the living and the dead — the Otherworld made audible
The horse-block Dignity under a false sentence: the queen who carries her guests rather than break under the charge she did not earn
The bottomless bag The cleverness that out-plots a forced marriage — Gwawl's undoing, and the game of Badger in the Bag
The collars of asses The enchantment endured in the Third Branch — humiliation worn without surrender, until the spell is undone
06

Mythology

Stories of Rhiannon

Rhiannon is the strongest female figure in the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, and the tales give her a complete arc: the uncatchable rider who chooses her husband, the accused mother who bears her penance, the captive of the enchantment who comes home still a queen. Every word of her story is medieval Welsh prose, preserved in two great manuscripts.

First Branch · Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed

The Rider on the Pale Horse

Pwyll, prince of Dyfed, goes up to Gorsedd Arberth, the mound where a man either suffers blows or sees a wonder, and there he sees a lady in shining gold brocade riding past on a great pale horse — at a slow, even pace. He sends his swiftest rider after her; the faster the pursuit, the farther she draws ahead. Day after day it happens, with Pwyll's own horse and Pwyll himself, until at last he calls out: 'Maiden, for the sake of the man you love best, wait for me.' She stops at once: 'I will wait gladly — and it would have been better for the horse had you asked me that long ago.' She is Rhiannon, daughter of Hefaidd Hên, and she has come of her own will: she is being given to a man she does not want, and she has chosen Pwyll instead.

First Branch · Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed

The Bag That Would Not Fill

At the wedding feast, Pwyll grants a request before he hears it — and the petitioner is Gwawl fab Clud, the suitor Rhiannon refused, who claims her by the promise. Rhiannon is not dismayed; she devises the counter-plot. A year later Pwyll comes to Gwawl's feast disguised as a beggar, asking only that a certain small bag she has given him be filled with food. The bag cannot be filled: it will never be full, she has told him, unless a true lord treads the food down with his feet and declares that enough has been put in. Gwawl rises and treads it down — and Pwyll draws the bag shut over him, blows his horn, and his war-band falls on the hall. Each man strikes the bag and asks what is in it; 'a badger,' comes the answer, and from that night the Welsh played the game called Badger in the Bag. Gwawl buys his release with sureties and a renounced claim, and Rhiannon marries the man she chose.

First Branch · Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed

The May-Eve Vanishing and the Penance

After three childless years the nobles of Dyfed press Pwyll to take another wife; he begs one year more, and Rhiannon bears a son. On the night of the birth the six women set to watch all fall asleep, and the child is gone. Terrified of the blame, the women kill the pups of a hound, smear the blood on Rhiannon's face, lay the bones beside her, and swear she has destroyed her own child. Pwyll will not put her away, but a penance is imposed: she is to sit beside the horse-block at the gate of Arberth, tell her story to every traveller who comes, and offer to carry guests and strangers to the court on her back. She accepts the sentence she did not earn, and she bears it without breaking.

The child, meanwhile, has fallen into the strangest rescue in Welsh prose. Teyrnon Twrf Liant, a lord by the sea, loses a foal every May-eve; keeping watch over his mare, he sees a great clawed hand reach through the window to seize the newborn foal, and strikes it off with his sword. Outside the door he finds a baby wrapped in silk brocade. He and his wife rear the boy as Gwri Wallt Euryn, Gwri of the Golden Hair — who grows with uncanny speed and bears, year by year, the very likeness of Pwyll. When Teyrnon hears of Rhiannon's penance, he knows what he must do, and he and his wife bring the boy to Arberth. Rhiannon looks at him and says that if this is true, her pryder — her care, her anxiety — is lifted from her. From that word the boy is named Pryderi, and the penance ends.

Third Branch · Manawydan fab Llŷr

The Enchantment of Dyfed and the Collars of Asses

After Pwyll's death, Rhiannon is given in marriage to Manawydan fab Llŷr, and the four of them — Rhiannon and Manawydan, Pryderi and his wife Cigfa — return to Dyfed. There comes a peal of thunder and a fall of mist, and when it lifts, the land is emptied: not a person, not a dwelling, not a beast remains. For two years the four live by hunting; then they try their hand at saddlery, shield-making, and shoemaking in the towns of England, and each time the local craftsmen drive them out for making work too good to allow. Back in empty Dyfed, a shining white boar leads Pryderi and Manawydan to a fortress that was never there before; Pryderi goes in, lays hold of a golden bowl hanging by chains above a marble fountain, and is rooted to the spot, struck dumb. Rhiannon goes in after him, takes hold of the same bowl, and is trapped beside him — and fort, bowl, and both prisoners vanish.

Manawydan undoes the spell by patience. Sowing wheat, he watches three ripe fields stripped bare by mice on successive nights, and on the third he catches one slow, heavy mouse and condemns it to hang as a thief. A clerk, a priest, and a bishop come one after another to beg it off, each offering more than the last, and Manawydan names his price: Pryderi and Rhiannon released, the enchantment lifted from Dyfed, and no vengeance taken. The mouse is the wife of Llwyd fab Cilcoed, transformed with all his household; Llwyd had laid the spell to avenge his friend Gwawl fab Clud. Pryderi comes home with the gate-hammer about his neck, and Rhiannon with the collars of asses about hers — the second humiliation she has worn like a queen, and the second she survives.

Second Branch · Branwen ferch Llŷr

The Birds of Rhiannon

Rhiannon never steps on stage in the Second Branch, but her birds do. When the seven survivors of the Irish war come back over the sea bearing the wounded head of Brân the Blessed, they feast at Harlech for seven years, and there come three birds — the Birds of Rhiannon, Adar Rhiannon — that begin to sing to them. Every other song seems unlovely beside it; and though the birds must be gazed at far out over the sea, their singing is as clear as if they sang in the hall. At Gwales in Penfro the company feasts on for eighty years with the uncorrupted head, in the presence of the birds' song, until the door toward Cornwall is opened and memory and sorrow return. Later Welsh tradition says of the Adar Rhiannon that their song wakes the dead and lulls the living to sleep — the music of a world that does not end.

Extended Lore

Rhiannon cannot be caught because she has already decided where she is going. The fastest horses in Dyfed gallop themselves to exhaustion behind a rider who is barely trotting — and the moment she is asked, she stops, because stopping was always hers to give. That is the secret the First Branch teaches about power: the pace of a sovereign is not set by her pursuers. The rest of her story tests the lesson. Falsely accused, she does not rage or plead; she sits down by the horse-block and carries her guests, one by one, until the truth walks home on its own legs. Trapped in the enchantment, she endures the collars of asses and comes out still the Great Queen. Endurance, in her telling, is not passivity — it is the refusal to let an unjust sentence write your name. She waits, she carries, she outlasts; and the land is hers again.
