Why Rhiannon is spelled that way — and what it costs to get it right

There is a version of this name the early internet had to settle for: rhiannon. This is the file on how Rhiannon replaced it.

Rhiannon is the strongest female figure in the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, and the tales give her a complete arc: the uncatchable rider who chooses her husband, the accused mother who bears her penance, the captive of the enchantment who comes home still a queen. Every word of her story is medieval Welsh prose, preserved in two great manuscripts.

The Name Before the Marks

Spoken, the name runs `/r̥iˈa.nɔn/` — 'hree-AH-non' — begin with a breathy, whispered r, and put the stress on the middle syllable, the Welsh penult.

Where the Name Stood First

Rhiannon is a textual figure: no temple, votive deposit, or inscription names her, and the reconstructed *Rīgantonā has no epigraphic attestation anywhere in the Celtic world. Her evidence is manuscript evidence. The Four Branches survive complete in the White Book of Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, NLW Peniarth MSS 4–5, c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, c. 1382–1410), with earlier fragments in Peniarth 6; the tales themselves are dated by debate rather than document, most often to the later eleventh or early twelfth century. The geography of her story is real — Arberth, the court of Dyfed, stands by modern Narberth in Pembrokeshire — but no sanctuary of hers is known. The nearest material horizon is comparative, not hers: the two hundred and more dedications to the Gaulish horse-goddess Epona recovered across the Roman west, against which scholars have long read the rider of Arberth. The temple on the web is the newest of these addresses — the first one that fits in a pocket.
