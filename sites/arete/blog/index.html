Overview

Arete (arete) — Excellence, Virtue · The highest fulfillment of aretē ​— belongs to the Greek tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Excellence, Virtue". The name means "Excellence, virtue".

Arete is the Greek personification and concept of excellence, virtue, and the fulfilment ​of a thing's proper function. For a knife, arete is sharpness; for a horse, speed; for a human being, the full development of mind, body, and character. The word carries no single ​English equivalent: it means virtue, but also skill, courage, moral goodness, and simply being the best version of what one is.

Mythology

Arete is more concept than character, but Greek mythology dramatised her in ‌one famous scene: Prodicus' allegory of Heracles at the crossroads. There the young hero must choose between two women, one representing Arete and the other Kakia (Vice). The choice shaped every later ​Western telling of the hero's education.

Heracles at the Crossroads (Prodicus)

The sophist Prodicus of Ceos told it, and Xenophon preserved it (Memorabilia 2.1.21–34): the founding myth of Greek moral education. The young ‌Heracles, at the threshold of manhood, sat alone in a quiet place, torn, when two tall women approached. One was beautiful, modest, her eyes cast down, dressed in white: Arete. The other ​was fed to plumpness, her eyes bright, her skin painted, dressed to be seen: Kakia, Vice. Kakia spoke first — a short road to every pleasure, “no mention of toil.” Arete answered: ‌“I dwell with the gods… nothing good comes to mortals without toil and sweat”; she promised labor first, then honor, and fame that outlives the grave. Heracles took her hand. Prodicus ends ​with Vice objecting that her rival offers nothing but sweat — and Arete answering: “you would know better had you ever done anything noble.” The allegory means virtue makes her case in full ‌knowledge that she will lose the argument on comfort.

Arete in the Odyssey (Homer)

In the Odyssey 7.53–77, Arete is also a queen — the wife of Alcinous, ruler of the seafaring ​Phaeacians, and daughter of his brother Rhexenor. Homer lavishes rare detail on her: her hand on the household, her mediation of disputes, the people’s love (“as a god she is honored in ‌the land”). When the shipwrecked Odysseus begs for passage home, Athēnē instructs him to pass the king and fall at the queen’s knees — for her favor decides the matter; Nausicaa had ​said it plainly (6.303–315), her mother is the power in the house. Whether the queen’s name is the common noun made flesh or simply a well-chosen aristocratic one, Homer lets her live the ​meaning: arete as the excellence of judgement and hospitality that actually governs. It means the word was never only a soldier’s virtue.

Aristotle’s Virtue (Philosophy)

Aristotle made arete the load-bearing wall of ‌Western ethics (Nicomachean Ethics I–II, VI). Human beings, like every thing, have an ergon — a function: the activity of the rational soul. The arete of a thing is whatever lets it ‌perform its function well; so the arete of the eye is seeing, of a horse running, of a human being rational activity in accordance with virtue. Virtue itself he locates in the will: ‌a hexis, a settled disposition, “a mean between extremes, determined by reason” — courage between rashness and cowardice, generosity between waste and meanness. The goal is eudaimonia, flourishing, which is activity of ​the soul expressing arete. The doctrine means morality is a skill: not a gift or a rule-book but a trained excellence, acquired exactly as a weaver or a lyre-player acquires one.

The ​Only Good (Stoicism)

The Stoics drew the sharpest line of all: arete is the only good (Diogenes Laertius 7.89–98, summarizing Stoic ethics). Health, wealth, reputation, even life itself are “indifferents” — preferred, ‌but not good; their opposites are indifferents too, only dispreferred. Only wisdom, justice, courage, and self-control count, because only they are always beneficial and always within our power. The sage who possesses arete ​is free in the chains-and-kings sense: no tyrant can take from her the one thing that is genuinely hers. Epictetus, himself enslaved, taught that the door to freedom is the judgement that ​externals are nothing; Marcus Aurelius, an emperor, wrote the same doctrine from the other end of the social world, rehearsing each morning the indifference of palaces and shacks alike. The doctrine means ‌virtue ethics’ oldest claim in its purest form: the good life is the one thing the good person can never lose.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Arms and athletic gear — The equipment of the warrior and athlete, the two primary arenas of Homeric arete.

Archaeology & Evidence

Personified Arete enters Greek art in the later ​fifth century BCE — a draped woman labelled ΑΡΕΤΗ on Attic and South Italian vases — and the Romans minted her counterpart Virtus on imperial coinage, the standing female with spear and ‌shield. The Choice of Heracles became a favorite of Roman floor mosaicists: surviving examples from Antioch and the western empire (second–third century CE) show the two women flanking the hero, Arete with her ​hand raised in argument — the sophist’s debate staged in household stone. On the Greek mainland the word itself is the monument: honorific decrees and epitaphs across the Aegean praise individuals “for ​their aretē,” so the abstraction survives less in temples than in thousands of inscribed stones. Arete’s civic shrine is lexical — the culture agreed with Aristotle that the city exists to make ‌excellence possible.

Across Cultures

The Greek Arete was identified by Romans with ​Virtus, the masculine personification of courage and moral excellence. In Christian thought the concept was absorbed into the cardinal and theological virtues. The English word 'arete' is now used mainly by philosophers and ‌mountain climbers: the latter borrowed it for the sharp ridge of a mountain, a fitting image of the narrow, difficult path to excellence.

Within the Greek tradition, closely related names in the ​corpus include Achérōn, Achilleús, Adámas, Ádōnis, Aḗr, and Aígyptos.

Cultural Legacy

Arete remains one of the most influential Greek words in Western ethics. Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics is essentially a study of arete, ​and modern virtue ethics continues to use the concept. In sports, business, and education, 'excellence' is still the dominant ideal, even if the Greek context — that excellence is tied to function ​and community — is often forgotten. The mountain-climbing term 'arête' preserves the metaphor of the steep, narrow path. Arete is the word the Greeks used for what it means to be good at ‌being human.

A Meditation

Arete asks us what we are for. The question sounds strange to modern ears, ​because we tend to think of human beings as self-creating rather than as having a function. But the Greek concept is more flexible than it first appears: arete is not a single recipe ‌but the question of how this person, in this situation, can act excellently.

To pursue arete is to accept that excellence has a cost. The road is steep, the training is long, ​and the temptations of ease are constant. Yet the alternative is not freedom but a failure to become what one could have been.
