Why Lóngwáng is spelled that way — and what it costs to get it right

There is a version of this name the early internet had to settle for: longwang. This is the file on how Lóngwáng replaced it.

The Dragon Kings stand at the crossing of two Chinas: the literary China of the great Ming novels, where they are foils, creditors, and comic bureaucrats, and the living China of the village temple, where they were the address to which a drought-stricken county sent its prayers. The same figure is mocked in the novel and begged in the field.

The Name Before the Marks

The name is attested as 龍王, as Journey to the West (Xiyouji) and Investiture of the Gods (Fengshen Yanyi) preserve it. On the evidence, it means "water deity in Chinese mythology".

Strip Lóngwáng to longwang and 2 pieces of evidence vanish:

  • o → ó — Stress on o: the stress position the ASCII form cannot show.
  • a → á — Stress on a: the stress position the ASCII form cannot show.

These are the small, provable things a restoration is made of: not taste, not branding — attestation.

Spoken, the name runs `/lʊŋ˧˥ waŋ˧˥/` — lUHNG-WAHNG — two even syllables, each rising from mid to high pitch; the 'o' of the first is like the 'oo' in 'book', the 'a' of the second like the 'a' in 'father'.

Where the Name Stood First

The material cult behind the Dragon Kings is far older than the kings themselves. Shang oracle bones (c. 1250–1045 BCE) record rain sacrifices addressed to river and water powers, and Han texts describe rain-making rites performed with clay dragons — Dong Zhongshu's Chunqiu Fanlu gives the liturgy, and Wang Chong's Lunheng argues against it. Han mirrors and tomb reliefs show the older cosmic dragon of clouds and ascent, not yet a crowned bureaucrat. The named Dragon Kings of the Four Seas crystallize in medieval China under Buddhist influence: nāga kings with submarine palaces appear in translated sūtras from the early centuries CE, the Tang court formally enfeoffed the dragon kings of the Four Seas with royal titles, and by the Song their temples stood in the official register of sacrifices. The literary cycle reaches canonical form in the Ming novels Fengshen Yanyi and Xiyouji. The temple on the web is the newest of these addresses — the first one that fits in a pocket.
