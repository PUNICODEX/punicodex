Mythology

Stories of Baekdusan

Baekdusan's mythology is a layered palimpsest: a Korean foundation cycle in which heaven descends to a white mountain, a Manchu birth legend in which the same mountain produces an ancestor, and a documented history — the cataclysmic eruption of 946 CE — that reads like a creation event. The sources are the Samguk Yusa, the Manchu Veritable Records, and the Goryeosa; they do not agree with each other, and they do not need to.

Foundation myth

Where Hwanung Descended

The founding cycle of Korea begins on a mountain. In the Samguk Yusa ('Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms', compiled by the monk Il-yeon in the 1280s), Hwanin permits his son Hwanung to descend from heaven to the human world; Hwanung descends with three thousand followers beneath a sandalwood tree on Taebaeksan, the 'Great White Mountain'. Il-yeon's text names Taebaek, but later Korean tradition — especially twentieth-century historiography in the North — confidently transferred the descent to Baekdusan, the greater white mountain of the far north. There Hwanung met a tiger and a bear who begged to become human; he gave them mugwort and garlic and commanded a hundred days in a cave. The tiger failed; the bear endured and became a woman, Ungnyeo, who bore Hwanung a son, Dangun Wanggeom, founder of Gojoseon. Whatever peak is named, the myth makes the mountain the hinge on which heaven's authority turns into a nation: Korea's story starts where the sky touches snow.

Manchu foundation myth

The Maiden, the Red Fruit, and Bukuri Yongson

The Manchus tell the same mountain a different birth story. The Manchu Veritable Records (Manzhou shilu) relate that three maidens of the Bukuri clan came to bathe in Bulhuri Lake at the foot of Changbai, the Chinese name for Baekdusan. A magpie dropped a red fruit; the youngest, Fekulen, picked it up and swallowed it, conceived without a husband, and bore a boy named Bukuri Yongson. Set adrift, the child was found and raised among the Wild People, and from him descended the Aisin Gioro clan — the lineage of Nurhaci and the Qing emperors. The Jin dynasty of the Jurchens had honoured the mountain centuries before, and in 1677 the Kangxi Emperor formally closed Changbai to settlement and ordered imperial offerings to its spirit. Read beside the Dangun cycle, the legend is startling: two peoples who would contest the twentieth century both trace their womb to the same crater lake, as if the mountain issued ancestors the way it issues rivers.

Historical cataclysm

The Night the Mountain Burned, 946

In 946 — the first year of King Jeongjong — the Goryeosa, the official history of the Goryeo dynasty, records laconically that fire burst from Baekdusan. Modern volcanology calls it the Millennium Eruption: one of the largest volcanic events of the last five thousand years, ranked VEI 7, ejecting on the order of a hundred cubic kilometres of material. Its ash — a layer scientists label B-Tm — blankets northern Japan and appears in the Greenland ice sheet, where it dates the event precisely; the Korean annals and the ice agree. The blast hollowed the summit into a caldera now filled by Cheonji, 'Heaven Lake'. No foundation narrative required the eruption; it simply gave the sacred mountain a history of terrifying power. The Koreans who named it 'White Head' for its snow were naming a sleeping catastrophe: the white is ash as much as ice, and the reverence the mountain commands has always carried an undertone of fear.

Mountain cult

The Tiger of the Sansin

In Korean religion Baekdusan is a sansin, a mountain god. The standard iconography of the sansin — an old white-bearded sage with a crouching tiger — hangs in Buddhist temples from Seoul to the provinces, and Baekdusan ranks among the guardian peaks of the whole peninsula. Shamanic pilgrimage long sought the mountain spirit's protection for travel, childbirth, and healing. In the twentieth century the mountain was drafted into a new cult: the North Korean state made it the stage of revolution, claiming Kim Il Sung's guerrilla camp in its forests and declaring Kim Jong-il born at a secret camp near Samjiyon; leaders now 'ascend the sacred mountain of the revolution' on major anniversaries, and the region's name, Samjiyon, means 'three pools', after the crater's waters. The scholarly caution is simple: worship of the mountain spirit is attested for centuries; the state cult is a modern graft. Both agree on the underlying theology — whoever holds Baekdusan holds a claim on heaven.

02

Baekdusan, the White-Head Mountain

sacred volcano, origin mountain, and axis of the Korean world

Baekdusan — 白頭山, 'White-Head Mountain' — is the volcanic peak where Korean tradition says heaven and earth first met. Its crater lake Cheonji ('Heaven Lake') and snow-capped summit mark it as an axis of the Korean world: source of the Yalu and Tumen rivers, object of shamanic pilgrimage for a millennium, and in the twentieth century a political symbol claimed from both sides of a divided peninsula.

Sacred Peak

Revered across the Korean cultural sphere as the most sacred of mountains — a guardian peak in the old geomantic order of the realm.

White-Head Mountain

Snow and pale pumice crown the 2,744-metre summit, giving the mountain its name and its aura of purity and celestial proximity.

Volcano of Origin

Korean tradition makes it the mountain of Hwanung's descent; Manchu tradition makes it the birthplace of its founding ancestor.

Border of Realms

The peak stands on the China–North Korea frontier; the 1712 Mukedeng stele marked the old Qing–Joseon boundary at its foot.

Sacred Symbols

Snow-capped peak The white head that gives the mountain its name and signifies purity and height.
Lake Chonji The crater lake at the summit — 'Heaven Lake' — a mirror of heaven and a symbol of origin.
Tiger and bear Animals of the mountain forests, echoing the Dangun myth of the bear who endured and the tiger who failed.
Pine tree The hardy evergreen that clings to volcanic slopes and symbolises longevity.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

백두산 (白頭山)

The name in its original Korean form. Baekdusan (백두산 (白頭山)) is attested in the source tradition — “White head mountain”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

baekdusan

The plain baekdusan form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Baekdusan

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Baekdusan. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Baekdusan.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Baekdusan and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Baekdusan was spoken

pɛk The first syllable of Baekdusan, carrying primary stress in the romanised form.
tu The middle syllable or element, pronounced with level pitch in Korean.
san The final syllable, often closing in a nasal or simple vowel in the Korean reading.

Modern Approximation

'BEHK-too-sahn' — The initial consonant is unaspirated and sounds closer to a soft 'p'; the second syllable is level. The name literally means White-Head Mountain.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

The initial consonant is unaspirated [p], closer to a soft 'p' than an English 'b'. The name is the Korean reading of 白頭山, 'White-Head Mountain,' for the snow and pumice that cap the volcanic summit. Tier 2: plain romanisation loses no distinctive mark.

Extended Lore

Baekdusan invites reflection on the relationship between name, place, and identity. A mountain does not need a myth to be sacred; it needs witnesses. Here the witnesses are layered and contradictory: the Samguk Yusa's Hwanung, the Manchu Veritable Records' virgin-born ancestor, the Goryeosa's eyewitness of fire, Joseon geomancers, Qing emperors who sealed the slopes, and modern states that turned the peak into a flag. Each age found on the mountain the origin it needed. The restoration of the capitalised scholarly form Baekdusan is a small act of recognition in the same spirit: it marks the name as a proper noun of continuing significance in Korean culture and resists the flattening effect of ASCII-only domain names. The white head keeps its own counsel; the naming is the human work, and it never ends.
