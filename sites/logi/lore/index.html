The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

ᛚᚢᚴᛁ

The name in its original Norse form. Logi (ᛚᚢᚴᛁ) is attested in the source tradition — “Flame”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

logi

The plain logi form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Logi

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Logi. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Logi.com → logi.com

The non-ASCII characters in Logi are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Logi.

04

Pronunciation

How Logi was spoken

/ˈlo.ɣi/ Old Norse Reconstruction
L- Voiced alveolar lateral [l], unchanged from Proto-Germanic; the name opens as cleanly as English 'low'
-o- Short mid back [o], as in English 'lot'; the Old Norse vowel is short, never the diphthong of English 'low'
-gi Voiced velar fricative [ɣ] — the soft, gargled medial g of Spanish 'lago', not the hard stop of English 'go' — followed by short close front [i]; a light second syllable closed by the vowel

Modern Approximation

'LOH-yi' — two short, even syllables with the weight on the first; keep the o short and round, and let the medial g soften to a voiced friction in the throat, as in modern Icelandic logi.

Etymological Kin

  • Old Norse logi, m., 'flame, blaze' — the common noun borne as a proper name
  • Modern Icelandic logi — still the everyday word for a flame, from hearth-talk to weather reports
  • Word-family log n. 'flame' and loga v. 'to burn with a flame, blaze' — the same root at work and at rest
  • Fornjótr's son Logi, brother of Hlér (the sea, called Ægir) and Kári (the wind) in Skáldskaparmál and Hversu Noregr byggðist
  • Distinct figure Loki Laufeyjarson shares the sound, not the word: the old equation of Loki with logi 'flame' is abandoned, and Snorri stages the two as opponents

The Accent / Script Rule

Old Norse Logi is a common noun made person: logi, m., 'flame, blaze', beside log, n., 'flame', and the verb loga, 'to burn with a flame'. The restoration and the ASCII form are the same word — the manuscripts write four plain letters and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing — so Logi is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule. The etymology is transparent in the North; the old scholarly difficulty was never the word's meaning but its neighbor: nineteenth-century philology equated the god Loki with logi, 'flame', on the strength of the similar sound, an equation now abandoned — the vowels differ in kind, the figures never merge in the texts, and Gylfaginning itself stages Logi as the opponent who out-eats Loki at Útgarðr. Attestation and lexicography follow Cleasby & Vigfusson, An Icelandic-English Dictionary (1874), s.v. logi; Zoëga, A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (1910), s.v. logi; and de Vries, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch (1962), s.v. logi.

05

Fire, Flame, Giant

The Trough, the Wildfire, and the Sons of Fornjótr

Logi is the Norse name for flame itself — the common noun logi, 'blaze', walking on two legs into the greatest hall of giants the mythology knows. In Útgarða-Loki's court he is served a trough of meat against the god Loki, and when the two eaters meet in the middle, Logi has consumed the meat, the bones, and the trough besides: wildfire does not chew, it consumes. He is catalogued in the norse pantheon under the domain "Fire, Flame, Giant", and his meaning is the plainest in the lexicon — "Flame".

The Trough

The full-length feeding-trough set on the hall floor; Loki clears the meat from the bones — Logi clears everything.

The Eating Contest

The one fair contest of Útgarðr: no enchantment was needed — the opponent was fire.

Villieldr

'Wildfire', the unmasking word: what sat down to eat with Loki was flame itself.

Hlér and Kári

His brothers in the Fornjótr genealogy: the sea and the wind — an elemental triad with fire between them.

Sacred Symbols

The feeding-trough Appetite made measurable: the only scoreboard in mythology that fire ever emptied
The eaten bones The line between hunger and combustion: Loki strips the bone, Logi strips the question
The flame (logi) The common noun kept as a name — fire called by the very word the hearth already knew
The brothers Hlér and Kári Sea and wind: the family that makes fire one element among three, not a freak among persons
06

Mythology

Stories of Logi

Logi has one great scene and one great family. The scene is the eating contest in Útgarða-Loki's hall, the showpiece of Þórr's eastern journey in Gylfaginning; the family is the Fornjótr genealogy, which makes him fire brothered to sea and wind. Between them the mythology says everything it has to say about him: he is not a character with a history but an element with a name — and his single appearance is constructed to prove exactly that.

Gylfaginning 44

The Road to Útgarðr

Þórr sets out east for Jǫtunheimr with Loki for company, and at a peasant's farm takes the children Þjálfi and Rǫskva as his servants. On the road they meet the giant who calls himself Skrýmir: they sleep, unknowing, in the thumb of his dropped glove, and Þórr's three hammer-blows in the night — which the giant greets as a leaf, an acorn, and a bird's dropping — will be explained only later. Skrýmir points them to Útgarðr, a stronghold so vast the travellers must tilt their heads back to see the top of it, and vanishes. Every step of the journey is a setup: by the time the company reaches the hall, the story has already taught the reader that nothing here is what it is called.

Gylfaginning 45

The Eating Contest

Útgarða-Loki, the giant-king, declares that no one may stay in his hall who is not master of some art or accomplishment beyond other men. Loki, travel-hungry and never at a loss, speaks first: no one in the hall can eat faster than he can. The king accepts the challenge and calls from the far bench a man named Logi. A trough full of meat is set down on the floor between them; Loki takes one end, Logi the other, and they eat toward each other. They meet in the middle — and there the verdict lies in plain sight. Loki has eaten every shred of meat from the bones; but Logi has eaten the meat, and the bones, and the trough as well. 'And it now seemed to everyone,' Snorri says, 'that Loki had lost the game.'

Gylfaginning 46–47

The Unmasking

The rest of the evening fails the same way: Þjálfi is outrun by Hugi, Þórr cannot drain the drinking-horn, cannot lift the grey cat, and is forced to one knee by the old foster-mother Elli. In the morning Útgarða-Loki walks the company outside — and when he has told the truth and they turn back, the stronghold is gone. Skrýmir was himself; the glove was the hall; the blows fell on three mountain-valleys. The horn's end stood in the sea, and the tide now answers to Þórr's drinking; the cat was the Midgard Serpent; Elli was old age, whom no one out-wrestles; Hugi was the king's own thought. 'And he who contended with Loki in the eating-contest was wildfire itself' — villieldr — and it was no wonder he made short work of the trough. Fire against hunger was never a contest of the same kind.

Skáldskaparmál / Hversu Noregr byggðist

The Sons of Fornjótr

Logi's other attestation is genealogical. Skáldskaparmál, teaching the kennings for wind, names Kári as Fornjótr's son and his brothers as Hlér and Logi; Hversu Noregr byggðist, the legendary preface to the Norwegian settlement preserved in the Flateyjarbók, gives the triad its famous form: Fornjótr had three sons — Hlér, 'whom we call Ægir', Logi, and Kári. Sea, fire, and wind as one old giant's household: the genealogy is not narrative but classification, the North keeping its elements in a family tree, with the sea Logi's brother and the wind his kin.

Vafþrúðnismál 51

The Flame That Goes Out

The word's longest echo is eschatological. When Óðinn asks the giant Vafþrúðnir who will survive the world's burning, the answer keeps Víðarr and Váli in the gods' sanctuaries 'when Surtr's flame dies down' — þá er sloknar surtalogi. The compound welds Logi's common noun to Surtr's name: the fire that ends the world and the flame that eats the trough are one word at two scales. Even eschatology, in Old Norse, is written in the vocabulary of the hearth.

Extended Lore

Names are not merely labels; they are compressed worlds. Logi compresses the oldest observation the North made about fire: it has appetite. The myths give every god a hunger — Óðinn for knowledge, Þórr for food and fight — and then stage one contest where hunger meets its master. Loki is the gods' own appetite, the mouth of the company, and he eats well; no shame attaches to losing as he lost. The lesson the hall teaches is a taxonomy, not a moral: some appetites are animal, and some are elemental, and the two are not the same kind of thing. Unicode restoration has nothing to rescue here — the name arrived whole, four letters the scribes and the ASCII machines spell exactly alike — and that plainness suits him: a word so common the hearth used it nightly, set in capital letters and given a bench in the hall of giants.
