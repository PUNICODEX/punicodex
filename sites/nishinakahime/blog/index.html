Overview

Nishinakahime (nishinakahime) — Rice, Agriculture · Day-sun wave princess — belongs to the Japanese ​tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Rice, Agriculture". The name means "Day-sun wave princess".

Nishinakahime is the Japanese goddess of potter's clay — the kami the Kojiki writes 波邇夜須毘売 and ​reads Hani-yasu-hime, 'the clay that yields.' Born, with her brother, from the dying goddess Izanami's body, she is the divinity potters invoke when earth must soften into vessel: patroness of the kiln, ​the wheel, and Japan's thousand-year ceramic tradition.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 日子波邇夜須毘売命. That ​attested script remains the primary evidence for the conventional romanization Nishinakahime. The canonical Latin spelling does not pretend to recover marks it never contained: it fixes one reviewed reference form while leaving ‌the original script visible for comparison.

The lexicon's original script 日子波邇夜須毘売命 carries the core name the Kojiki writes 波邇夜須毘売神 and reads Hani-yasu-hime, 'the clay that yields,' with the honorific 日子 and the ​title 命 (-no-mikoto). The Nihon Shoki writes the same goddess 埴山姫 (Haniyama-hime), 'Clay-Mountain Princess' — the 埴 (hani, 'potter's clay') element written directly. The restored form Nishinakahime romanizes the Hepburn reading of ‌the lexicon's longer spelling; the traditional reading of the Kojiki name is Hani-yasu-hime.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ni.ɕi.na.ka.hi.me/ — Modern Standard Japanese (Hepburn).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ni- — ​Alveolar nasal [n] plus close front [i] — light and short.
  • -shi- — Voiceless alveolo-palatal fricative [ɕ] plus [i]; the soft 'shee' of the Hepburn reading.
  • -naka- — Alveolar nasal [n], open ​central [a], voiceless velar [k], open central [a] — level and even.
  • -hime — Glottal [h], [i], bilabial [m], close-mid [e]; in the Kojiki's spelling, 毘売 marks the feminine -hime, 'princess'.

For the ​modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'NEE-shee-nah-kah-hee-meh' — the Hepburn reading of the restored form. The Kojiki's name, 波邇夜須毘売, reads 'HAH-nee-YAH-soo HEE-meh'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Kojiki spelling — ​波邇夜須毘売神 (Hani-yasu-hime-no-kami) — hani 埴 'potter's clay' + yasu 安 'easy, workable': the clay that yields
  • Nihon Shoki spelling — 埴山姫 (Haniyama-hime) 'Clay-Mountain Princess'; also 埴安神 (Haniyasu-no-kami)
  • Japanese — 埴輪 haniwa — ​'clay rings', the Kofun-period terracottas; the same hani 'clay' lives on in their name

Nishinakahime is a plain restoration by the mechanical rule: the ASCII form loses no distinctive feature. The kami ‌behind the name is the one the Kojiki writes 波邇夜須毘売神 and reads Hani-yasu-hime — 'the clay that yields' — born from Izanami's body in her fatal illness; the lexicon's longer original-script form ​日子波邇夜須毘売命 carries the honorific 日子 and the title 命. She is a minor but vividly attested kami of potter's clay, not a figure of the imperial cult.

Mythology

Nishinakahime's mythology is two ‌sentences long in the Kojiki — and they are among the most striking in the whole canon. Everything else the tradition knows about her is in her name: hani, potter's clay, and ​yasu, the yielding. The potters of Japan's ancient kiln towns supplied the rest of her story — a thousand years of practice laid at her feet.

The Dying Goddess Bears the Clay ‌Kami (Kojiki)

Izanami gave birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi, and the fire burned her so badly that she sickened and lay dying. From her body in its extremity came the last ​kami of the living generation: from her vomit, the metal-mountain deities Kanayamahiko and Kanayamahime; from her urine, the water goddess Mizuha-no-me; and from her bowels, the brother and sister of clay — ‌Hani-yasu-biko and Hani-yasu-hime (Kojiki, birth of Kagutsuchi; the Nihon Shoki gives her the parallel names Haniyama-hime, 'Clay-Mountain Princess,' and Haniyasu-no-kami). The texts state this with the same flat calm as everything else ​in the divine genealogies, yet the implication is immense: the kami of humanity's three great materials — metal, water, clay — are born from the mother's death-agony. In Shinto's most radical birth ‌story, the technologies that make civilization possible are not stolen from heaven or won by conquest; they are what a dying goddess leaves behind.

A Name That Means 'Clay That Yields' (Name)

​Hani-yasu is a name that explains itself, the way kami names so often do. Hani (埴) is potter's clay, the earth dug from the subsoil that holds water and takes any shape; ​yasu (安/易) is 'easy, gentle, workable.' The goddess is, literally, the clay that yields — earth soft enough to form. The great Kokugaku scholar Motoori Norinaga, in his Kojikiden, glossed the name ‌as hani-neyasu: clay kneaded and worked until its stiffness softens and it becomes obedient to the hand. The spelling is itself a lesson: the Kojiki's characters 波邇夜須 are phonetic choices made for ‌sound rather than sense, which is why later writers preferred the semantic form 埴, 'clay,' that the Nihon Shoki uses in Clay-Mountain Princess. Shinto rarely makes abstract theology; here the theology is ‌a craft instruction. The name is a recipe: work the clay until it yields — and to read the name correctly is already to know what she is.

Three Gifts of Civilization from ​One Body (Kojiki)

Read together, the kami born from Izanami's failing body form a complete inventory of material culture. Kanayama-hime, from the vomit, is metal — ore, smelting, the smith's art. Mizuha-no-me, ​from the urine, is water — wells, irrigation, the pure stream. And Hani-yasu-hime, from the feces, is clay — the kiln, the pot, the fired vessel that outlives its maker. Japan confirmed ‌the inventory in its shrines: the clay siblings are enshrined in pottery districts as the kiln ancestors (tōso), and the Kanayama kami at mines and smithies. The distribution of a goddess's dying ​body across human technology is unique in the Kojiki: no other birth narrative so completely fuses physiology and craft. Her mythology is her etymology writ large — from the body of the ​mother earth-goddess comes the very substance that, worked by human hands, holds grain, sake, and the ashes of the dead.

The Kiln Ancestors (Folk Tradition)

The imperial chronicles give Hani-yasu-hime two clauses; ‌Japanese potters gave her a thousand years. The great ceramic towns — Bizen, Shigaraki, Echizen, Seto, Tanba, Tokoname, the six ancient kilns (rokkoyō) — enshrine the clay kami as patrons and founders ​of their craft, under the titles Clay-Mountain Princess and Clay-Mountain Prince, and kiln observances still open with offerings to the earth the pots are made from. The tradition's material memory runs deeper ​than the shrines: the haniwa, the 'clay rings' and hollow figures set on the great tombs of the Kofun age (3rd–6th centuries), carry her element hani in their very name — clay ‌that was never forgotten as sacred stuff. A goddess born in a moment of utmost indignity became the quiet patron of Japan's most refined art: the tea bowl's wabi, the Bizen jar's ​unglazed fire-marks — all of it earth-plus-fire, her two inheritances in one vessel.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Nishinakahime concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed ‌statement about the name:

  • Hani 埴, potter's clay — The worked earth itself; what she is, not merely what she rules.
  • The kneaded lump — Clay worked until it yields (hani-neyasu): patience ​made visible, the potter's first offering.
  • Kiln flame — Kagutsuchi's fire tamed by craft — the second element that turns earth into vessel.
  • Haniwa cylinders — The 'clay rings' of the Kofun ​tombs, named for the same hani — her element's most famous archaeological form.
  • The unglazed vessel — The tea-bowl aesthetic of wabi: value found precisely where the clay is least disguised.

Archaeology ​& Evidence

No shrine can be proven hers from inscriptions of her mythic era — her attestation is textual — but her element dominates the Japanese archaeological record. The haniwa cylinders, houses, ​warriors, and dancing figures of the Kofun period (3rd–6th c. CE) are her name's material echo; major concentrations survive at the Saitobaru kofun group, Miyazaki Prefecture (a designated Special Historic Site), and ‌the Shibayama district of Chiba. The Sue-ware tradition (5th–6th c.) left hundreds of anagama tunnel-kiln sites across western Japan — among them the Sanage kiln complex in Aichi Prefecture — whose grey ‌stoneware marks the arrival of high-fired ceramics. The textual witnesses are the Kojiki (712 CE) and the Nihon Shoki (720 CE), both preserved through the medieval manuscript tradition.

Across Cultures

Hani-yasu-hime was never absorbed into a Buddhist bodhisattva or a ‌combinatory shrine the way Inari or Hachiman were; the folk tradition kept her discrete — a local craft deity of kiln and clay-pit — and her syncretism is better described as occupational ‌than theological. The meaningful comparisons are pan-Eurasian. Across the world, creator gods make humankind from clay: Enki and Ninhursag shape the first worker from earth's mud in Sumerian myth; the K'iche' gods ‌form people from maize dough in the Popol Vuh; Prometheus fashions mankind from earth and water; and in Hebrew tradition ʾādām is the one made from ʾădāmāh, red arable earth — a ​kinship the Greek adamas echoes elsewhere in this very lexicon. The Japanese case is distinctive in one detail: elsewhere the potter-god shapes humanity; here the goddess is the clay itself, and what ‌is shaped from her — vessel, figurine, kiln-fired haniwa — is her continuing body.

Kindred figures in the PuniCodex cross-tradition index include Anāhitā, Auðhumla, Ọbalúayé, Bꜣstt, Cernunnos, and Cōātlīcue, each linked through ​earth / mother / fertility.

Cultural Legacy

Her legacy is written into the Japanese language and landscape. The word hani never left the language: haniwa, the terracotta rings and figures of the ​Kofun-period tombs, are her name's most famous compound, and the Kofun age (c. 250–538 CE) left them in their tens of thousands — the Saitobaru kofun cluster in Miyazaki alone preserves over ​three hundred. The six ancient kilns (rokkoyō) — Bizen, Echizen, Seto, Shigaraki, Tanba, Tokoname — still work the clays their regions have yielded for a millennium and a half, and potters' guilds ‌and kiln shrines still honor the clay kami at the start of the firing season. The tea ceremony, which elevated the humble, unglazed, slightly irregular bowl into the summit of taste, is ​in a sense her aesthetic theology: value discovered precisely where the material is least disguised. She has no festival on the national calendar and no seat in the imperial rites — her ​cult belongs to the workshops — but anyone who has held a Japanese tea bowl has held something her name explains.

A Meditation

Shinto's honesty about where gods come from has no sharper example. The clay goddess is born from the dying Izanami's excrement — the lowest moment of the greatest mother ​in the canon — and the Kojiki records it without apology, in the same breath as the fire that killed her. The tradition refuses the instinct to clean its gods up. Precisely ​because she comes from what every culture is taught to be ashamed of, Hani-yasu-hime is the patron of the material every culture actually lives by: the worked earth beneath the refinement, the ‌clay beneath the glaze.

Her theology is a discipline of attention. The potter at the wheel repeats the name's own gloss — knead, work, wait for the yielding — and in the ​yielding discovers that matter itself is not dead but responsive, almost willing. In an age of plastics and pixels, clay that must be dug, kneaded, thrown, and fired remains one of the ‌last materials that answer the hand directly. That responsiveness is what her name means, and it is what the dying goddess left behind for the living.
