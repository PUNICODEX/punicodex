Overview

The Original Script

The name is ​preserved in Younger Futhark as ᚼᛅᛏᛁ.

The scholarly transliteration is hati.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • ᚼ (hagall) writes /h/
  • ᛅ (ár) writes /a/, /á/ and /æ/
  • ᛏ (Týr) writes both /t/ ‌and /d/
  • The spelling hati is a normalized phonetic reconstruction; Younger Futhark does not distinguish voiced/voiceless stops or separate short and long vowels

The normalized Old Norse form is Hati — already plain ​ASCII, with no diacritic or distinctive letter for the restoration to recover; in the Younger Futhark the name would have been carved ᚼᛅᛏᛁ (h-a-t-i, four runes with no ambiguity). Because the name ‌is pure ASCII, its domain was registered long ago in the ordinary way: hati.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection and is watched for release — no form of ​the name is currently owned, and this temple claims none.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈhɑ.ti/ — Old Norse Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • H- — Voiceless glottal fricative [h], as ‌in English 'hat'; Old Norse initial h is fully sounded.
  • -a- — Short open back unrounded [ɑ], held brief — Old Norse short a, not the long á.
  • -ti — Voiceless dental ​stop [t] and short front [i]; the second syllable is unstressed and clipped, as in modern Icelandic.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'HAH-tih' — two short syllables, stress on the ​first; the vowels are short and plain, with no diphthong and no final nominative -r, for the name ends in a stem vowel, not an ending.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • ​Old Norse — Hati Hróðvitnisson, 'Hati, son of Hróðvitnir' — the fame-wolf's son of Grímnismál and Gylfaginning.
  • Common noun — hati, 'hater, enemy' — the ordinary Old Norse agent noun, from the verb ​hata, 'to hate'.
  • English cognate — hate — the verb hata and English hate descend from the same Proto-Germanic word, *hatōjan-.
  • Modern Icelandic — Hati, unchanged in spelling since the medieval manuscripts.

​Hati is a plain restoration by the mechanical rule: the normalized Old Norse form is already plain ASCII — there is no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the ASCII alphabet to lose. ‌Unlike most Old Norse masculine theonyms it carries no nominative -r, since the name is the agent noun hati ('the hater') with a weak-declension stem vowel. The name's transparency is its whole story: ​the wolf is called, simply, 'the Hater'.

Mythology

Hati's mythology is a single image carried by a handful of verses: a wolf, a moon, and a chase that ends only with the world. ‌The Poetic Edda names him once, Snorri gives him his quarry and his ending, and Völuspá's seeress supplies the brood he springs from.

The Pair Before Heaven (Grímnismál 39)

The name's one ​poetic attestation comes in Óðinn's great catalogue of the cosmos in Grímnismál, where the disguised god enumerates the heavens for the torture-bound Geirrøðr: 'Skǫll is the name of the wolf who follows the ‌bright-faced god to the sheltering wood; but the other is Hati, Hróðvitnir's son, who runs before the bright bride of heaven.' The bright bride of heaven is the sun; the wolf who runs ​before her is on the heels of the moon, which travels ahead of her. In four lines the Edda converts the machinery of the sky into a hunt: what astronomy calls a ‌course, the poem calls a chase.

Skǫll After the Sun, Hati After the Moon (Gylfaginning 12)

Snorri systematizes the verse. When Gangleri asks why the sun hurries so — 'she speeds as if ​she were afraid' — High One answers that she has good cause: 'the one who seeks her is right behind her, and she has no other way out than to run.' Two wolves ‌do the work of the heavens: Skǫll chases the sun and will catch her, and 'the other wolf is called Hati Hróðvitnisson; he runs in front of her and wants to catch the ​moon — and that is what will happen.' Snorri's gloss fixes the reading the poem left compressed, and with it the moon's doom is put on the schedule of things foretold.

Mánagarmr, ​the Moon-Hound (Gylfaginning 12)

In the same chapter Snorri names the mightiest of the wolf-brood: Mánagarmr, 'moon-hound' — 'he will be filled with the flesh of all who die, and he will swallow ‌the moon and spatter the sky and all the air with blood; from this the sun will lose its brightness.' Whether Mánagarmr is Hati under a second name or a third wolf beside ‌the pair, the manuscripts and the handbooks leave open; Simek and Lindow record the identification as probable but unprovable. What is certain is the image: the moon taken, and the blood of the ‌swallowed light staining heaven.

The Brood of the Ironwood (Völuspá 39-40)

Völuspá's seeress supplies the kennel. 'In the east sat the old one in the Ironwood and bred there Fenrir's brood; from ​all of them comes one in particular, a snatcher of the moon in a troll's skin. He gorges on the life of doomed men, reddens the gods' dwellings with red blood; the sunshine ​turns black in the summers after, and all weathers run hostile.' The poem names no name — but the tungls tjúgari, the 'snatcher of the moon', is the role Grímnismál and Snorri assign ‌to Hati, and the handbooks read the figures together: the Ironwood breeds the chase, and the chase ends the world.

The Catch at Ragnarǫk (Gylfaginning 51 · Völuspá)

The ending is shared ​by every witness. When Ragnarǫk comes, the wolves' running stops: Skǫll takes the sun, Hati takes the moon, Fenrir himself swallows Óðinn, and 'the sun turns black, earth sinks into the sea, the ​bright stars vanish from the sky.' The Edda's cosmology is built so that the nightly order of the heavens is only an interval — the long meanwhile between the start of the chase ‌and its foreknown end. Hati never fails of his quarry; he simply has not caught it yet.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Hati concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, ​each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The running wolf — Pursuit without rest: the sky itself read as a hunt that has never once paused
  • The fleeing moon — Máni's course ​as flight — the measured path of the heavens reinterpreted as terror
  • Járnviðr, the Ironwood — The eastern forest where the wolf-brood is bred: chaos with an address, growing older by the year
  • ‌The darkened sky — Völuspá's 'the sunshine turns black' — the eclipse and the world's ending written in one image

Archaeology & Evidence

Hati is a wholly textual figure: no runestone names him, ​no votive deposit invokes him, and no image can be securely assigned to him. Wolf iconography is plentiful in the Viking-Age record — the wolf-rider and great-wolf scenes on the Gotland picture stones ‌have long been argued over, and Hyrrokkin's wolf-mount at Baldr's funeral was carved in Óláfr pái's hall (Húsdrápa, c. 983) — but no carving carries an inscription that ties a wolf to ​the moon-chase. The name's witnesses are the manuscripts: Grímnismál in the Codex Regius (c. 1270) and AM 748 I 4to, Völuspá in the Codex Regius and Hauksbók, and Gylfaginning in the Codex Regius ​of the Prose Edda and its sister codices (Codex Wormianus, Codex Upsaliensis), all recording material centuries older. The þulur of Skáldskaparmál preserve Hati and Hróðvitnir among the poetic heiti for 'wolf' — the ​tradition's own file of the name.

Across Cultures

Hati has no cult, no continental cognate, and no existence outside the Eddic record: he belongs to the North's own eschatological machinery. Scholars since the nineteenth century have noted that ​the doubling of the apocalyptic wolf — one chasing the sun, one the moon — is probably a secondary systematization of the older single figure, the bound Fenrir who breaks loose at the ​world's end; Simek and Lindow treat Skǫll and Hati as elaborations within the Fenrir-complex rather than independent tradition. What the pair does uniquely well is etiology: the chase gives the sky a reason ‌for its haste, and the wolf drawing near gives the North its explanation of the eclipse — when the pursuer closes, the moon darkens. The devouring-monster account of eclipses is a comparative ‌commonplace across cultures; the Norse version is distinctive for giving its monsters names, a pedigree, and a date.

Cultural Legacy

Hati's afterlife is the ‌eclipse-wolf's: wherever the Ragnarǫk story is retold, the pair Skǫll and Hati run in the sky above it. Victorian and later handbooks of Norse myth made the chase a set-piece of cosmological ​explanation — the sky's motions as a hunt, the eclipse as the moment the wolf gains — and modern retellings, novels, and games keep the two wolves as Ragnarǫk's outriders. In scholarship the ‌name is the standing example of the Edda's transparent theonymy: a god-figure called, without ornament, 'the Hater'. The name never entered the living name-pool as Baldr's did; it survives where it was born, ​in the poetry, doing the one job it was given.

A Meditation

Hati is the Edda's most economical idea: take the verb 'to hate', give it legs, and set it running after the light. There is no ​malice-scene, no dialogue, no character beyond the name — only pursuit. And that is the point. The Norse imagination looked at the most orderly thing in the world, the clockwork of sun ​and moon, and read it as flight: the lights hurry because something hates them and is coming. Time itself becomes the distance still left between the wolf and the moon — every night ‌the moon escapes is a night the world continues. Hati asks the uncomfortable question the whole Ragnarǫk cycle asks: how do you keep the light moving, knowing exactly what follows it? The Edda's ​answer is the one it gives everywhere. You keep running. The chase is the world.
