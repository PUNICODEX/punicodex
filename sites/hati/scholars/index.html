01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Hati (hati) ​— Moon-Chaser, ‌Chaos · The ‌Hater, Son ​of the ​Fame-Wolf — is ​the wolf ​the Edda sets ​running after ‌the moon: Hati ​Hróðvitnisson, son ​of the fame-wolf ‌Fenrir, who ​runs before ‌the sun and ​at the ‌heels of Máni, ​and who ​is fated to ​seize his ​quarry out of ​the sky ‌when Ragnarǫk ​comes.[1]

His name ‌is one ​of the most ‌transparent theonyms ​in the corpus: ‌Old Norse ​hati is the ‌ordinary agent ​noun 'hater, ​enemy', from the ‌verb hata, ‌'to hate' — ‌the wolf ​is called, simply, ​'the Hater'.[3] ‌His dossier is ​thin and ​exact: one ‌verse of Grímnismál ​names him ​and his father, ‌Snorri's Gylfaginning ​gives him his ‌quarry and ​his foreknown success, ​and Völuspá ​supplies the ​Ironwood kennel he ‌springs from, ‌where the old ​giantess breeds ‌Fenrir's brood and ​'from all ​of them comes ‌one in ‌particular, a ‌snatcher of the ‌moon in ​a troll's skin'.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the name as ​Hati — ​identical to the ‌ASCII input, ​because normalized ​Old Norse writes ​no diacritic ‌in this name; ​the mechanical ​rule therefore places ​it in ‌Tier 2, a ​statement about ‌orthography and ​never about the ‌figure. The ​project holds no ‌domain for ‌this name: the ​plain-ASCII hati.com ​is registered to ​a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 12 (Skǫll and Hati; Mánagarmr) and 51 (Ragnarǫk), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Grímnismál 39 and Völuspá 39–40 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. hati, hata.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested ‌in Old Norse ‌as Hati, always ​with the ​patronymic Hróðvitnisson, 'son ​of Hróðvitnir'. ​It is the ​ordinary Old Norse ‌masculine agent ​noun hati, 'hater, ​enemy', formed ‌on the verb ​hata, 'to hate' ‌— the ​same Proto-Germanic word ‌(hatōjan-) that ​gives English hate.[1] ​The etymology is ​not disputed; ​the name means ​what it ‌says. The patronymic ​fixes his pedigree: ‌Hróðvitnir*, 'fame-wolf', ​is a poetic ‌heiti of ​Fenrir, so the ‌verse that names ​Hati names ‌him as the ​bound wolf's ​son.[2]

The ASCII ‌form is not ‌a compromise ‌here: normalized Old ​Norse Hati ​is already written ‌in plain ASCII ​letters. There ​is no diacritic, ‌no thorn ​or eth, no ​vowel-length mark for ‌the early ​domain-name system to ‌have stripped. The ​name is ​classified Tier 2 ​by the ​project's mechanical rule ‌— the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​the ASCII form ‌loses. Note ​also what the ​name lacks: unlike ‌most Old ‌Norse masculine theonyms ‌(Baldr, Hermóðr), ‌it carries no ​nominative -r, because ‌it is ​a weak-declension agent ​noun ending ​in a stem ‌vowel.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

​- h → ​H — ‌Same, capitalized

  • ​a → a ​— Same

​- t → ‌t — ​Same

  • i ‌→ i — ​Same

The ‌project holds no ​domain for ‌this name: hati.com ‌is held by ​a third ​party outside the ​PuniCodex collection ​and is watched ‌for release; this ‌temple claims ​no domain.

Sources

  1. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. hati, hata, Hróðvitnir.
  2. Zoëga, Geir T., A Concise Dictionary of Old Icelandic (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1910), s.v. hati; de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. hata.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈhɑ.ti/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- H- ​— Voiceless ‌glottal fricative ​[h], as in ​English hat; ‌Old Norse ​initial h ‌is fully ​sounded

  • ‌-a- — Short ​open back ​unrounded [ɑ], ​held brief ​— Old ​Norse short a, ‌not the ​long á

‌- -ti ​— Voiceless ‌dental stop [t] ​and short ‌front [i]; ​the second ‌syllable is ​unstressed and clipped

​For the ‌modern speaker, ‌the closest ‌approximation is: ​'HAH-tih' — two ​short syllables, ‌stress on ​the first, ​with plain ‌short vowels and ​no final ​nominative -r.

‌Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the ​name:

  • Old ​Norse — ​Hati Hróðvitnisson, ​the moon-chasing ‌wolf of ‌Grímnismál and Gylfaginning

​- Common ‌noun — ​hati, 'hater, ​enemy', the ‌agent noun of ‌hata, 'to ‌hate'

  • ‌English cognate ​— hate, ‌the same Proto-Germanic ​word, *hatōjan-

​- Modern ​Icelandic — ‌Hati, unchanged ​in spelling since ​the medieval ​manuscripts

Hati ‌is Tier ​2 because ​the restoration preserves ​nothing ASCII ‌cannot carry: ​no diacritic, ‌no thorn ​or eth, ‌no length mark. ​The name's ‌distinctiveness is ‌semantic, not ​orthographic — ​it is the ​verb 'to ​hate' given ‌legs and ‌set after ​the moon.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ​manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​Hati — ​Old Norse ‌(North ​Germanic), attested ​13th c. ‌CE in ​the Codex ‌Regius (Grímnismál) ​and the ‌Codex Regius ​of the ​Prose ​Edda (Gylfaginning), ​recording material ​centuries older.[1] ‌Before the ​Latin bookhand, ‌Old Norse ​names were ‌carved ​in the ‌Younger Futhark; ​no runic ‌attestation of ​this name ​survives, so ‌the manuscript ‌form is ‌the ​primary witness.

​The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds ​step by ​step:

  • ‌The name ​is the ​agent noun ‌hati ​'hater', formed ‌on the ​verb hata ​'to hate' ​— a ​weak-declension masculine, ‌hence the ‌final stem ​vowel ‌and the ​absence of ​the nominative ‌-r.
  • ‌In the ‌Younger Futhark ‌it would ​have been ‌carved ​ᚼᛅᛏᛁ (h-a-t-i), ​four runes ​with no ‌ambiguity.
  • ​Normalized Old ​Norse orthography ​writes no ‌diacritic ​in this ​name: the ​vowels are ‌short, and ​every letter ‌is plain ​ASCII.
  • ‌The Unicode ​restoration ‌Hati therefore ‌differs from ​the ASCII ​input only ​by capitalization ​— Tier ‌2 by ‌the mechanical ​rule.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. hati.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Hati is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌'Moon-Chaser, Chaos' ​— and ​the sources ​earn both terms ​of it.[1]

​### The ‌Moon-Chaser

His ​sole function ​in the ‌corpus is ​pursuit: Grímnismál sets ‌him running ​before the ‌sun, Snorri ​assigns him ​the moon ​as his quarry, ​and Völuspá's ​'snatcher of ‌the moon ​in a ‌troll's skin' ​is the role ‌the handbooks ​read as ‌his. The ​chase is ‌his whole ​mythology.

Chaos

​He belongs ‌to the ‌forces the ‌gods cannot ​bind: bred ​in the ‌Ironwood from Fenrir's ​own line, ​he is ‌one of ​the certainties ​of Ragnarǫk ‌— the catch ​Snorri says ‌simply 'will ​happen'. His ​chaos is ​not randomness ​but inevitability: order ‌pursued.

‌The Eclipse-Wolf

​The handbooks ‌read the ​chase as ​the sky's ‌own etiology: when ‌the wolf ‌draws close ‌to his ​quarry, the ‌moon darkens ​— the medieval ​North's explanation ​of the ‌lunar eclipse, ​and the ​reason the ​figure endures in ‌every account ​of the ​northern sky-myths.[2]

​### The ‌Fenrir-Complex

Hati ​never acts alone ‌in the ​tradition: he ‌is one ​face of ‌the apocalyptic ‌wolf-complex that ​also includes Skǫll, ​Mánagarmr, Garmr, ​and Fenrir ​himself — ‌the kennel ‌of the ​world's ending.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 12, trans. Faulkes (1987); Grímnismál 39 and Völuspá 39–40, trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Hati, Skǫll; Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sköll.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Hati ‌is narrative ​and celestial ​— ​the corpus ​gives ​him no ‌regalia, ​but the ​chase ‌itself fixed ​a small ‌set ​of images:[1]

‌- ​The running ​wolf ​— pursuit ​without ​rest: the ‌sky itself ​read ‌as a ​hunt ‌that has ​never ‌once paused
​- ‌The fleeing ​moon — ​Máni's ‌measured course ‌reinterpreted ‌as flight: ​what ​astronomy calls ‌a ​path, the ​poem calls ‌terror
​- Járnviðr, ​the ‌Ironwood — ​the ‌eastern forest ​where ​the old ​giantess breeds ​Fenrir's ‌brood: chaos ‌with ​an address, ‌growing ​older by ​the ‌year

  • ‌The darkened ‌sky ‌— Völuspá's ​'the ‌sunshine turns ​black ​in the ​summers ‌after': the ​eclipse and ​the ​world's ending ‌written ​in one ​image

​- The ‌troll's ​skin — ‌the seeress's ​phrase ‌for the ​moon-snatcher's ‌guise: the ‌wolf-shape ​worn as ​a ​monster's garment ​over a ‌name ‌the poem ​withholds[2]

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Hati's mythology is ​a single image carried by ‌a handful of verses: a ‌wolf, a moon, and a ​chase that ends only with ​the world.

The Pair ​Before Heaven (Grímnismál 39)

The ​name's one poetic attestation ​comes in Óðinn's great catalogue ‌of the cosmos in Grímnismál: ​'Skǫll is the name of ​the wolf who follows the ‌bright-faced god to the sheltering ​wood; but the other is ‌Hati, Hróðvitnir's son, who runs ​before the bright bride of ‌heaven.' The bright bride of ​heaven is Sól; the wolf ​who runs before her is ​on the heels of the ​moon that travels ahead of ​her. In four lines the ‌Edda converts the machinery ​of the sky into a ‌hunt.[1]

Snorri's System (Gylfaginning ​12)

Snorri systematizes the verse. ‌When Gangleri asks why the ​sun hurries so — 'she ‌speeds as if she were ​afraid' — High One answers ‌that the one who seeks ​her is right behind her. ​Two wolves do the work ‌of the heavens: Skǫll chases ‌the sun and will catch ‌her, and 'the other wolf ​is called Hati Hróðvitnisson; he ​runs in front of ‌her and wants to catch ​the moon — and that ​is what will happen.'[2]

‌Mánagarmr, the Moon-Hound

In the ​same chapter Snorri names the ​mightiest of the brood: Mánagarmr, ‌'moon-hound' — 'he will be ​filled with the flesh of ‌all who die, and he ​will swallow the moon and ​spatter the sky and all ​the air with blood.' Whether ​Mánagarmr is Hati under a ‌second name or a ‌third wolf beside the pair, ​the manuscripts leave open; Simek ‌and Lindow record the identification ​as probable but unprovable.[3]

​The Brood of the Ironwood ‌(Völuspá 39–40)

The seeress supplies ‌the kennel: 'In the east ‌sat the old one in ‌the Ironwood and bred there ​Fenrir's brood; from all of ‌them comes one in particular, ​a snatcher of the moon ​in a troll's skin. He ​gorges on the life of ‌doomed men, reddens the ​gods' dwellings with red blood; ​the sunshine turns black in ​the summers after.' The poem ‌names no name — but ​the tungls tjúgari, the 'snatcher ​of the moon', is the ​role Grímnismál and Snorri assign ‌to Hati.[1]

The Catch ​(Gylfaginning 51 · Völuspá)

The ‌ending is shared by every ​witness: when Ragnarǫk comes, the ‌running stops — Skǫll takes ​the sun, Hati takes the ‌moon, Fenrir swallows Óðinn, and ‌the bright stars vanish ​from the sky. The Edda's ​cosmology is built so that ​the nightly order of the ​heavens is only an interval: ‌the long meanwhile between the ‌start of the chase and ​its foreknown end.[2]

  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 12 and 51, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  3. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Mánagarm; Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sköll.

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Hati ​has no ‌cult, no ‌continental cognate, ​and no ​existence outside ​the Eddic ​record: he ​belongs to ‌the North's ​own eschatological ​machinery. Scholars ‌have long ​noted that ‌the doubling ​of the ‌apocalyptic wolf ​— one ​chasing ​the sun, ​one the ​moon — ‌is probably ​a secondary ‌systematization of ​the older ‌single figure, ​the bound ‌Fenrir who ​breaks loose ‌at the ​world's end; ​Simek and ‌Lindow treat ‌Skǫll and ‌Hati ​as elaborations ​within the ‌Fenrir-complex rather ​than as ​independent tradition, ‌and Garmr, ​the blood-caked ​hound of ‌Gnipahellir who ​bays loose ‌at Ragnarǫk, ​belongs to ​the same ​kennel.[1]

What ​the pair ‌does uniquely ‌well is ​etiology. ‌The devouring-monster ​account of ​eclipses is ‌a comparative ‌commonplace across ‌cultures; the ‌Norse version ​is distinctive ‌for giving ​its monsters ​names, a ​pedigree, and ‌a date. ​The chase ​gives the ​sky a ‌reason ​for its ​haste — ​Sól hurries ‌because Skǫll ​is behind ‌her — ​and the ‌wolf drawing ​near gives ‌the medieval ‌North its ​explanation of ​the eclipse: ​when the ​pursuer closes ‌on Máni, ‌the moon ​darkens.[2]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Hati's ​afterlife is ‌the ‌eclipse-wolf's: wherever ​the ​Ragnarǫk ​story is ​retold, ​the pair ‌Skǫll ​and Hati ​run ‌in the ​sky ‌above it. ​The ‌nineteenth-century ​myth handbooks ​that ​introduced Norse ​cosmology ​to English ‌readers ​made the ‌chase ​a set-piece ‌of ​mythological ‌explanation — ​the ‌sky's motions ​as ​a hunt, ‌the ‌eclipse as ‌the ​moment the ​wolf ‌gains ​— and ​every ‌modern retelling ​of ​Ragnarǫk, from ‌children's ​mythologies to ‌novels ​and games, ​keeps ​the ​two wolves ‌as ‌the ending's ​outriders.[1]

‌In scholarship ​the ​name is ‌the ‌standing example ‌of ‌the ​Edda's transparent ‌theonymy: ​a figure ​called, ​without ornament, ‌'the ​Hater'. Unlike ​Baldr ​or Freyja, ‌the ​name ​never entered ​the ‌living Scandinavian ​name-pool; ‌it survives ​where ‌it was ​born, ‌in the ‌poetry ​and ​its commentaries, ​doing ​the one ‌job ‌it was ​given.[2]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Hati ​is a ‌wholly textual ‌figure: no ​runestone names ​him, no ​votive ​deposit invokes ​him, and ‌no image ​can be ​securely assigned ‌to him.[1] ​Wolf ‌iconography is ​plentiful in ‌the Viking-Age ​record — ​the wolf-riders ​and ​great-wolf scenes ​on the ‌Gotland picture ​stones have ‌been argued ​over ‌since Lindqvist, ​and Úlfr ‌Uggason's Húsdrápa ​(c. 983) ‌proves that ​wolf-mounted ​figures of ‌myth were ‌carved in ‌pre-Christian halls ​— but ​no carving ‌carries ​an inscription ​that ties ‌a wolf ​to the ​moon-chase. The ‌stones ​are witnesses ‌to a ​world that ​knew wolves ​of myth, ​not ‌to this ‌wolf.[2]

The ​name's true ‌witnesses are ​the manuscripts: ​Grímnismál in ‌the ‌Codex Regius ‌(c. 1270) ‌and AM ​748 I ‌4to, Völuspá ​in ​the Codex ​Regius and ‌Hauksbók, and ​Gylfaginning in ​the Codex ​Regius ‌of the ​Prose Edda ​and its ​sister codices, ‌all recording ​material ‌centuries older. ​The þulur ‌of Skáldskaparmál ​preserve Hati ‌and Hróðvitnir ‌among the ​poetic ​heiti for ​'wolf' — ​the tradition's ‌own file ‌of the ​name.[3]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda knows ‌Hati by ‌exactly one ​verse, and it ​is enough. ​Grímnismál 39, ​in Óðinn's catalogue ​of the ‌cosmos, names ​the two ​wolves of the ‌sky: Skǫll ​follows the ‌bright-faced god to ​the sheltering ‌wood, 'but ​the other ​is Hati, Hróðvitnir's ​son, who ​runs before ​the bright bride ‌of heaven' ​— the ‌wolf ahead ​of the sun, ‌on the ​heels of ‌the moon.[1] The ​surrounding stanzas ‌supply the ​chase's context: ​Sól's horses Árvakr ‌and Alsviðr, ‌the shield ‌Svalinn held before ​the sun, ​and the ‌whole machinery ​of a heaven ​in motion.

‌Völuspá contributes ​the brood and ​the ending. ‌Its seeress ​sees the ‌old giantess in ​the Ironwood ​breeding Fenrir's ​kind, 'and from ​all of ‌them comes ‌one in ​particular, a snatcher ‌of the ​moon in ​a troll's skin', ‌who gorges ‌on the ‌doomed and ‌reddens the gods' ​dwellings — ‌the tungls ​tjúgari the handbooks ​read as ​Hati in ‌his Ragnarǫk ​guise.[2] The same ​poem's eschatology ​supplies the ‌frame: the sun ​turning black ​and the ​stars vanishing ‌are the catch ​made visible. ‌Vafþrúðnismál adds ​the moon's own ‌pedigree — ​Máni, Mundilfœri's ‌son, who ‌'journeys every day' ​and counts ​the years ​for men: the ​fleeing light ‌the wolf ‌was bred ​to follow.[3]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 37–39 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Völuspá 39–40 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Vafþrúðnismál 23 (Mundilfœri's children) (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌Hati's systematizer. Gylfaginning ‌builds the ​sky first — ​Mundilfari's children ​Sól and Máni ​set to ​drive the horses ‌of the ​sun and moon ​— and then, ‌when Gangleri ​remarks that the ‌sun hurries ​as if afraid, ‌High One ​springs the trap ​the whole ​cosmology was set ​to hold: ​'the one who ‌seeks her ​is right behind ‌her, and ​she has no ‌other way ​out than to ‌run'. Two ​wolves are named: ‌Skǫll, who ​will take the ​sun, and ‌'Hati Hróðvitnisson; he ‌runs in front ‌of her ​and wants to ​catch the ‌moon — and ​that is ​what will happen'.[1]

‌Snorri's craft ​deserves note: he ​takes Grímnismál's ‌compressed verse — ​the wolf ‌'before the bright ​bride of ​heaven' — and ​reads its ​geometry, setting the ‌moon-chaser ahead ‌of the sun ​because the ‌moon travels ahead ​of her. In ​the same ‌chapter he adds ‌Mánagarmr, the ‌'moon-hound', mightiest of ‌the brood, ​who will swallow ‌the moon ​and spatter the ​sky with ​blood; whether this ‌is Hati ​renamed or a ​third wolf, ​the tradition leaves ‌open.[1] Gylfaginning ​51 pays the ​debt: at ​Ragnarǫk the wolves' ‌running ends ​in the catches ‌foretold, and ​the sky goes ‌dark.[2] Elsewhere the ​Prose Edda ‌keeps the name ‌filed where ​skalds could find ​it: the ​þulur of Skáldskaparmál ​list Hati ‌and Hróðvitnir among ‌the heiti ​for 'wolf'.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 12, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 51, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur (wolf-heiti), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of ​the wolf ​Hati. ​No runestone ​names ‌him, no ​amulet ​invokes him, ‌and ​no inscription ‌yet ​found links ‌a ​carving to ​the ​moon-chase — ​a ​silence he ‌shares ​with the ‌whole ​Fenrir-complex, whose ‌figures ​belong to ‌poetry ​and prophecy ‌rather ​than to ​dedicatory ‌practice.[1]

What ‌the ‌runic record ​offers ​is orthographic, ‌not ​cultic. In ​the ‌Younger Futhark ​of ​the Viking ‌Age ​the name ‌would ​have been ​carved ​ᚼᛅᛏᛁ — ​h-a-t-i, ‌four runes ‌with ​none of ‌the ​underspecification that ​complicates ‌longer names; ‌the ‌rendering is ‌a ​normalized reconstruction, ‌since ​no Viking-Age ​carving ​of the ‌name ​is known. ​The ​etymological raw ‌material ​is equally ​plain: ​the verb ‌hata, ​'to hate', ‌is ​common Germanic ‌vocabulary, ​and the ‌agent ‌noun hati ​needs ​no poetic ​license ​to mean ‌what ‌the myth ​requires.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Sköll, Fenrir.
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. hata; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. hati.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌Hati has ‌no medieval ​narrative ​afterlife. The ​legendary sagas ​and the ​kings' ‌sagas never ​stage him; ​no fornaldarsaga ‌hero ​meets the ‌moon-wolf, and ​Saxo Grammaticus, ‌who ​retells so ​much of ​the gods' ​history ​as Danish ‌prehistory, gives ​no corresponding ‌figure. ​His medieval ‌existence outside ​the mythological ‌poems ​is that ‌of a ​learned word: ​the ‌þulur — ‌the metrical ‌name-lists the ​skalds ​used as ‌a thesaurus ​— preserve ​Hati ‌and Hróðvitnir ​among the ​heiti for ‌'wolf', ​which means ‌the name ​circulated as ​raw ​material for ​verse long ‌after its ‌myth ​was fixed.[1]

‌That afterlife ​is instructive. ​A ‌skald composing ‌a wolf-kenning ‌could reach ‌for ​'Hati' and ‌count on ​his audience ​hearing ​both the ‌animal and ​the chase ​after ​Máni; the ‌theonym and ​the common ​noun ​folded into ‌one another, ​exactly as ‌the ​myth intended. ‌The name's ​whole career ‌— ‌poem, prose ​systematization, þula ​— never ​left ​the written ‌tradition, and ‌never needed ​to.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál, þulur (wolf-heiti), ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Hati.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Hati ​is the ‌Edda's ‌most economical ​idea: ​take the ​verb 'to ​hate', ​give it ‌legs, ​and set ​it ‌running after ​the ‌light. There ​is ‌no malice-scene, ​no dialogue, ​no ​character beyond ​the ​name — ‌only ​pursuit. And ‌that ​is the ‌point. ​The Norse ‌imagination looked ​at ‌the most ​orderly ​thing in ‌the ‌world, the ‌clockwork ​of sun ​and ‌moon, and ​read it ​as ‌flight: the ​lights ​hurry because ‌something ​hates them ‌and ​is coming.[1]

​Time ​itself becomes ​the distance ‌still ‌left between ​the ‌wolf and ​the ​moon — ‌every ‌night Máni ‌escapes ‌is a ​night the ‌world ​continues. Hati ​asks ​the uncomfortable ‌question ​the whole ​Ragnarǫk ​cycle asks: ‌how ​do you ​keep the ​light ‌moving, knowing ​exactly ‌what follows ​it? ‌The Edda's ​answer ‌is the ‌one ​it gives ​everywhere. You ​keep ​running. The ‌chase ‌is the ​world.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 12, trans. Faulkes (1987); Völuspá 39–40, trans. Larrington (2014).
