The name Bunjil reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the aboriginal tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Bunjil in Later Traditions

Bunjil is not a solitary deity but one half of a Kulin social and cosmological pair. The eaglehawk moiety, headed by Bunjil, is balanced by the crow moiety, headed by Waang — a pairing that structures kinship, marriage, and ritual obligation across the Kulin nations, so that every person belongs to one ancestor's side and marries into the other. Different clans preserve different episodes: the Woiwurrung stress the sky ascent and Altair, the Boonwurrung remember the rising sea, and the peoples of Gariweld keep the place-based accounts tied to Bunjil's Shelter. In the colonial period the name was recorded under many spellings — Winjeel, Wingeel, Pundjel, Bunjel — reflecting dialect variation and the difficulty of rendering Kulin phonology in English orthography; several survive in Victorian place names.

Modern Legacy

Bunjil remains a powerful and public symbol of Aboriginal Victoria. Bunjil's Shelter in the Black Range Scenic Reserve is a protected cultural site and a focus of education and careful tourism. Melbourne's skyline carries his name and form: Bruce Armstrong's monumental eagle sculpture Bunjil (2002) rises from Wurundjeri Way in the Docklands, and Bunjil Place in Narre Warren carries the name into the suburbs. For the Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung, Boonwurrung, and other Kulin communities, Bunjil is not only an ancestral figure but a living authority in Welcome to Country ceremonies, land-care work, language revival, and the ongoing negotiation of recognition and reconciliation — the creator whose law still describes how Country should be held.

In the Earth

The principal material site associated with Bunjil is Bunjil's Shelter, a rock-art shelter in the Black Range Scenic Reserve within Gariwerd (the Grampians region), Victoria, where a red-ochre painting depicts the creator figure flanked by two dingoes. The site is protected under the Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006 and is managed with Traditional Owner input. Textual witnesses include A. W. Howitt's ethnographic notes, Aldo Massola's 1968 compilation of south-eastern traditions, and contemporary community publications by Kulin educators and language custodians — together forming an unusually well-documented record of a living tradition.

The Meditation

What Bunjil means — beyond the dictionary

Bunjil teaches that creation is not a single event but a continuing responsibility. He did not make the land and depart; he shaped it, gave it law, and then withdrew into the sky so that he could go on watching. The eaglehawk's eye is still understood to be turned toward Country.

For non-Indigenous visitors, Bunjil is an invitation to understand 'Country' not as real estate but as a living inheritance — a place that includes people, animals, plants, water, sky, and story, all held together by obligations that precede the colony by tens of thousands of years. The name Bunjil, even in its simple Latin transcription, carries that whole relationship. To restore the capital is a small act; to remember what it names is the larger one — and the first of the obligations it describes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Bunjil, Creator Eaglehawk, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Bunjil?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Bunjil is /ˈbʊn.dʒɪl/ — approximately 'BUN-jil' — stress the first syllable; the 'j' is the 'j' of 'jump', and the final vowel is short like the 'i' in 'kit'.

02What does Bunjil mean?

Bunjil means The eaglehawk in the aboriginal tradition.

03What are the symbols of Bunjil?

Bunjil is associated with Wedge-tailed eagle (The living form of Bunjil and the largest bird of prey in Australia; a sign of creative power and watchful authority), Spear (The instrument with which Bunjil commanded the rising sea to stop, and a mark of law-giving authority), Black swan (Bunjil's two wives, who became the stars flanking Altair after his ascent), Rainbow (His son Binbeal, the rainbow spirit, who continues the creative lineage in the sky).

04Why restore Bunjil in Unicode?

Bunjil is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Bunjil?

“Shaping the Land and the Law” — Bunjil's stories belong to the Dreaming of the Kulin nations — the Woiwurrung, Boonwurrung, Wathaurong, and their neighbors — recorded by A. W. Howitt, Aldo Massola, and the communities themselves. They explain how Country was made, how law was given, and how the creator withdrew into the sky so that he might continue to watch over the world. The full telling lives on the lore page.
