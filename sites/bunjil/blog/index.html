Overview

Bunjil (bunjil) — Creator Eaglehawk — belongs to the Aboriginal traditions of the Kulin nations in central Victoria, Australia. The name ​means 'the eaglehawk' and refers to the wedge-tailed eagle ancestor who shaped Country, gave law, and then withdrew into the sky.

In Kulin cosmology Bunjil is one of two moiety ancestors, paired ​with Waang the crow. The stories credit him with forming the land's surface, establishing social and ecological obligations, and finally ascending to become the star Altair. His wives became the black swans beside ​him, and his son Binbeal is the rainbow.

PuniCodex restores the name as Bunjil. The plain ASCII form bunjil is a technological compromise required by the domain-name system: it drops only the ​capital initial that marks a proper name in English scholarly citation. The Name

The name Bunjil is a theonym recorded from the Woiwurrung, Boonwurrung, and related Kulin ​languages of central Victoria. It denotes the wedge-tailed eaglehawk as creator ancestor. Because these languages were — and in revitalisation efforts continue to be — oral traditions, there is no pre-colonial alphabetic original ​script. The name survives in Latin transcriptions made by early settlers, missionaries, and anthropologists.

Etymology

The name Bunjil is attested as ​a theonym in the Kulin languages of central Victoria, particularly Woiwurrung and Boonwurrung. It refers to the wedge-tailed eaglehawk (Aquila audax) in its ancestral, creative aspect. No reconstructed Proto-Pama–Nyungan form can be offered ‌with confidence; the word is recorded from the specific languages in which Bunjil's cult was central.

Early colonial collectors produced a range of spellings — Winjeel, Wingeel, Pundjel, Bunjel, Punjel — as ​they attempted to write Aboriginal sounds with English letters. Modern Kulin communities and Victorian institutions generally prefer Bunjil or Bundjil.

Because the source languages were oral, the 'original script' question does not arise ​in the usual sense. PuniCodex treats the capitalised Latin transcription Bunjil as the scholarly citation form and the lowercase bunjil as the DNS compromise.

Pronunciation

The conventional scholarly reading of ​the name is /ˈbʊn.dʒɪl/ — English transcription of the Kulin theonym.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'BUN-jil' — stress the first syllable, pronounce the 'j' as in 'jump', and keep ‌the final vowel short like the 'i' in 'kit'.

Because the ASCII form bunjil already encodes the same conventional spelling, the restoration Bunjil does not add a stress mark, length mark, or ​distinctive letter. Mythology

Bunjil's stories belong to the Dreaming of the Kulin nations, especially the Woiwurrung, Boonwurrung, and Wathaurong peoples. They explain how Country was made, how law was given, and how the ‌creator withdrew into the sky so that he might continue to watch over the world.

Shaping the Land and the Law (Creation)

In the Dreaming, Bunjil travelled across what is now central ‌Victoria. He formed the mountains, traced the rivers, named the plants and animals, and laid down the laws that govern how people relate to one another and to Country. These laws are not ‌separate from the land; they are remembered in place names, songlines, and the seasonal responsibilities of each clan. (Massola 1968; Howitt 1904)

The Sea That Would Not Stop Rising (Deluge)

A Boonwurrung ​story tells of a time when the Kulin nations fell into conflict, neglecting family and Country. The sea rose in anger and threatened to flood the land. The people went to Bunjil and ​asked him to intervene. Bunjil agreed, but only if the people would change their ways and respect the law. He walked to the water's edge, raised his spear, and commanded the sea ‌to stop. It did. (Briggs, 'Boon Wurrung Story')

Lifted into the Sky (Ascent)

When his work on earth was finished, Bunjil gathered his family and asked Waang, who kept the winds in skin ​bags, to release enough wind to carry them to the sky. Waang opened the whirlwind bag, and the wind lifted Bunjil and his people upward. Bunjil became the bright star Altair; his ​two wives, the black swans, became the stars on either side. Some accounts say he left this world from the island of Deen Maar. (Howitt 1904; Mudrooroo 1994)

Bunjil's Shelter (Sacred site)

In ‌Gariwerd, the country now known as the Grampians, Bunjil took shelter in a cave in the Black Range. The rock-art site known as Bunjil's Shelter is one of the most important Aboriginal ​art places in Victoria and remains under the care of Traditional Owners. It is not merely a memory of the ancestor; it is a place where his presence is understood to remain. (Parks ​Victoria 2009)

Symbols & Iconography

  • Wedge-tailed eagle — Bunjil's living form and the largest bird of prey in Australia; a sign of creative authority and watchfulness.
  • Spear — the implement with which ‌he commanded the rising sea to stop; also a sign of law-giving power.
  • Black swan — Bunjil's two wives, who became the stars flanking Altair after his ascent.
  • Rainbow — his son Binbeal, ​the rainbow spirit.
  • Whirlwind / wind bag — the means of his ascent to the sky, released by Waang or Bellin-Bellin.

Archaeology & Evidence

The principal material site associated with Bunjil is ‌Bunjil's Shelter, a rock-art shelter in the Black Range Scenic Reserve within Gariwerd (Grampians National Park region), Victoria. The site is protected under the Aboriginal Heritage Act 2006 and is managed with Traditional ​Owner input. Textual witnesses include A. W. Howitt's ethnographic notes (AIATSIS MS 69), Aldo Massola's 1968 compilation, and contemporary community publications by Kulin educators and language custodians.

Realm & Domain

Bunjil's domain ​is creation itself, conceived not as a single moment but as an ongoing relationship with Country. In Kulin thought he is the wedge-tailed eaglehawk who shaped the physical world, established law, and then ​took his place in the sky as a continuing watcher.

His influence extends across several registers. As Creator of Country he is responsible for landforms, water, plants, and animals. As Law-Giver he ​established the obligations that bind people to one another and to the land. As Sky Ancestor he provides a celestial point of reference, identified with the star Altair. As Moiety Founder he heads ‌the eaglehawk half of the Kulin social order, balanced by Waang the crow.

These domains are not abstract categories; they are lived in the seasonal calendar, the songlines, the rock-art site of ‌Bunjil's Shelter, and the contemporary cultural and environmental work of Kulin communities.

Across Cultures

Bunjil is not a solitary deity but one half of a Kulin social and cosmological pair. The eaglehawk moiety, ​headed by Bunjil, is balanced by the crow moiety, headed by Waang. This pairing structures kinship, marriage, and ritual obligations across the Kulin nations. Different clans emphasise different episodes — the Woiwurrung ‌stress the sky ascent and Altair, the Boonwurrung remember the rising-sea story, and the Wathaurong preserve place-based accounts tied to the coast and to Bunjil's Shelter. In the colonial period the name was ​recorded under many spellings — Winjeel, Wingeel, Pundjel, Bunjel — reflecting dialect variation and the difficulty of rendering Aboriginal phonology in English orthography.

Cultural Legacy

Bunjil remains a powerful symbol of Aboriginal Victoria. Bunjil's Shelter in the Black Range Scenic Reserve is a ‌protected cultural site and a focus of education and tourism. Public art, including the giant sculpture Eagle in Melbourne, takes Bunjil as its inspiration. For the Wurundjeri, Boonwurrung, and other Kulin communities, Bunjil ‌is not only an ancestral figure but a living authority in discussions of land care, cultural identity, and reconciliation.

A Meditation

Bunjil teaches that creation is not a single event but a continuing responsibility. He did not make the land and depart; he shaped it, gave it law, and ​then withdrew into the sky so that he could go on watching. The eaglehawk's eye is still understood to be turned toward Country.

For non-Indigenous visitors, Bunjil is an invitation to understand 'Country' ​not as real estate but as a living inheritance — a place that includes people, animals, plants, water, sky, and story, all held together by obligations that precede the colony. The name ​Bunjil, even in its simple Latin transcription, carries that whole relationship. To restore the capital is a small act; to remember what it names is the larger one.
