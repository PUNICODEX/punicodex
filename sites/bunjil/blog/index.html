How Bunjil got its capital back

The ASCII form bunjil looks like any other lowercase domain string. But Bunjil is not any other name: it is the creator eaglehawk of the Kulin nations of central Victoria, the ancestor who shaped Country, gave law, and then withdrew into the sky as the star Altair. This post explains why the Unicode restoration capitalises the first letter, what that capital letter preserves, and how a Tier 2 classification still honours a figure of immense cultural depth.

At a Glance

  • Restored name: Bunjil
  • ASCII form: bunjil
  • Meaning: "The eaglehawk"
  • Domain of influence: Creator, law-giver, sky ancestor, moiety founder
  • Pantheon: Australian Aboriginal (Kulin nations)
  • Classification: Tier 2
  • Original script: None — the Kulin languages were traditionally oral; the name survives in Latin transcription

Overview

Bunjil (bunjil) — Creator Eaglehawk — belongs to the Aboriginal traditions of the Kulin nations. In the Dreaming he shaped the land, water, plants, and animals of central Victoria and established the laws that govern relations between people and Country. He is one of two moiety ancestors, paired with Waang the crow. After his creative work was complete, he ascended to the sky, where he is identified with the bright star Altair; his two wives became the black swans beside him, and his son Binbeal is the rainbow.

PuniCodex restores the name as Bunjil. The plain ASCII form bunjil is a technological compromise: the early domain-name system could not carry meaning, only letters, and it forced every name into lowercase. The restoration adds back the capital initial that English and Australian scholarly convention uses for a proper name. Because no diacritic or special letter is needed to recover the conventional spelling, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference; the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for DNS.

The Name

The name is attested in the Woiwurrung, Boonwurrung, and related Kulin languages of central Victoria. It refers to the wedge-tailed eaglehawk (Aquila audax) in its ancestral, creative aspect. Because these languages were — and in revitalisation efforts continue to be — oral traditions, there is no pre-colonial alphabetic original script. The name survives in the Latin transcriptions made by early settlers and anthropologists.

The PuniCodex restoration Bunjil simply applies the standard scholarly convention of capitalising a proper name. The ASCII form bunjil is therefore not an older or more authentic spelling; it is the domain-name system stripping away the visual signal that this is a name. The spoken name and the mythic role remain unchanged.

Pronunciation and Classification

The conventional scholarly reading is /ˈbʊn.dʒɪl/ — stress the first syllable, pronounce the 'j' as in 'jump', and keep the final vowel short.

Bunjil is Tier 2 because the ASCII form bunjil already contains the conventional spelling. No stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the name. The restoration is a typographic rather than a phonemic recovery: it restores the capital that marks a proper name, not a sound that would otherwise be lost.

Myths and Symbols

Bunjil's stories are preserved in the Dreaming narratives of the Kulin nations. A Woiwurrung account tells how Bunjil shaped the land and then asked Waang, keeper of the winds, to release a whirlwind that carried him and his family into the sky. A Boonwurrung story tells of a time when the sea rose in anger at human conflict; Bunjil stopped the flood only after the people agreed to live by the law. The rock-art site known as Bunjil's Shelter, in Gariwerd, remains one of the most important Aboriginal art places in Victoria.

His symbols are drawn from the living world: the wedge-tailed eagle, the spear of authority, the black swans that were his wives, the rainbow that is his son Binbeal, and the whirlwind that lifted him to the sky.

Why the Capital Matters

For a name like Bunjil, the difference between bunjil and Bunjil can look trivial. It is not. The capital letter is the only visual cue, in plain Latin text, that we are looking at a proper name rather than a common noun. In a project devoted to restoring the scholarly citation forms of names, that cue is evidence. The capital does not add sound, but it adds recognition: it says that this string refers to a specific ancestral person, not to an eaglehawk in general.

The restoration also makes explicit the cultural asymmetry built into DNS. The domain-name system was designed for ASCII and for lowercase; it cannot preserve the capitalisation, the diacritics, or the non-Latin scripts that many names require. Bunjil's restoration is a small repair of that asymmetry.

The Meaning Behind the Name

The name Bunjil encodes more than a simple label. In the Aboriginal tradition that holds it, the name carries the history of the country, the authority of ancestral law, and the obligations of the living custodians. The plain ASCII form bunjil is a modern technological convenience: it allows the name to travel through the domain-name system and other digital infrastructure, but it cannot represent the full cultural weight that the name carries in ceremony and song.

PuniCodex restores the name as Bunjil because the capitalized scholarly form is the standard way to cite a proper noun from an oral tradition. This restoration is not a cosmetic change. It marks the name as belonging to a living culture and asserts that digital infrastructure should be able to honor such names rather than flatten them into lowercase ASCII strings.

Mythology and Worldview

The stories of Bunjil are not entertainment or simple explanations of natural phenomena. They are title deeds to country, instructions for ceremony, and maps of moral responsibility. In the accounts recorded by anthropologists and preserved by custodians, Bunjil appears as a figure whose actions shaped the land and established the patterns that human beings must follow.

These stories are held under traditional protocols. Some details are appropriate for public discussion, while others are restricted to initiated members of the community or to specific kin groups. The public scholarly record therefore gives only the outer layer of a much deeper body of knowledge. Even so, it is enough to show that Bunjil is not a static mythological curiosity but a living presence in Aboriginal law and identity.

Cultural Significance Today

In contemporary Australia, Bunjil continues to appear in Aboriginal art, dance, environmental management, and public ceremony. Indigenous artists depict the figure in bark paintings, rock-art renewals, and digital media, always in ways that respect the custodial protocols of the communities that hold the name. Welcome-to-country ceremonies, language-revival programs, and land-care initiatives all draw on the authority of ancestral figures such as Bunjil.

At the same time, the name reminds us that the digital world was built on assumptions that privilege ASCII text. Domain names, file systems, search engines, and social-media handles all tend to treat lowercase English letters as the default. Restoring Bunjil as a flagship temple is a small but concrete step toward challenging that default and making space for Indigenous names in the digital realm.

Why This Restoration Matters

PuniCodex classifies Bunjil as a Tier 2 restoration. This means that the ASCII form bunjil already represents the same phonemic and orthographic sequence as the restored form; no diacritic or special letter is required to recover the scholarly spelling. The restoration therefore focuses on the proper-noun convention: the capitalized form Bunjil is the respectful citation form, while bunjil remains a practical compromise for digital systems that cannot yet honor Indigenous typographic conventions.

Even without a registered Unicode domain, Bunjil deserves a flagship temple. The domain-name market may make the exact string unavailable, but the name itself remains a vital part of human heritage. By building a temple around it, PuniCodex preserves the scholarly form, explains its significance, and keeps watch for the day when the digital infrastructure of names can fully include the world's oral traditions.

A Note on Sources and Respect

The scholarly record on Bunjil draws on nineteenth- and twentieth-century ethnography, more recent anthropological fieldwork, and the guidance of Aboriginal custodians and institutions such as AIATSIS. Early colonial accounts must be read critically: they often imposed European religious categories on Aboriginal beliefs and ignored the protocols that govern the transmission of sacred knowledge. Contemporary presentations of Bunjil therefore prioritize Indigenous authorship and custodial authority.

Readers who wish to learn more should seek out resources produced by the Aboriginal nations that hold the name, as well as scholarly works that cite Indigenous collaborators. The PuniCodex temple is offered as a starting point for that journey, not as the final word on a living tradition.

Related Names

The Aboriginal traditions of Australia hold many figures whose stories overlap or contrast with Bunjil. Sky beings, ancestral creators, and totemic ancestors appear in different regional forms, and the same figure may be known by more than one name or spelling across language groups. Bunjil belongs to a network of names that includes Baiame, the Kamilaroi sky father; Bunjil, the Kulin eaglehawk creator; Ngalyod, the rainbow serpent of the north; and Yurlungur, the great copper python of Yolŋu country. Each name encodes a specific relationship to country, law, and custodial responsibility, and none is interchangeable with another.

For researchers and readers, the important distinction is between the capitalized scholarly citation form Bunjil and the lowercase ASCII form bunjil. The capitalized form marks the name as a proper noun and situates it within the scholarly and custodial discourse of Aboriginal studies. The lowercase form is a technological artifact, useful for domain names and search queries but carrying none of the cultural weight of the citation form.
