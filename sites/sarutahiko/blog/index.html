Overview

Sarutahiko (sarutahiko) — Crossroads, ​Guidance — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "Monkey-field prince".

Sarutahiko is the kami of crossroads and guidance who leads Ninigi's descent from heaven.

PuniCodex restores the name as Sarutahiko. The ​plain ASCII form sarutahiko is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in the ​basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is treated as a plain form. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, while ​the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 猿田彦. Etymologically it means "Monkey-field prince".

Sarutahiko means "monkey-field prince," from ‌saru (monkey), ta (rice field), and hiko (prince/lad). The name may refer to a place or to the monkey as a messenger animal. He is the kami who stands at the boundary ​between worlds and guides those who cross it.

The ASCII form sarutahiko survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ​ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Sarutahiko gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 猿田彦 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested ​in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Sarutahiko (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //sa.ɾɯ.ta.çi.ko//.

‌The characters 猿田彦 are semantic: 猿 (monkey), 田 (rice field), 彦 (prince/lad). The name describes him as a local earth kami of the fields, later elevated to a guide of the heavenly ​descent.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /sa.ɾɯ.ta.çi.ko/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'SARUTAHIKO' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English ​diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Sarutahiko is a simpler restoration because the ASCII form sarutahiko already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, ​or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short syllable receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as separate ​morae. The name Sarutahiko therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English speaker stresses one syllable heavily. The Hepburn romanization used here records the modern standard pronunciation; it ​does not preserve the historical pitch accent of the word, which differs across dialects. Mythology

​When Ninigi prepared to descend from heaven, Amaterasu sent Sarutahiko to guide him. At the Ame-no-ukihashi bridge Sarutahiko blocked the way with his great size and fierce demeanour. The goddess Ame-no-Uzume went ‌forward, pacified him, and persuaded him to lead the descent. In gratitude he married Ame-no-Uzume and became the ancestor of the Sarume clan of ritual dancers.

The narrative of Sarutahiko participates in the ​larger mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because ‌these chronicles were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of ​sarutahiko illustrates how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

The Blockade at the Floating Bridge (Kojiki / Nihon Shoki)

‌When Ninigi no Mikoto, grandson of Amaterasu, prepared to descend from Takamagahara to rule the Central Land of Reed-Plains, an awesome figure stood at the eight-fold crossroads at the earthward end of ​the Floating Bridge of Heaven. The Kojiki describes Sarutahiko with a long nose, a red and shining face, eyes like eight-span mirrors, and a beard that hung down; the Nihon Shoki variant measures ‌him more exactly — a nose of seven hand-spans, a height over seven feet, eyes red like mirrors — and says the heavenly envoys sent to negotiate all stood back in fear. ​Only Ame-no-Uzume, goddess of dawn, mirth, and dance, stepped forward. She spoke to him boldly and well, and the gatekeeper was won: he asked who was coming and offered himself as guide. ​No force moved him and no god outranked him; what opened the road was speech. The myth's meaning is a charter of legitimacy: heaven's claim to the land passes through an earthly ‌gatekeeper's consent — rule is negotiated at the threshold, not imposed from the sky.

The Marriage That Founded a Line (Kojiki)

After the descent was accomplished, Ame-no-Uzume married Sarutahiko, and the Kojiki ‌draws the genealogical consequence: Uzume is the ancestress of the Sarume — the “monkey-women” — the order of female ritual dancers of the Yamato court, whose sacred performances are the root from which ‌kagura, and in part the comic spirit of Noh, descend. The marriage folds the unruly leader of the earthly kami into the celestial order without domesticating him: he remains the red-faced giant, ​she remains the dancer who out-talked him, and together they become the founding couple of Japanese sacred performance. Structurally the pairing is exact — the goddess who moves between worlds weds the ​god who guards the crossing — and politically it is shrewd: the oldest indigenous power of the archipelago is not defeated by Ninigi's line but married into it. The myth remembers that ‌Japan's ritual arts were born at a boundary, from a negotiation.

The Drowning at Azaka (Kojiki)

Even gatekeepers die. The Kojiki records that Sarutahiko went to Ise, to the Isuzu River at ​Azaka, where a giant clam closed upon his hand while he fished; he fell into the water and drowned. The tradition speaks the event in three divine names given to his spirit as ​he sank: Sokodokumitama, “Bottom-Reach-Spirit,” as he went to the riverbed; Tsubutatsumitama, “Rising-Bubble Spirit,” as his breath broke the surface; and Awasakumitama, “Bubble-Bursting Spirit,” as the bubbles burst and were gone. Anthropologist Emiko ‌Ohnuki-Tierney reads the episode through the monkey symbolism latent in his name — the macaque with one hand trapped in a shell is a stock figure of Japanese folktale. The undertone of ​the myth is initiatory: the god of boundaries meets his own limit at the water's edge, and in the telling of his death the river at Ise gains a guardian presence that ​outlasts the god's body.

Archaeology & Evidence

Sarutahiko is attested textually in the Kojiki ‌and Nihon Shoki and iconographically in shrine statuary and ema. Tsubaki Grand Shrine preserves one of the principal cult centres dedicated to him.

The material record associated with Sarutahiko must be read ​alongside the textual traditions; shrines, ritual objects, and landscape features preserve practices that the chronicles only summarize. Excavations and architectural history therefore offer independent evidence for how devotion to sarutahiko developed across ​periods, even when written sources present a single mythic moment as eternal.

Realm & Domain

Sarutahiko is the kami of crossroads, thresholds, and guidance. He stands at the boundary between the human ​world and the realm of the kami, ensuring safe passage and correct direction. He is the patron of travellers, immigrants, and those beginning new ventures.

The domain of Sarutahiko is understood within the ​broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, buddhas, and local spirits. Shrines, temples, and household rites all invoke the figure in ‌ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Sarutahiko therefore names not only a mythic person but a living field of ritual activity: pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer ‌continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. This combinatory or "syncretic" context is essential for interpreting the domains listed above.

Across Cultures

‌Sarutahiko is associated with Ame-no-Uzume and with the kagura dance. His long nose and red face connect him to the tengu and to mountain ascetics. He is also a patron of immigrants and ​of those crossing boundaries, both physical and social.

The combinatory religious culture of Japan means that Sarutahiko was rarely understood in isolation. Medieval honji-suijaku theory, imperial ritual, and popular devotion continually redefined ​the figure in relation to Buddhist, Daoist, and local cults. These later reinterpretations are as historically significant as the original mythic portrait, because they show how a name accumulates meanings across communities ‌and centuries.

Cultural Legacy

Sarutahiko is venerated at Tsubaki Grand Shrine in Mie and at many crossroads shrines. He appears in the Aikido tradition as a model of correct orientation and in ‌popular culture as the long-nosed kami who guides travellers.

Today Sarutahiko continues to appear in shrine pilgrimage, festival observance, anime, manga, video games, and scholarly discourse. The name functions as both a ‌religious referent and a cultural symbol, a flexibility that has allowed it to survive the formal separation of Shinto and Buddhism and the secularisation of modern Japan. Restoring the conventional Hepburn romanization keeps ‌the scholarly citation intact while acknowledging the living diversity of its meanings.
