Overview

Sarutahiko (sarutahiko) — Crossroads, Guidance · Monkey-field prince — belongs to the Japanese tradition, where it is ​catalogued under the domain "Crossroads, Guidance". The name means "Monkey-field prince".

Sarutahiko is the kami who stands at the boundary between heaven and earth. When Ninigi descended from Takamagahara, Sarutahiko blocked the ​way until Ame-no-Uzume persuaded him to become the guide. With his long nose, glaring eyes, and red face, he became the model for the taikomochi and tenjin figures of Japanese folk performance ​and the patron of those who stand at thresholds.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /sa.ɾɯ.ta.hi.ko/ — Modern ‌Standard Japanese (Hepburn).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Sa- — Voiceless alveolar fricative [s] plus open central [a]
  • -ru- — Alveolar tap [ɾ] plus close back unrounded [ɯ]
  • -ta- — Voiceless alveolar stop [t] plus ​open central [a]; from 田 'field'
  • -hiko — Voiceless glottal [h], close front [i], voiceless velar [k], open-mid [o]; from 彦 'prince/lad'

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'SAH-roo-tah-hee-koh' — ‌the 'r' is a light tap, and all vowels are short.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Japanese — 猿田彦 (Sarutahiko) — saru 'monkey' + ta 'field' + hiko 'prince'; the ​saruta element is etymologically obscure (Ashkenazi, Handbook of Japanese Mythology).
  • Wife — Ame-no-Uzume-no-mikoto, the dancing goddess; the Kojiki makes her the ancestress of the Sarume.
  • Descendants — The Sarume (猿女, 'monkey-women') clan of ​female ritual dancers, from whom the kagura tradition descends (Kojiki).

Mythology

Sarutahiko's ​mythology is compact but complete in its own way: the earth-born kami who blocks the heavenly descent and becomes its guide; the husband of the dancing goddess; a drowned god whose spirit ​is still named at Ise. Around these Kojiki and Nihon Shoki episodes, folk religion built a vast afterlife of crossroads, masks, and martial arts.

The Blockade at the Floating Bridge (Kojiki / Nihon ​Shoki)

When Ninigi no Mikoto, grandson of Amaterasu, prepared to descend from Takamagahara to rule the Central Land of Reed-Plains, an awesome figure stood at the eight-fold crossroads at the earthward end ‌of the Floating Bridge of Heaven. The Kojiki describes Sarutahiko with a long nose, a red and shining face, eyes like eight-span mirrors, and a beard that hung down; the Nihon Shoki ​variant measures him more exactly — a nose of seven hand-spans, a height over seven feet, eyes red like mirrors — and says the heavenly envoys sent to negotiate all stood back in ‌fear. Only Ame-no-Uzume, goddess of dawn, mirth, and dance, stepped forward. She spoke to him boldly and well, and the gatekeeper was won: he asked who was coming and offered himself as ​guide. No force moved him and no god outranked him; what opened the road was speech. The myth's meaning is a charter of legitimacy: heaven's claim to the land passes through an ‌earthly gatekeeper's consent — rule is negotiated at the threshold, not imposed from the sky.

The Marriage That Founded a Line (Kojiki)

After the descent was accomplished, Ame-no-Uzume married Sarutahiko, and the Kojiki ​draws the genealogical consequence: Uzume is the ancestress of the Sarume — the “monkey-women” — the order of female ritual dancers of the Yamato court, whose sacred performances are the root from ‌which kagura, and in part the comic spirit of Noh, descend. The marriage folds the unruly leader of the earthly kami into the celestial order without domesticating him: he remains the red-faced giant, ​she remains the dancer who out-talked him, and together they become the founding couple of Japanese sacred performance. Structurally the pairing is exact — the goddess who moves between worlds weds the ‌god who guards the crossing — and politically it is shrewd: the oldest indigenous power of the archipelago is not defeated by Ninigi's line but married into it. The myth remembers that ​Japan's ritual arts were born at a boundary, from a negotiation.

The Drowning at Azaka (Kojiki)

Even gatekeepers die. The Kojiki records that Sarutahiko went to Ise, to the Isuzu River at Azaka, ​where a giant clam closed upon his hand while he fished; he fell into the water and drowned. The tradition speaks the event in three divine names given to his spirit as ‌he sank: Sokodokumitama, “Bottom-Reach-Spirit,” as he went to the riverbed; Tsubutatsumitama, “Rising-Bubble Spirit,” as his breath broke the surface; and Awasakumitama, “Bubble-Bursting Spirit,” as the bubbles burst and were gone. Anthropologist Emiko ‌Ohnuki-Tierney reads the episode through the monkey symbolism latent in his name — the macaque with one hand trapped in a shell is a stock figure of Japanese folktale. The undertone of the ‌myth is initiatory: the god of boundaries meets his own limit at the water's edge, and in the telling of his death the river at Ise gains a guardian presence that outlasts ​the god's body.

The Great Kami of the Crossroads (Folk religion)

Only seven kami bear the title Ōkami, “Great Kami,” and Sarutahiko is almost alone among them in being an earthly (kunitsukami) ​rather than a heavenly deity — Inari is the other. Medieval Shugendō adopted him as a protector of mountain ascetics; later folk religion made him patron of roads, travelers, comedians, and performers — ‌his grotesque mask leads the Menkake Gyōretsu, the masked procession still held at Kamakura's Tsurugaoka Hachimangū, and Ueshiba Morihei, founder of Aikido, made Sarutahiko's stance between heaven and earth the model of ​aiki, standing “one with the universe.” His cult geography is anchored by Tsubaki Grand Shrine in Mie — first among some two thousand branch shrines — and Sarutahiko Jinja beside the Isuzu ​River at Ise, near the place of his drowning. The pattern across the centuries is consistent: whoever stands at a threshold — pilgrim, dancer, martial artist — stands where Sarutahiko stood first.

Archaeology & Evidence

Sarutahiko's cult ​geography is unusually legible. Tsubaki Grand Shrine at Suzuka in Mie Prefecture, set at the foot of Mount Nyūdō, is the head shrine of his cult, first among some two thousand branch shrines; ‌Sarutahiko Jinja stands by the Isuzu River at Ise, beside the river of his drowning, and Ōasahiko Shrine in Tokushima is another ancient seat. Images of the god — the red shining ​face, hanging beard, long nose — survive in shrine statuary, festival regalia, and the masks of the Menkake Gyōretsu procession still performed at Kamakura's Tsurugaoka Hachimangū, a medieval rite whose masks are preserved ‌as temple treasures. No inscription from the archaic period names him; his earliest witnesses are the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720) manuscripts themselves.
