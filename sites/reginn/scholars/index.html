01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Reginn (reginn) ​— Dwarf ‌Smith, Fafnir's Brother ‌· The ​great one — ​is the ​master smith of ​the Völsung ​cycle: son of ‌Hreiðmarr, brother ​of Fáfnir and ​Ótr, foster-father of ‌Sigurðr, and ​the forger of ‌the sword ​Gram from the ‌shards of ​Sigmundr's blade. Cheated ​of his ​share of the ​cursed otter-ransom ​when Fáfnir murdered ‌their father ​for the gold ‌of Andvari, ​he spent a ‌lifetime arming ​an instrument of ‌revenge — ​and was killed ‌by it, ​on the counsel ​of birds, ‌when Sigurðr tasted ‌the dragon's blood ‌and understood ​their speech.[1]

The ​name belongs ‌to the same ​word as ​ragnarök's regin: Old ‌Norse regin, ​'the ruling powers, ​the gods', ‌which as a ​compounding prefix ‌intensifies to 'mighty, ​great' — ​hence the traditional ​gloss 'the ​great one'.[2] The ‌eddic prose ‌describes him with ​exact economy: ‌the most skilled ​of all men, ​a dwarf ‌in stature, wise, ‌grim, and ‌skilled in magic.[3]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the name as ‌Reginn — ​identical to the ​ASCII input ​but for the ‌capital, because ​normalized Old Norse ​writes no ​diacritic in this ‌name. The ​mechanical rule therefore ​places the ​name in Tier ‌2: nothing ​distinctive is lost ‌in transit, ​a statement about ‌orthography and never ​about the ‌smith. The temple ‌is domain-less: ​the plain-ASCII reginn.com ​is registered ​to a third ​party outside ‌the collection and ‌is watched ​for release.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the otter's ransom; the curse of Andvari; Fáfnir's parricide), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. regin, Reginn.
  3. Reginsmál (prose introduction), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ‌Reginn — the ​smith of the ​Völsung cycle ​and, in the ​dwarf-catalogue of Völuspá, ​simply one dwarf ‌among the many ​made. Its etymology ​is among the ‌more satisfying in ​the northern onomasticon. ‌Reginn belongs with ​Old Norse regin ‌(neuter plural), ​'the ruling powers, ​the gods' — ​Germanic ragina-, 'counsel, ​decree', compare Gothic ​ragin, 'counsel, decision' ‌— the very ​word that stands ‌inside ragnarök, 'the ​doom of the ‌powers'. As the ​first element ‌of compounds the ​same stem intensifies: ‌reginþing is the ​mighty assembly, and ​Cleasby–Vigfusson gloss the ‌prefix use as ‌'great, mighty'.[1] The ‌personal name therefore ​means 'the mighty ​one, the great ‌one' — ​a dwarf carrying ​the word for ‌the gods themselves, ​which the tradition ​may well have ‌intended as irony.

​The ASCII form ‌is not a ​fallback here but ​the form itself: ​normalized Old ​Norse writes six ‌plain Latin letters ‌and a capital. ​The Unicode restoration ‌Reginn therefore preserves ​nothing the ASCII ​alphabet loses, and ‌the mechanical rule ‌classifies the name ‌Tier 2 — ‌an orthographic ​fact, not a ‌judgment on the ​figure.[2]

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • ​r → R ‌— Same, capitalized

​- e → ​e — Same
​- g → ‌g — Same
​- i ​→ i — ​Same

  • n ‌→ n — ​Same
  • n ‌→ n — ​Same

The project ‌holds no domain ​for this name. ‌The plain-ASCII reginn.com ‌is registered to ​a third ​party outside the ​collection and is ​watched for release; ‌this temple claims ‌nothing it does ​not hold.[3]

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /ˈreɣ.inː/ ​— Old ​Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by phoneme:

​- Re- ​— Voiced ‌alveolar trill ​[r] and ​short close-mid ‌front [e], stressed ​on the ‌first syllable ​as in ‌every Old ​Norse word
​- -ginn — ​Medial g ​after a ​vowel is ‌the voiced ​velar fricative [ɣ], ‌a soft ​scrape in ‌the throat ​rather than ‌a stop, ​then short [i], ‌then the ​geminate nasal ​[nː] held ‌audibly across ‌the close

‌For the modern ​speaker, the ​closest approximation ‌is: 'REH-yin' ​— two ​beats, the ‌medial g softened ​toward the ​light fricative ‌of Spanish ​lago, the ‌final n ​held a fraction ​long.

Kindred ​and historical ​forms of ‌the name:

‌- Old ​Norse — Reginn, ‌son of ​Hreiðmarr, foster-father ​of Sigurðr
‌- Younger ‌Futhark — ‌ᚱᛁᚴᛁᚾᚾ (r-i-k-i-n-n), the ‌Viking-Age runic ​rendering

  • ‌Continental Germanic ​— no ​Reginn by ​name; the Nibelungenlied's ‌Mime is ​the corresponding ​smith-fosterer of ​Siegfried[2]
  • ‌Modern Scandinavian — ​Regin, the ​simplified modern ​form, with ‌Icelandic keeping ​Reginn

Reginn ‌is Tier 2 ​because the ‌restoration preserves ​nothing the ‌ASCII form ‌loses: no ​diacritic, no distinctive ​letter, no ​vowel length. ​English and ‌Old Norse ‌write the ​name identically.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Regin, Mime.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Reginn — ​Old Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ​13th c. CE ‌in the Codex ​Regius of ​the Poetic Edda ‌(Reginsmál, Fáfnismál) ​and the codices ‌of Snorri's Edda, ​recording material ‌centuries older.[1] Before ​the Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse ​names were carved ​in the Younger ​Futhark; no ‌runic attestation of ​the name ‌survives, though the ​Sigurðr stones carve ‌his story, ​so the manuscript ‌form remains the ​primary witness. ‌The runic rendering ​is ᚱᛁᚴᛁᚾᚾ (r-i-k-i-n-n).

​The scholarly ‌rendering proceeds step ‌by step:

‌- The name ​is a simplex, ​not a ‌compound: six letters, ​two syllables, a ​geminate nn ‌the manuscripts write ​double.

  • In ​the Younger ‌Futhark, ᛁ (ís) ​writes both ‌/i/ and /e/, ​and ᚴ (kaun) ​writes both ​/k/ and /g/ ​— the spelling ‌rikinn is ‌therefore a normalized ​phonetic reconstruction, since ‌the sixteen-rune ​row distinguishes neither ​voicing nor ‌vowel height here.

‌- Old Norse ‌orthography needs ‌no diacritic: both ​vowels are short, ‌and no ​distinctive letter (þ, ​ð, æ, ǫ) ​occurs in ‌the name.

  • ​The Unicode ​restoration Reginn is ​therefore identical to ‌the ASCII ​form — the ​restoration is a ​matter of ‌canonical reference, not ​rescued orthography.
  • ‌The name's ​etymology — Old ‌Norse regin ​'the ruling powers, ‌the gods', the ‌word inside ​ragnarök, with the ​intensifying prefix use ​'mighty' — ​is registered in ‌the lexica, not ‌settled by ​the spelling.[2]

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).
  2. de Vries, Jan, Altnordisches etymologisches Wörterbuch, 2nd ed. (Leiden: Brill, 1962), s.v. regin; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. regin.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Reginn is ​catalogued under ‌Dwarf Smith, ‌Fafnir's Brother ​— and ​the pairing ​is exact: ​the sources ​define him ‌wholly by his ​craft and ​his feud, ‌and the ​feud is ‌the brother's.[1]

​### Dwarf ‌Smith

The ​eddic prose ​makes him 'the ​most skilled ​of all ​men, and ‌a dwarf ​in stature' ‌— the ​tradition's finest ‌smith, teacher of ​chess, runes ‌and tongues, ​and the ‌only craftsman ​in the ​cycle whose ‌work never ‌fails. His ‌brothers-in-craft among the ​gods' smiths ​are Brokkr ‌and Sindri, ​who forge ​Mjölnir and ‌Draupnir for ​a wager; ​Reginn forges ‌for revenge.[2]

​Fafnir's Brother

‌Everything Reginn ​does follows ​from the ​otter's ransom: ​Fáfnir killed ‌their father ‌Hreiðmarr for ​Andvari's gold and ‌kept it ​all, and ​Reginn — ‌wronged and ‌patient — ‌went to ‌breed the ​dragon-slayer.[1]

‌Fosterage

At King ​Hjálprek's court ​he fostered ​the young ‌Sigurðr, the ​strongest tie ​the northern ​world knew ‌short of blood ​— made, ​in his ​case, in ‌order to ​be broken.[1]

‌### Betrayal

​The cycle's ‌coldest arc: ​the hand that ‌reforged Gram ‌meant to ​claim the ​hoard, and ​only the ​speech of ‌birds told ‌the foster-son ​in time.[1]

Sources

  1. Völsunga saga, chs. 13–20, trans. Jesse L. Byock, The Saga of the Volsungs (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1990).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the otter's ransom; the dwarf-smiths' wager), trans. Faulkes (1998).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography of ‌Reginn is ‌narrative rather ​than cultic ​— the ​corpus gives ​him ​no regalia ‌but fixed ​around his ​scenes a ‌small set ​of objects ‌no retelling ​can ‌omit:[1]

  • ​The sword ​Gram — ​reforged from ​the shards ​of Sigmundr's ‌blade, ​keen enough ‌to cleave ​the anvil ‌to the ​stock and ‌cut a ​floating ‌wool-pack in ​the Rhine: ​craft completing ‌a broken ‌lineage, and ‌the instrument ​of its ​maker's ‌death
  • ​The anvil ​— the ‌measure of ​true forging, ​where two ‌flawed ​blades broke ‌before Gram ​stood the ​test: the ​smith's honesty ​enforced by ‌his ‌own work

​- Andvaranautr ‌— the ​cursed ring ​of the ‌otter's ransom, ‌which Andvari ‌swore ‌would kill ​two brothers: ‌the feud's ​first cause[2]
​- The ​dragon's blood ‌— ​the taste ​on Sigurðr's ​tongue that ‌opened bird-speech: ​the moment ​the foster-son ​learned ‌the foster-father's ​plan

  • ‌The nuthatches ​— the ‌birds on ​Gnitaheiðr whose ‌counsel no ‌retelling ​omits: let ​the ring ​be the ​brother's ransom, ‌and cut ‌off his ​head[3]

Sources

  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the otter's ransom; Andvaranautr and the curse), trans. Faulkes (1998).

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Reginn's mythology runs ​the length of the Völsung cycle ‌— the ransom in Skáldskaparmál, the ‌fosterage in Reginsmál and Völsunga saga, ​the reforging of Gram, and the ​death on Fáfnir's heath.

The ​Otter's Ransom (Skáldskaparmál)

Óðinn, Hœnir and ​Loki came to Andvari's falls, where ​Reginn's brother Ótr fished in otter ‌shape; Loki killed him with ​a stone, and the gods carried ​the skin into Hreiðmarr's house and ‌boasted of the catch. Hreiðmarr and ​his sons seized the gods and ‌demanded weregild — the skin filled ​and covered with gold. Loki forced ‌the dwarf Andvari to yield his ​hoard and, at the last, the ​ring Andvaranautr; Andvari laid the ​curse: the gold would be the ​death of two brothers and the ​strife of eight princes, and no ‌man should enjoy it. The curse ​kept its accounts. Hreiðmarr refused his ‌sons any share; Fáfnir killed his ​father in his sleep and took ‌all; Reginn got nothing, and ​Fáfnir went out to Gnitaheiðr to ‌brood on the hoard in dragon ​shape.[1]

The Foster-Father (Reginsmál; Völsunga ‌saga)

The eddic prose introduces him ​exactly: Reginn was Hreiðmarr's son, the ​most skilled of all men and ‌a dwarf in stature; he was ‌wise, grim, and skilled in magic. ‌He came to King Hjálprek's ​court, where the boy Sigurðr — ​Sigmundr's posthumous son — was being ‌raised, and became his foster-father, teaching ​him chess, runes and tongues. All ​the while he worked on the ‌boy's pride: Fáfnir lies on Gnitaheiðr, ​and your father left you nothing. ​With Óðinn's counsel, in the stranger's ‌guise the poems call Hnikarr, ​Sigurðr chose the horse Grani, Grani's ‌sire being of Sleipnir's line.[2]

​The Reforging of Gram (Völsunga saga)

​Sigurðr demanded a sword. Reginn forged ​the best his craft could make; ​Sigurðr struck it on the anvil ‌and it shattered. A second, better ‌blade broke the same way. Then ​Hjördís gave up the fragments ‌of Sigmundr's sword — the blade ​Óðinn himself had shattered in her ​husband's last battle — and from ‌them Reginn reforged Gram. It bit ‌the anvil down to the stock ‌and did not break, and it ‌cut a pack of wool drifting ​on the Rhine as easily as ‌it cut water. The tradition's ​purest statement about craft: peerless skill, ​wholly in service of a grudge.[3]

​### The Blood on the Tongue ‌(Fáfnismál; Völsunga saga)

On Gnitaheiðr Sigurðr ​dug a pit in the dragon's ​path and drove Gram up into ​Fáfnir's heart from below. Dying, the ‌dragon warned him: the gold ​will be your death, and Reginn ​betrayed me — he will betray ​you. Reginn crept out, praised the ‌killing, and asked one service: cut ​out the heart, roast it, and ‌let me eat it. Testing the ​roast, Sigurðr burned his finger and ‌put it to his mouth; the ​dragon's blood touched his tongue ‌and he understood the speech of ‌birds. The nuthatches said: Reginn lies ​in the heather planning betrayal; eat ​the heart yourself and grow wise; ​cut off his head. Sigurðr took ​the counsel, and the curse closed ‌its second account — two brothers ‌dead, and the gold passed on ​with Andvaranautr, unenjoyed.[4]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the otter's ransom; the curse; Fáfnir's parricide), trans. Faulkes (1998).
  2. Reginsmál (prose and stanzas), trans. Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2014).
  3. Völsunga saga, chs. 13–18, trans. Byock (1990).
  4. Fáfnismál (prose and stanzas), trans. Larrington (2014); Völsunga saga, chs. 18–20, trans. Byock (1990).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Reginn's clearest ​kin are ‌inside the ‌Germanic corpus ​itself. The ​Nibelungenlied's Mime ​— the ​smith who ​fosters Siegfried, ‌forges for ​him, and ​schemes to ‌send him ​against the ‌dragon so ​the hoard ‌will come ​free — ​is the ​Continental form ​of the ​same figure, ‌softened in ​the High ‌German epic ​but identical ‌in structure: ​the fosterer ‌who breeds ​his own instrument.[1] ‌Old English ​tradition preserves ​the smith-type ‌differently, in ‌Weland of ‌Beowulf and ​Deor, the ​artificer whose ‌craft serves ​his revenge ​— the ‌type to ​which Reginn ​belongs.

Within ‌the Norse ​corpus his ‌brothers-in-craft are ​the dwarf-smiths ​of the ​gods: Brokkr ​and Sindri, ‌who forge ‌Mjölnir, Draupnir ​and Gullinbursti ‌for stakes ​no higher ​than a ‌wager — ‌the clean ‌contrast that shows ‌what Reginn ​is: a ‌smith whose ​work serves ​a feud ​instead of ‌a boast. ​The modern ​handbooks keep ​him where ‌the sources ​put him ​— the ​Völsung cycle's ‌necessary smith, ​indispensable and ‌damned, and ​the tradition's ‌study in ​fosterage betrayed.[2] ‌Within the ‌corpus, his ​related figures ​are the ​household his ​story binds ‌and breaks: ‌Fáfnir, Andvari, ​and Sigurðr.

Sources

  1. Das Nibelungenlied, ed. and trans. A. T. Hatto, 2nd ed. (Harmondsworth: Penguin, 1969), for the Mime parallel.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Regin; Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Regin.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Reginn's ​afterlife runs ‌along two ‌tracks. ​In the ​learned ​tradition he ​survived with ​the ‌whole Völsung ​material: William ​Morris's ‌Sigurd the ​Volsung ‌(1876) gives ​the foster-smith ‌his ​full cold ​arc — ​the ​broken blades, ​the ‌shards of ​Sigmundr, the ‌anvil ​test — ‌and Wagner ​compressed ‌Reginn with ​Mime ‌into the ​Nibelung smith ​of ‌the Ring ‌cycle, where ‌the ​brother scheming ​for ‌the hoard ​survives almost ​intact.[1]

‌In the ​comparative tradition ​he ‌stands beside ​Weland ‌and the ​Greek Hephaistos ​as ​the northern ​proof that ‌the ‌master craftsman ​is ‌never a ​safe ally: ​modern ‌scholarship treats ‌him as ‌the ‌cycle's study ​in ‌fosterage betrayed, ​the figure ​through ​whom the ‌tradition asks ​what ​a teacher ​owes ‌a student. ​Retellings of ​the ​Sigurðr story, ‌from the ​Victorian ‌paraphrases to ​the ‌modern handbooks, ​keep all ‌four ‌of his ​scenes — ​the ​ransom, the ​fosterage, ‌the reforging, ‌and the ​birds.[2]

Sources

  1. Morris, William, The Story of Sigurd the Volsung and the Fall of the Niblungs (London: Ellis and White, 1877).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Regin, Fafnir.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Reginn is ​a textual ‌figure, but the ‌Völsung cycle ​is among the ​most picture-rich ​narratives in the ​North, and the ​smith's scenes ‌were carved. The ​Viking-Age Sigurðr ​iconography survives on ‌the Swedish ​runestones at Ramsund ‌(Sö 101) ​and Gök (Sö ‌327) in ​Södermanland and on ​the Drävle stone ​(U 1163) ​in Uppland; the ​Ramsund carving ‌shows the cycle's ​whole cast ‌— the slain ​dragon as ‌the rune-bearing serpent ​band, Sigurðr ‌at the fire ​with his thumb ‌in his ​mouth, the horse ​Grani loaded ‌with the hoard, ‌the birds ‌in the tree, ​the otter ​by the falls ‌— and, ​at the carving's ​edge, the smith's ‌tools laid ​beside a headless ​body, the ‌standard scholarly reading ​of which ‌is the killed ​Reginn.[1] In ​the Insular world ​the Manx ​crosses at Jurby, ‌Malew and Andreas ‌carry the ​roasting scene, and ‌in England ​the Halton cross ​in Lancashire ‌and the York ‌Minster grave ‌slab carry related ‌episodes.

The ​manuscript witnesses are ‌the Codex Regius ​of the ​Poetic Edda for ​Reginsmál and ‌Fáfnismál, the codices ​of Snorri's ​Edda for Skáldskaparmál's ​prose account ‌of the ransom, ​and the ​vellum of Völsunga ​saga with its ‌paper descendants ​for the fullest ‌prose life.[2] ​No runic inscription ‌invokes Reginn ​by name; the ‌carvings tell ‌the story, and ​the stones ​keep their silence ​about the smith ​except where ‌the tools lie ‌by the ​headless man.

Sources

  1. Sawyer, Birgit, The Viking-Age Rune-Stones: Custom and Commemoration in Early Medieval Scandinavia (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2000); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Fafnir, Regin (the Sigurðr carvings).
  2. Völsunga saga, trans. Jesse L. Byock (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1990), introduction on manuscripts and transmission.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda knows ‌Reginn twice ‌over. The dwarf-catalogue ​of Völuspá ​— the ​Dvergatal — lists ​a Reginn ​among the ‌dwarfs made, 'Reginn ​and Ráðsviðr', ​a name ‌in the roll ​of the ‌craft-folk with ​no story attached.[1] ‌Whether the ​compiler meant ​the Völsung smith ​or simply ​the fitting ​name — the ‌'mighty one' ​— the catalogue ‌proves the ​name belonged ‌to the dwarf ​world before ‌it belonged ​to the legend.

‌The legend ​itself the ​Edda gives in ‌two poems. ‌Reginsmál opens ‌with the prose ​that defines ​him — ‌Hreiðmarr's son, the ​most skilled ​of men, ‌a dwarf in ​stature, wise, ​grim, and ‌skilled in magic ​— and ‌lets him ​tell Sigurðr the ​history of ​the gold ​and press him ‌toward Gnitaheiðr; ‌the poem ​closes with the ‌choosing of ​the horse ​Grani under the ‌stranger Hnikarr, ‌Óðinn in ‌his road-guise. Fáfnismál ‌carries the ​second half: ‌the dying dragon's ​double warning ​that the ​gold will kill ‌Sigurðr and ​that Reginn, who ​betrayed him, ​will betray ‌the boy too; ​the heart-roasting; ​the nuthatches' ​counsel in the ‌branches; and ​the killing ‌of the foster-father ​whose plot ‌the birds ​laid bare.[2] Between ‌them the ‌poems give ​the whole arc ​— the ​wrong, the ​weapon, and the ‌wages — ‌without Snorri's ​prose frame.

Sources

  1. Völuspá (the dwarf-catalogue), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Reginsmál and Fáfnismál, trans. Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Reginn's ‌mythological frame. ​Skáldskaparmál tells ​the otter's ​ransom as ​the answer ​to a ‌skald's question ​— why ​is gold ‌called the ​otter's payment? ‌— and ​so ‌preserves the ​feud's first ​cause: Loki's ​stone, Hreiðmarr's ​weregild, Andvari's ​forced hoard ‌and the ​ring Andvaranautr, ‌the curse ​on the ‌gold, Fáfnir's ​parricide, and ‌Reginn's exclusion ​from ‌his share. ​Snorri compresses ​the aftermath ‌into a ‌sentence that ‌contains the ​whole Völsung ​cycle: Fáfnir ‌became a ​dragon on ​Gnitaheiðr, and ‌Reginn became ​Sigurðr's foster-father ​and urged ‌him ​to the ‌killing.[1]

The ​gold-kennings of ​Skáldskaparmál keep ​the story ​alive in ‌the poets' ‌shorthand: gold ​is 'the ‌otter's payment', ​'the fire ​of the ‌serpent's lair', ‌'Fáfnir's inheritance' ‌— ‌every skald ​who paid ‌a compliment ​in gold ​quoted, without ​knowing it ‌needed quoting, ​the feud ​of Hreiðmarr's ​sons.[2] Snorri's ‌account and ​the eddic ​poems differ ​in ‌detail — ​Snorri knows ‌no pit-digging ​and gives ‌the heart-eating ​differently — ‌and the ‌divergences are ​themselves evidence: ​two independent ​witnesses to ​a story ‌the whole ‌North was ​telling.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (the otter's ransom; the curse; Fáfnir and Reginn), trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Viking Society for Northern Research, 1998).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for gold), trans. Faulkes (1998).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The runic ​corpus contains ‌no invocation of ‌Reginn by ​name — no ​runestone asks ​his favour and ​no amulet ​bears the theonym ‌— but ​the Viking-Age stones ​tell his ‌story in pictures. ​The great ‌Sigurðr carvings of ​eleventh-century Sweden ‌at Ramsund (Sö ​101) and ​Gök (Sö 327) ​in Södermanland ​set the whole ​cycle beside ‌their memorial inscriptions: ​the rune-band ‌itself is the ​dragon, pierced ‌from below by ​Sigurðr's sword; ‌beside it stand ​the fire ‌and the roasting ​heart, the ​listening hero with ‌his thumb ‌in his mouth, ‌the birds ​in the tree, ​the otter, ‌and the smith's ​tools laid ​out beside a ‌headless body ​— the standard ​scholarly reading ‌of which is ​Reginn, identified ‌by his trade.[1]

​What can ​be said runologically ​of the ​name itself is ‌the rendering: ‌in the Younger ​Futhark it ‌would have been ​carved ᚱᛁᚴᛁᚾᚾ ​(r-i-k-i-n-n), where ᛁ ‌ís writes ‌both /i/ and ‌/e/ and ‌ᚴ kaun writes ​both /k/ ‌and /g/, so ​the transliteration ​rikinn is a ​normalized phonetic ‌reconstruction; the sixteen-rune ​row underspecifies ​exactly the features ​the Latin ‌manuscript spelling supplies.[2] ​The carvings ​at Ramsund and ​Gök are ‌commemorative monuments, not ​cult objects ‌— the story ​was worth ‌a Christian landowner's ​bridge and ‌stone a generation ‌before and ​after the conversion, ​which says ​more about the ​cycle's hold ‌on the North ‌than any ​invocation could.

Sources

  1. Sawyer, Birgit, The Viking-Age Rune-Stones: Custom and Commemoration in Early Medieval Scandinavia (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2000); Lindow, John, Norse Mythology (Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Fafnir (the Sigurðr carvings).
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), runic appendix.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Reginn's ‌fullest medieval ‌life is ​the Völsunga saga's. ​The thirteenth-century ​prose compilation ​sets out everything ​the poems ‌imply: his ​arrival at ​King Hjálprek's court, ‌his teaching ​of the ‌boy — ​chess, runes, and ‌tongues — ​the slow ​incitement against Fáfnir, ​the two ​blades that ​broke on ‌the anvil, the ​fetching of ‌Sigmundr's shards ​from Hjördís, ‌the reforging of ​Gram and ‌its twin ​tests, and the ‌end on ​Gnitaheiðr when ​Sigurðr, warned ‌by the nuthatches, ‌cut off ‌his foster-father's ​head.[1] The ​saga also keeps ‌the episodes ​the poems ​skip: Óðinn's road-side ‌counsel to ​dig the ​pit, the ‌vengeance on Lyngvi ​for Sigmundr, ‌and the ​loading of ​the hoard onto ​Grani.

The ​Continental-descended Þiðreks ‌saga, compiled in ‌Norway from ​Low German ‌material, retells ​the fosterage and ​the dragon-slaying ‌in its ‌own key ‌— proof that ‌the story ​of the ‌smith who raised ​the dragon-slayer ​circulated up ​and down ‌the Germanic world ​with every ​detail renegotiated ​except one: ‌the foster-father dies ​by the ​sword he ​made.[2] And the ‌skaldic tradition ​absorbed the ‌feud into ​its very diction, ‌for Skáldskaparmál's ​gold-kennings — ‌the otter's ‌payment, the serpent-lair's ​fire — ​kept Hreiðmarr's ​sons in every ​compliment a ‌poet ever ‌paid a ​generous prince.[3]

Sources

  1. Völsunga saga, chs. 13–20, trans. Jesse L. Byock, The Saga of the Volsungs (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1990).
  2. Þiðreks saga af Bern, trans. Edward R. Haymes, The Saga of Thidrek of Bern (New York: Garland, 1988).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for gold), trans. Faulkes (1998).

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Reginn is ​the Edda's ‌coldest lesson ‌about skill ​without loyalty. ​Everything he ​makes is ​perfect: the ​teaching, the ‌horse-choosing, the ​reforged blade, ​the patient ‌plan. Everything ​he is ‌fails. The ​tradition states ‌the paradox ​without comment ​— the ​most skilled ​of all ​men, a ‌dwarf in ​stature, wise ‌and grim ​and versed ‌in magic ​— and ‌then lets ​the birds say ‌what no ​human in ​the story ‌can afford ‌to say: ‌the foster-father ​is lying ​in the ‌heather, measuring ​the boy ​for a ‌grave.[1]

What ​damns Reginn ​is not ‌incompetence but ​inversion. Fosterage ‌was the ​northern world's ​sacred adoption; ​the smith ​turned it ‌into a ‌tool. The ​sword he ‌forged was ​true, and ​the hand ‌that forged ‌it was ‌false, and the ‌whole cycle ​hangs on ‌the difference ​between those ​two sentences. ​In a ‌mythology that ​runs on ​oaths, Reginn ​is the ‌oath kept ​to the ​letter of ​revenge and ‌broken in ​the spirit ‌of kinship ​— and ‌Gram, the ​blade that ‌would not ‌break, is ​the tradition's ​way of ​saying that ​craft outlives ‌the craftsman, ‌and testifies ​against him.[1]

Sources

  1. Fáfnismál (the nuthatch stanzas), trans. Larrington (2014); Völsunga saga, ch. 20, trans. Byock (1990).
