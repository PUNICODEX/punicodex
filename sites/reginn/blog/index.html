The hidden history behind Reginn

Some names in the lexicon need rescuing from the address bar — a diacritic reclaimed, a lost letter put back. Reginn needs nothing of the kind, and his story needs everything: the coldest arc in the Völsung cycle, from a skinned otter to a headless smith, told across the Poetic Edda, the Prose Edda, and Völsunga saga, and carved in stone beside a Swedish bridge. This post walks the full record — name, runes, sound, myth, and scholarly apparatus — and ends where the record gets honest: the smith who forged the dragon-slaying sword has no domain in the collection, because the plain-ASCII reginn.com was registered by someone else long ago, and the project watches and waits.

Overview

Reginn (reginn) — Dwarf Smith, Fafnir's Brother · The great one — belongs to the Norse tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Dwarf Smith, Fafnir's Brother".

Reginn, son of Hreiðmarr and brother of Fáfnir and Ótr, is the master smith of the Völsung cycle — 'the most skilled of all men, and a dwarf in stature; wise, grim, and skilled in magic', as the eddic prose puts it. Cheated of his share of the cursed otter-ransom when Fáfnir murdered their father for the gold of Andvari, he fostered the young Sigurðr at King Hjálprek's court and reforged from the shards of Sigmundr's blade the sword Gram — the one weapon that could kill the dragon. But the hand that forged the slayer meant to claim the hoard, and when Sigurðr tasted the dragon's blood and understood the speech of birds, the foster-son turned Gram on the foster-father.

PuniCodex restores the name as Reginn — identical to the ASCII input but for the capital, because normalized Old Norse writes no diacritic in this name. The mechanical rule therefore places it in Tier 2: nothing distinctive is lost in transit, a statement about orthography and never about the smith. No domain is claimed: the plain-ASCII reginn.com is registered to a third party outside the collection and is watched for release.

The Name

The name is attested in normalized Old Norse as Reginn, and its etymology is among the more satisfying in the northern onomasticon. Reginn belongs with Old Norse regin (neuter plural), 'the ruling powers, the gods' — Germanic ragina-, 'counsel, decree', compare Gothic ragin, 'counsel, decision' — the very word that stands inside ragnarök, 'the doom of the powers'. As the first element of compounds the same stem intensifies: reginþing is the mighty assembly, and Cleasby–Vigfusson gloss the prefix use as 'great, mighty'. The personal name therefore means 'the mighty one, the great one' — a dwarf carrying the word for the gods themselves, which the tradition may well have intended as irony.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in Younger Futhark as ᚱᛁᚴᛁᚾᚾ — the sixteen-rune Viking-Age row in which the name would have been carved on wood, bone, and stone; the transliteration runs r-i-k-i-n-n.

Here the runic row underspecifies exactly what the manuscripts supply: ᛁ ís writes both /i/ and /e/, and ᚴ kaun writes both /k/ and /g/, so the spelling rikinn is a normalized phonetic reconstruction rather than a transcription of any surviving carving. No Viking-Age carving of the name is known — though the Ramsund and Gök runestones carve his whole story, tools and headless body included — and the primary witness remains the manuscript spelling of the Eddas, which is itself plain ASCII.

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈreɣ.inː/ — Reconstructed Old Norse.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: REH-yin — two beats, stress on the first syllable, the medial g softened toward the light fricative of Spanish lago ([ɣ], never the hard stop of English go), the final n held a fraction long.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Old Norse — Reginn, son of Hreiðmarr, foster-father of Sigurðr
  • Younger Futhark — ᚱᛁᚴᛁᚾᚾ, the Viking-Age runic rendering
  • Continental Germanic — no Reginn by name; the Nibelungenlied's Mime is the corresponding smith-fosterer of Siegfried
  • Modern Scandinavian — Regin, the simplified modern form, with Icelandic keeping Reginn

The stress falls on the first syllable, as in every Old Norse word. The e is short, the medial g is the soft voiced fricative Old Norse uses between vowels, and the geminate nn is held audibly across the close — the one articulatory event the spelling actually records.

Mythology

Reginn's mythology runs the length of the Völsung cycle — the ransom in Skáldskaparmál, the fosterage in Reginsmál and Völsunga saga, the reforging of Gram, and the death on Fáfnir's heath.

The Otter's Ransom (Skáldskaparmál)

It begins with a skinned otter. Óðinn, Hœnir and Loki came to Andvari's falls, where Reginn's brother Ótr fished in otter shape; Loki killed him with a stone, and that evening the gods carried the skin into Hreiðmarr's house and boasted of the catch. Hreiðmarr and his sons seized the gods and demanded weregild: the skin filled and covered with gold. Loki forced the dwarf Andvari to yield his hoard and, at the last, the ring Andvaranautr; Andvari laid the curse — that gold would be the death of two brothers and the strife of eight princes, and no man should enjoy it. The curse kept its accounts. Hreiðmarr refused his sons any share; Fáfnir killed his father in his sleep and took all; Reginn got nothing, and Fáfnir went out to Gnitaheiðr to brood on the hoard in dragon shape.

The Foster-Father (Reginsmál; Völsunga saga)

The eddic prose introduces him with exact economy: Reginn was Hreiðmarr's son, the most skilled of all men and a dwarf in stature; he was wise, grim, and skilled in magic. He came to the court of King Hjálprek, where the boy Sigurðr — Sigmundr's posthumous son — was being raised, and became his foster-father, teaching him chess, runes and tongues. All the while he worked on the boy's pride: Fáfnir lies on Gnitaheiðr, and your father left you nothing. With Óðinn's counsel, in the stranger's guise the poems call Hnikarr, Sigurðr chose the horse Grani. The foster-bond was the strongest tie the northern world knew short of blood — and Reginn made it in order to break it.

The Reforging of Gram (Völsunga saga)

Sigurðr demanded a sword. Reginn forged the best his craft could make; Sigurðr struck it on the anvil and it shattered. A second, better blade broke the same way. Then Hjördís gave up the fragments of Sigmundr's sword — the blade Óðinn himself had shattered in her husband's last battle — and from them Reginn reforged Gram: it bit the anvil down to the stock and did not break, and it cut a pack of wool drifting on the Rhine as easily as it cut water. The scene is the tradition's purest statement about craft: Reginn's skill is real, peerless, and wholly in service of his revenge.

The Blood on the Tongue (Fáfnismál; Völsunga saga)

On Gnitaheiðr Sigurðr dug a pit in the dragon's path and drove Gram up into Fáfnir's heart from below. Dying, the dragon warned him: the gold will be your death, and Reginn betrayed me — he will betray you. Reginn crept out, praised the killing, and asked one service: cut out the heart, roast it, and let me eat it. Testing the roast, Sigurðr burned his finger and put it to his mouth; the dragon's blood touched his tongue and he understood the speech of birds. The nuthatches said: Reginn lies in the heather planning betrayal; eat the heart yourself and grow wise; cut off his head. Sigurðr took the counsel, and the curse closed its second account — two brothers dead, and the gold passed on with Andvaranautr, unenjoyed, exactly as the dwarf had sworn.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Reginn is narrative rather than cultic — the corpus gives him no regalia, but fixed around his scenes a small set of objects no retelling can omit:

  • The sword Gram — reforged from the shards of Sigmundr's blade: craft completing a broken lineage, and the instrument of its maker's death
  • The anvil — the measure of true forging, where two flawed blades broke before Gram stood the test
  • Andvaranautr — the cursed ring of the otter's ransom: the feud's first cause
  • The dragon's blood — the taste on Sigurðr's tongue that opened bird-speech: the moment the foster-son learned the foster-father's plan
  • The nuthatches — the birds on Gnitaheiðr whose counsel no retelling omits: let the ring be the brother's ransom, and cut off his head

Archaeology & Evidence

Reginn is a textual figure, but the Völsung cycle is among the most picture-rich narratives in the North, and the smith's scenes were carved. The Viking-Age Sigurðr iconography survives on the Swedish runestones at Ramsund (Sö 101) and Gök (Sö 327) in Södermanland and on the Drävle stone (U 1163) in Uppland; the Ramsund carving shows the cycle's whole cast — the slain dragon as the rune-bearing serpent band, Sigurðr at the fire with his thumb in his mouth, the horse Grani loaded with the hoard, the birds in the tree, the otter by the falls — and, at the carving's edge, the smith's tools laid beside a headless body, the standard scholarly reading of which is the killed Reginn. In the Insular world the Manx crosses at Jurby, Malew and Andreas carry the roasting scene, and in England the Halton cross in Lancashire and the York Minster grave slab carry related episodes. The manuscript witnesses are the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, the codices of Snorri's Edda, and the vellum of Völsunga saga.

Realm & Domain

Reginn's domain is Dwarf Smith, Fafnir's Brother — and the pairing is exact: the sources define him wholly by his craft and his feud, and the feud is the brother's.

Dwarf Smith

The eddic prose makes him the most skilled of all men and a dwarf in stature — the tradition's finest smith, teacher of chess, runes and tongues, and the only craftsman in the cycle whose work never fails. His brothers-in-craft among the gods' smiths are Brokkr and Sindri, who forge Mjölnir and Draupnir for a wager; Reginn forges for revenge.

Fafnir's Brother

Everything Reginn does follows from the otter's ransom: Fáfnir killed their father Hreiðmarr for Andvari's gold and kept it all, and Reginn — wronged and patient — went to breed the dragon-slayer.

Fosterage

At King Hjálprek's court he fostered the young Sigurðr, the strongest tie the northern world knew short of blood — made, in his case, in order to be broken.

Betrayal

The cycle's coldest arc: the hand that reforged Gram meant to claim the hoard, and only the speech of birds told the foster-son in time.

Across Cultures

Reginn's clearest kin are inside the Germanic corpus itself. The Nibelungenlied's Mime — the smith who fosters Siegfried, forges for him, and schemes to send him against the dragon so the hoard will come free — is the Continental form of the same figure, softened in the High German epic but identical in structure: the fosterer who breeds his own instrument. Old English tradition preserves the smith-type differently, in Weland of Beowulf and Deor, the artificer whose craft serves his revenge. Within the Norse corpus his brothers-in-craft are the dwarf-smiths of the gods: Brokkr and Sindri, who forge Mjölnir, Draupnir and Gullinbursti for stakes no higher than a wager — the clean contrast that shows what Reginn is: a smith whose work serves a feud instead of a boast. Within the corpus, his related figures are the household his story binds and breaks: Fáfnir, Andvari, and Sigurðr.

Cultural Legacy

Reginn's afterlife runs along two tracks. In the learned tradition he survived with the whole Völsung material: William Morris's Sigurd the Volsung gives the foster-smith his full cold arc — the broken blades, the shards of Sigmundr, the anvil test — and Wagner compressed Reginn with Mime into the Nibelung smith of the Ring cycle, where the brother scheming for the hoard survives almost intact. In the comparative tradition he stands beside Weland and the Greek Hephaistos as the northern proof that the master craftsman is never a safe ally: modern scholarship treats him as the cycle's study in fosterage betrayed, the figure through whom the tradition asks what a teacher owes a student.

A Meditation

Reginn is the Edda's coldest lesson about skill without loyalty. Everything he makes is perfect: the teaching, the horse-choosing, the reforged blade, the patient plan. Everything he is fails. The tradition states the paradox without comment — the most skilled of all men, a dwarf in stature, wise and grim and versed in magic — and then lets the birds say what no human in the story can afford to say: the foster-father is lying in the heather, measuring the boy for a grave. What damns Reginn is not incompetence but inversion. Fosterage was the northern world's sacred adoption; the smith turned it into a tool. The sword he forged was true, and the hand that forged it was false, and the whole cycle hangs on the difference between those two sentences. In a mythology that runs on oaths, Reginn is the oath kept to the letter of revenge and broken in the spirit of kinship — and Gram, the blade that would not break, is the tradition's way of saying that craft outlives the craftsman, and testifies against him.

The Unicode Restoration

Reginn is classified as Tier 2, and the classification is the lesson. The tier rule is mechanical, not editorial: a name is information-rich when the restoration preserves something the ASCII form loses — a diacritic, a distinctive letter, a vowel length. Old Norse Reginn carries none of these. The manuscripts write six plain Latin letters and a capital, and the ASCII alphabet loses nothing in copying them. Tier 2 is not a demotion of the smith; it is an honest report about his name's orthography. The PuniCodex tier system exists to make exactly this distinction without flinching.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines inherit whatever spelling the web normalizes — and for a plain-ASCII name, normalization is not the risk; confusion is. Reginn the Völsung smith, regin the word for the gods inside ragnarök, Regin the living Scandinavian given name, reginn.com the third-party registration: a canonical record is what keeps them sorted. This temple is the lexicon's fixed point for the foster-father of Sigurðr — tier, sources, etymology and all — whether or not the address ever comes home.
