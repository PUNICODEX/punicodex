Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Sanskrit Narasiṃha, from nara 'man' + siṃha 'lion'; the man-lion avatāra of Viṣṇu who slew Hiraṇyakaśipu.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Sanskrit — नरसिंह (narasiṃha) — nara 'man' + siṃha 'lion'; the compound names the avatāra's hybrid body
  • Pali — Narasiha — the Middle Indic form of the name used in Pali commentarial literature
  • Sinhala — Siṃhala 'lion(-abode)', source of the island name Ceylon/Sri Lanka — a living cognate of siṃha, 'lion'
  • Modern descendants — Narasiṃha, Narasingh, Narasimhudu — the name survives across North and South Indian languages and as a male given name

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Narasiṃha in Later Traditions

Narasiṃha's afterlife is a study in devotional geography. In South India he became a royal deity: the Vijayanagara court identified itself with Viṣṇu's protective avatāras, and the colossal Ugra-Narasimha at Hampi still shows what the identification looked like in stone. Eastward, Khmer sculptors carved the disemboweling of Hiraṇyakaśipu at Banteay Srei (c. 967 CE), proof that the Purāṇic cycle traveled with Sanskrit culture beyond India. Śaiva tradition answered with the Śarabha episode, in which Śiva must subdue the man-lion's unquenchable rage — a counter-telling that kept Narasiṃha at the center of inter-sectarian argument. In Tantric practice his mantra became standard armor against danger; in vernacular bhakti, Prahlāda's story anchored the claim that devotion outlasts every worldly power.

Modern Legacy

Narasiṃha remains among the most actively worshipped forms of Viṣṇu. Ahobilam's nine shrines and Simhachalam near Visakhapatnam — where the deity is kept under a coat of sandalwood paste renewed annually — anchor major pilgrimage circuits, and Narasimha Jayanti, falling on the full moon of Vaiśākha, celebrates the twilight of the boon. The name itself never left circulation: Narasiṃha, Narasingh, and Narasimhudu serve as given names across India, and new temples — most visibly Yadadri in Telangana — continue to rise around the old story. Modern retellings, from children's comics to cinema, keep returning to the two figures the Purāṇas made unforgettable: the father with the airtight contract, and the son with only a name.

In the Earth

The earliest monumental telling of the cycle stands at Udayagiri in Madhya Pradesh: Cave 6 (c. 400–401 CE, reign of Candragupta II) carves Narasiṃha disemboweling Hiraṇyakaśipu across his lap — one of the first dated images of the avatāra, and already iconographically complete. The sixth-century Dāśāvatāra temple at Deogarh adds a canonical panel within a full Viṣṇu program. In the far south, Ahobilam's nine shrines in the Nallamala hills embed the myth in living pilgrimage geography. The Vijayanagara capital at Hampi preserves the colossal monolithic Ugra-Narasimha, seated with the demon's body across his thighs, attributed to the early sixteenth century. Outside India, the Banteay Srei temple at Angkor (c. 967 CE) renders the same disemboweling in pink sandstone, with Prahlāda kneeling calmly beside the violence.

The Meditation

What Narasiṃha means — beyond the dictionary

Narasiṃha is the divine answer to a lawyer's question: what can kill the man who closed every door? The tradition's answer refuses the terms of the contract — not by breaking it, which would make the god a cheat, but by stepping into the one space the contract could not name: twilight, threshold, claw, man-lion. It is mythology doing what it does at its best, exposing the poverty of exhaustive conditions. The boon fails not because it was poorly drafted but because reality is wider than any list. And the myth's second movement is the greater one: the killing does not end the story — a child's unafraid gentleness does. Between the disemboweled tyrant and the blessed boy, the Purāṇas place the cycle's true thesis: the ferocity that guards the universe is finally governed by, and governs itself for, devotion.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Narasiṃha, Man-Lion Avatar, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Narasiṃha?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Narasiṃha is /narasimha/ — approximately 'NARASIMHA' — pronounce the restored form with attention to the preserved diacritics and length.

02What does Narasiṃha mean?

Narasiṃha means The Man-Lion — the fourth avatāra of Viṣṇu in the sanskrit tradition.

03What are the symbols of Narasiṃha?

Narasiṃha is associated with The man-lion form (The theriomorphic body that defeats the boon: leonine mane and claws joined to human torso and intelligence.), Claws (nakha) (Neither weapon nor hand — the talons that honor the letter of the boon while breaking its intent; the nails themselves are hymned (nakhastuti).), The pillar (stambha) (The palace column that conceals the god; in theology, the stambha is the imperishable inner support of all beings.), Twilight (sandhyā) (Neither day nor night — the loophole hour at which the law of the boon is honored and destroyed at once.), The serene child (Prahlāda standing unafraid beside the killer — devotion as the one power the man-lion will always answer.).

04Why restore Narasiṃha in Unicode?

Plain ASCII narasimha strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Narasiṃha?

“The Boon of Hiraṇyakaśipu” — The stories of Narasiṃha tell how Viṣṇu defeated a loophole-perfect boon: neither man nor beast could kill the tyrant, so the god came as both at once — a man-lion bursting from a palace pillar at twilight, on a threshold, with claws. The Purāṇic cycle turns on the devotion of the tyrant's own son. The full telling lives on the lore page.
