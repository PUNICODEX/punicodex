Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈjʌmə/ ‌— Sanskrit (IAST: Yama).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Ya- — Palatal y as in “young”; the a is short and open.
  • -ma — The name means “twin” — Yama is the twin of ​Yamī in the earliest tradition.

Mythology

Yama’s mythology moves from Vedic pathfinder to epic judge to Purāṇic king of hells. He begins as the twin who chose to ​die — the first of the race to walk the path — and becomes the stern accountant whose city the dead enter by the southern gate. Buddhism and the epics gave him ​his tortures and his buffalo; the Veda kept his dignity.

The Twin Dialogue (Rigveda 10.10)

The oldest Yama-text is a dialogue: Yama and his twin Yamī, the first two beings, and Yamī’s ‌love for her brother. Yama refuses — ‘the immortals watch’ — and the hymn becomes the charter of exogamy and of the incest-taboo itself, argued between the world’s first twins in the world’s ​first debate.

The Path of the Fathers (Rigveda 10.14)

The funeral hymn sends the dead king along the path Yama blazed, past the two dogs and the waters, to the feast of ‌the fathers where Yama waits with Varuṇa. ‘Leave the sin here, keep the glory’ — the formula of every Vedic funeral, spoken to the first traveller’s successor.

The King Under the Earth ​(Mahābhārata)

In the epic’s underworld-book, Yama rules below with his city Saṃyamanī, his account read by Citragupta, his noose and his buffalo — the god of justice who once disguised himself as ‌a crab to test a king. The epic makes him dreaded and fair: the judge who cannot be bribed because he already owns everything.

The Lord of the Narakas (Purāṇic Hell)

The ​Purāṇas enumerate eighty-four hells under Yama’s administration, each a precise torment for a precise sin — the liar’s tongue, the thief’s hands. Yet the same texts insist his justice is karma’s arithmetic, ‌not malice: even the tortures are purification, and release comes in time.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Yama concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement ​about the name:

  • The First Mortal — He died before anyone — the twin who found the path to the fathers and became its king.
  • The Noose — His pāśa, the noose, ‌finds every soul at its hour; none outrun it, though all may beg.
  • The Two Dogs — Śyāma and Śabala, four-eyed, follow his road and report the dead; they guard the way.
  • ​Judge of the Deeds — In the Mahābhārata and Purāṇas he reads the record of every soul — the karma, weighed without scales.

Across Cultures

The Avestan Yima, king of the golden ​age who kept death from his realm, is Yama’s Iranian twin — the same name, opposite function: where Yima postponed death, Yama administers it. Buddhism made Yama a dharmapāla, converted in the ​end by the Buddha’s teaching, and carried him east as Enma in Japan, where he still judges with his mirror and staff.

Kindred figures in the PuniCodex cross-tradition index include Hádēs (underworld ‌/ death), ꜣnpw (underworld / death), Kérberos (underworld / death), Ereškigal (underworld / death), Forseti (justice / law / truth), and Garmr (underworld / death).

Cultural Legacy

Yama is the ancestor of ​every Asian judge-of-the-dead: Enma-ō in Japan, Yŏmna in Korea, Yanluo in China — a continuous iconography from the Veda to manga. The word ‘Yamī’ survives in the river Yamunā; and the maxim ​‘even Yama’s writ runs by karma’ structures the moral imagination of half the world’s population.
