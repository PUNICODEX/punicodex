From original script to Unicode restoration

The name Amoghasiddhi reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the buddhist tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Sanskrit — amogha, 'unfailing, not vain' — the negated adjective of mogha, 'useless, fruitless' (Monier-Williams s.v.).
  • Sanskrit — siddhi, 'accomplishment, attainment, success' — from the root sādh, 'to accomplish', whence also sādhana, 'the means of accomplishment'.
  • Cousin words — siddha, 'the perfected one', and sādhaka, 'the practitioner' — the whole family of finishing what is begun.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Amoghasiddhi in Later Traditions

Amoghasiddhi's closest relative is Śiva, in a relationship of deliberate supersession: the root tantra has Vajrapāṇi of the karma family subdue Maheśvara on Mount Meru and trample him into the mandala as its guardian — conquest written as conversion. In East Asia the five-Buddha mandala traveled with esoteric Buddhism: in the Shingon school of Japan, Amoghasiddhi appears as Fukūjōju Nyorai, the 'Tathāgata of Infallible Success', enthroned in the north of the Diamond-Realm mandala preserved in the ninth-century scrolls of Tō-ji. His consort Samayatārā is simply the Green Tārā absorbed into the five-Buddha family scheme, so that the most beloved goddess of Tibetan devotion is also, doctrinally, his wisdom made feminine. Modern Western reception has been quieter than for his companions: he is known chiefly through museum galleries and meditation literature, where his name circulates as a compact promise — that accomplishment pursued without vanity does not fail.

Modern Legacy

Amoghasiddhi's legacy is liturgical rather than narrative, and vast in that register. He presides over the top registers of countless Nepalese paubha paintings and Tibetan thangkas, where his image is among the most standardized in Himalayan art — green, right hand raised, seated on a throne borne by garuḍas. In Japan the esoteric schools chant his mantra within the Kongōkai mandala rites performed at Tō-ji and Kōya-san since the ninth century, and Fukūjōju remains a familiar presence in temple statuary from Nara to Kamakura. His doctrine survives as a compact psychology: envy — the conviction that others accomplish while I do not — is transmuted into all-accomplishing action, because karma performed for others cannot fail. In contemporary dharma centers his name is invoked less often than Tārā's, but it answers a question hers does not: whether the effort itself is worth anything. His name is the vow that it is.

In the Earth

There are no ancient temples of Amoghasiddhi, no inscriptions naming him, no early coins: he appears first in manuscript and image. Palm-leaf copies of the Sarva-tathāgata-tattva-saṅgraha survive among the Newar Buddhist manuscripts of the Kathmandu Valley, and his figure becomes canonical in paubha painting from the twelfth century and in Tibetan thangkas from the thirteenth, where the five Buddhas crown the registers of monastery murals across the Himalaya. In East Asia the ninth-century Tō-ji mandala scrolls preserve him as Fukūjōju in the Kongōkai, among the oldest monumental images of the system. His material record is therefore neither a site nor a relic but a genre — the painted mandala, portable and reproducible, in which the north never lacks its green Buddha.

05

The Meditation

What Amoghasiddhi means — beyond the dictionary

Every tradition personifies its favorite anxiety. The Greeks made a goddess of ruin, the Norse of old age; Buddhism personified the fear of wasted effort. Amoghasiddhi is the answer to the question every practitioner asks in the dark middle of the work: is any of this actually working? His name is the vow — not vain, not useless, infallible.

Look at what his assigned affliction is: envy, the poison of comparison, the certainty that accomplishment lives in other people's hands. His teaching does not suppress that energy; it redirects it, because all-accomplishing wisdom acts for others, and action done selflessly for others cannot fail — there is no one left for it to fail in front of. The raised hand is not a trophy. It is a promise made to the frightened, by the one Buddha whose name is not a description but a guarantee.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Amoghasiddhi, Infallible Success, North, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Amoghasiddhi?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Amoghasiddhi is /amoghasiddhi/ — approximately Say 'amoghasiddhi' with the stress and length implied by the scholarly restoration 'Amoghasiddhi'.

02What does Amoghasiddhi mean?

Amoghasiddhi means 'Unfailing Accomplishment' — the fifth Dhyānibuddha, lord of the karma family in the buddhist tradition.

03What are the symbols of Amoghasiddhi?

Amoghasiddhi is associated with The double vajra (viśvavajra) (The crossed thunderbolt stamped on his throne base — unshakeable means, doubled for the four directions, the karma family's seal.), The abhaya mudrā (The raised right hand, palm outward: fearlessness offered as a teaching, not brandished as a triumph.), The garuḍa throne-bearers (The sovereign birds that carry his seat in the iconographic manuals — emblems of power subdued and enlisted.), Green (His body-color in the five-Buddha system: the hue of ripening action, of autumn and of deeds coming to fruit.), The wind (vāyu) (His element — movement and transmission, the deed carried outward to its object; he is the least static of the five.).

04Why restore Amoghasiddhi in Unicode?

Amoghasiddhi is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Amoghasiddhi?

“The Fifth from the One” — Amoghasiddhi has no biographical myths — he was never born, never walked the earth, never died. He belongs to the tantric synthesis of the early medieval period, when Mahāyāna Buddhism organized its meditation Buddhas into a mandala of five. His stories are the ritual and iconographic programs that grew around his name from the eighth century on, above all in the Sarva-tathāgata-tattva-saṅgraha, the root tantra of the system. The full telling lives on the lore page.

Religious Studies

  • Guhyasamāja Tantra, ch. III — the earliest systematic tantric mandala of the five Dhyānibuddhas, naming Amoghasiddhi in the northern seat of the karma family.
  • Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha (Tattvasaṃgraha Tantra) — the paradigmatic narrative of the five tathāgatas' subjugation of the unruly forces, with Amoghasiddhi presiding over the north, the wind element, and all-accomplishing wisdom.
  • Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti, v. 8 — the liturgical verse naming the five tathāgatas (Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi) that underlies the whole pentad system.
  • Benoytosh Bhattacharyya, An Introduction to Buddhist Esoterism (Oxford University Press, 1932) — early scholarly exposition of the five Dhyānibuddhas and the karma family.
  • David L. Snellgrove, Indo-Tibetan Buddhism: Indian Buddhists & Their Tibetan Successors (Shambhala, 1987) — standard modern account of the tantric Buddha-family system from which Amoghasiddhi's iconography derives.
  • John C. Huntington & Dina Bangdel, The Circle of Bliss: Buddhist Meditational Art (Columbus Museum of Art / Serindia, 2003) — museum exhibition catalogue documenting Amoghasiddhi and the five Buddha families in Indian and Tibetan art.
