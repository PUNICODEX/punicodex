The hidden history behind Beli

Some restorations rescue a name from the address bar; this one rescues a question from a footnote. Beli — Beli mawr, Beli the Great, son of Manogan — is the ancestor-king who stands at the head of the Mabinogion's British dynasties, the name to which the medieval Welsh royal pedigrees climb, and quite possibly the last Welsh shadow of a continental god whose altars called him 'the bright one'. The name arrives already whole, in plain Latin letters, with nothing to restore — and everything to explain. This post walks the full record: the four sons, the pedigrees, the Latin afterlife as Geoffrey's Heli, the Belenus debate, and the honest state of the address bar — beli.com is held outside the collection, and the temple stands, for now, without a domain of its own.

Overview

Beli (beli) — Light, Sun · Bright, shining — belongs to the Celtic tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Light, Sun". The name's traditional gloss is "Bright, shining", reached through its long-proposed connection with the continental god Belenos — a link the handbooks record as a possibility, not a proof.

Beli mawr uab Manogan is the king of the Island of Britain whose death begins the tale of Lludd and Llefelys: father of Lludd, Caswallawn, Nynniaw, and 'a fourth son called Llefelys'. The Second Branch watches his son Caswallawn take the crown from the children of Llŷr under a veil of illusion; the Harleian genealogies carry the royal lines of Wales back to him; Geoffrey of Monmouth Latinizes him as Heli, father of Lud, Cassibelaunus, and Nennius.

PuniCodex records the name as Beli, the attested manuscript spelling, identical in ASCII and Unicode — which places it in Tier 2. The plain-ASCII domain beli.com is registered to a third party and held outside the collection; it is watched for release, and this temple meanwhile stands without a domain of its own.

The Name

The name is attested in Middle Welsh as Beli, characteristically with the epithet — Beli mawr uab Manogan, 'Beli the Great, son of Manogan' — in the Mabinogion and in the genealogical tracts. Geoffrey of Monmouth writes it in Latin as Heli.

The traditional gloss is 'bright, shining', and it arrives by way of the continental theonym Belenos / Belinus — the Celtic Apollo of Tertullian and Herodian — conventionally derived from the root bhel-, 'to shine'. The link between the Welsh ancestor and the god is one of the oldest proposals in Celtic scholarship; the etymology of Belenos itself is disputed, and no text performs the identification. This temple records the gloss as the tradition hands it down, with the uncertainty attached.

Two cautions belong with the name. Geoffrey's other Belinus — son of Dunvallo, brother of Brennius — is a different figure who shares nothing with Beli Mawr but the name. And Beli was a living Welsh personal name: Beli son of Rhun stands in the Gwynedd pedigree, a dynast, not the ancestor-king.

Because the manuscripts already write the name in plain Latin letters, the ASCII form is not a flattening but the attested spelling itself: there is no diacritic to restore and no distinctive letter that ASCII loses.

The Original Script

The name is rendered in the Latin alphabet as Beli — the Middle Welsh manuscript form preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch (Aberystwyth, National Library of Wales, Peniarth 4–5, c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (Oxford, Jesus College MS 111, c. 1382–1410), the two codices on which every modern edition of the Mabinogion rests. The genealogies that make him famous survive in British Library, Harley MS 3859 (c. 1100).

Welsh names of this stratum were never carved in ogham or runes: the inscribed stones of early medieval Wales belong to a commemorative horizon that passes the mythological figures by, and no stone carries this name. The manuscript page is the primary and only witness — and on it, the name is already complete. The restoration's work here is not recovery but fidelity: Beli, spelled exactly as the scribes wrote it.

Pronunciation

The pronunciation of the name is /ˈbɛlɪ/ — Modern Welsh standard.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: BEL-ee — two syllables, stress on the first, the e open as in 'bed', never the long ay of 'bailey'.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Middle Welsh — Beli (mawr uab Manogan), the manuscript form
  • Latin — Heli, Geoffrey of Monmouth's form
  • Proposed cognate — Gaulish Belenos / Latin Belinus
  • Attested form — Beli

The first syllable opens with the voiced bilabial [b] and the open front vowel [ɛ]; the second closes on a clear [l] and the short [ɪ]. Two syllables, first one stressed — as compact in the mouth as the name is on the page.

Mythology

Beli has no adventure of his own: his story is his house. The tales know him as the dead king whose sons inherit the world; the pedigrees know him as the root.

The Four Sons of Beli

The tale of Lludd and Llefelys opens with the settlement of the island: Beli the Great, son of Manogan, was king of this island, and he had three sons — Lludd, Caswallawn, and Nynniaw — and, as the story tells, a fourth son called Llefelys. When Beli died, the kingdom of the Island of Britain fell to Lludd as the eldest, and Lludd ruled it prosperously, rebuilding the walls of London and living there most of the year. Llefelys went to France, won the old king's only daughter, and inherited that kingdom in his turn. What follows — the three plagues, the brothers' counsel shouted across the Channel — belongs to the sons. Beli's part is finished in the opening lines, which is precisely his function: the point from which legitimacy flows.

Caswallawn's Veil

The Second Branch remembers Beli's house at the moment it takes the crown. While Brân the Blessed marched into Ireland to avenge Branwen, he left seven regents in Britain, his son Caradog at their head. Caswallawn son of Beli marched against them wearing a veil of illusion, so that no one could see him — only his sword killing. He slew the seven; Caradog died of a broken heart; and Caswallawn son of Beli became king in London. The branch never explains or excuses the usurpation: while the children of Llŷr spent themselves overseas, the children of Beli took the island — the two great houses passing the crown between them without a battle anyone could see.

The Root of the Royal Trees

Outside the tales, the record that made Beli matter was parchment of a drier kind. The Harleian genealogies carry the pedigrees of the British kingdoms back generation by generation until they reach Beli mawr, who stands near the root of the trees. To claim a throne in early Wales was in part to publish a pedigree, and the pedigrees converge on him; the convention held for centuries in the genealogical tracts. He is the standing proof that in the British tradition, ancestry is not decoration on kingship — it is the thing itself.

Heli, Father of Lud

Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) took the Welsh Beli into Latin and made him a chapter of world history. As Heli he fathers three sons: Lud, Cassibelaunus, and Nennius. Lud succeeds him and rebuilds Trinovantum with noble walls, renaming it Kaerlud — whence, Geoffrey says, London, and the gate called Ludgate. Through the Historia, one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages, Beli's house entered the bloodstream of European letters.

The Bright One Behind the King

Older than any Welsh manuscript is a continental witness the scholars keep setting beside his name. Around the year 200, Tertullian lists the local gods of the provinces and gives the Noricans Belenus; a generation later, Herodian tells how, at the siege of Aquileia in 238, the citizens credited their deliverance to Apollo, the god the region worshipped under the name Belis. The altars agree: dedications Belino Augusto sacrum cluster at Aquileia and across the eastern Alpine provinces. Whether the Welsh ancestor descends from this 'bright, shining' god is the standing question of Beli scholarship — and the honest verdict is the one the handbooks give: a venerable possibility, unproven. This temple holds the question open.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography of Beli is institutional rather than mythic — the regalia of a founding king:

  • The Island of Britain — the single realm he held and his sons inherited; kingship imagined whole before it was ever divided
  • The family tree — the pedigrees of the Welsh dynasties growing back to one root
  • The veil of illusion — Caswallawn's invisible cloak, the house of Beli taking the crown unseen
  • The walled city — London, rebuilt by his son Lludd, the dynasty's memory fixed in stone and street-names
  • The sun's brightness — the traditional gloss of the name, carried from the Belenos inscriptions into the Welsh ancestor

No ancient image of Beli survives; his iconography is genealogical, not cultic. The tradition left him no statue — only a place at the root of every royal tree in Wales.

Archaeology & Evidence

Beli has no archaeological footprint: no inscription, image, or votive object from Britain names him, and the inscribed stones of early medieval Wales carry no reference to the ancestor-king. His evidence is manuscript evidence — the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest for the tales, Harley MS 3859 for the pedigrees. The material record that matters belongs to his proposed continental cognate: the dedications Belino Augusto sacrum from Aquileia and the eastern Alps are real stones with a real theonym — the strongest archaeological fact in the whole dossier, and comparative evidence only.

Realm & Domain

Beli's domain is catalogued as Light, Sun — the name's traditional solar reading, inherited from the 'bright, shining' god behind it. His attested role is something adjacent: the light at the root of the tree rather than a god in the sky.

Light

The traditional gloss reaches Beli through Belenos, the continental god the Latin sources equated with Apollo. Whether the Welsh ancestor descends from the god is unproven, but the gloss is old, and it fits the figure's function: he is the name the whole tradition is lit from.

Sun

No Welsh text gives Beli a solar myth — no chariot, no journey, no setting. The solar reading lives entirely in the etymology and in the continental altars. What the Welsh record offers instead is the genealogical counterpart of a sun: the single source from which the royal lines radiate.

The Kingship the Domain Implies

What the texts actually give Beli is the Island of Britain: its crown, its walled city, and the sons who inherit both. If a solar god stands behind him, the Welsh tradition has long since dimmed the god into the ancestor — and the ancestor is the figure the record can defend.

Across Cultures

Beli's cross-cultural argument is the Belenos question, and it runs in three directions at once. East to the continent: the Celtic Apollo of the Aquileian altars and the Norican provinces, 'the bright one' of the inscriptions. West to Ireland, where O'Rahilly proposed that the ancestor-figure Bile — whose name is also the Irish word for a sacred tree — preserves the same original: three literatures, three ancestors, one dimmed god at the root. And within Wales, to the other fountainheads of dynasty: Dôn, mother of the Children of Dôn, and the house of Llŷr, whose sons Brân and Manawydan lose the island's crown to Beli's son in the Second Branch. PuniCodex claims no identification in any direction: the comparison is the handbooks', and they mark it unproven.

Cultural Legacy

Beli's afterlife is genealogical before it is literary. The medieval pedigrees made descent from Beli Mawr the deepest claim a British dynasty could print, and the convention outlived the Middle Ages: Tudor and Stuart antiquaries, tracing the crown to British and Trojan roots, found Beli waiting at the root of the trees Geoffrey had Latinized. Through Geoffrey's Heli he fathered the legend of London itself — Lud's town, Lud's gate, the name written into the city's streets — and through his son Cassibelaunus he stands two generations above Shakespeare's Cymbeline, whose Britain is still Beli's island. In modern Wales the name persists quietly: Beli remains a Welsh personal name, and Beli Mawr keeps his place in the national memory as the grandfather of kings.

The Scholarly Record

The account of Beli given in this edition rests on the witnesses and reference works listed below. The Mabinogion supplies the narrative, in Sioned Davies's Oxford translation; Bartrum's Early Welsh Genealogical Tracts and Phillimore's printing of the Harley 3859 genealogies secure the pedigrees; Geoffrey of Monmouth, in the Reeve–Wright edition, supplies the Latin afterlife; Tertullian and Herodian supply the continental comparison. On the name itself the record is honestly uncertain: the Belenos connection and the gloss 'bright, shining' are the tradition's own proposal, recorded here with the handbooks' verdict attached — unproven.

A Meditation

Beli is the patron of everything that matters most and does least: the dead father, the founding reign, the name at the root of the tree. He fights no monster, wins no bride, casts no spell; the Mabinogion finishes him in a sentence, and then builds a world on him. That is how ancestry actually works — the founder is never the hero of the story, only its premise. Beli asks the genealogist's hard question of every institution and every family: not what have you done, but what are you descended from, and does the root still hold. Some roots run deeper than the record can follow. Beli is the place where Britain's goes dark.

The Unicode Restoration

Beli is classified as Tier 2, and the standard is mechanical rather than editorial: the name arrives in the Latin alphabet, without diacritics, in the manuscripts themselves, so the restoration preserves nothing ASCII loses. There is no thorn to rescue, no macron to replace — only a spelling to keep faithful. That is the point of this temple: a restoration project earns its keep not only where marks were stripped, but where the record must be told honestly — a debated etymology recorded as debated, a manuscript spelling transmitted unchanged, a famous ancestor left exactly as the scribes wrote him.

Why This Restoration Matters

In 2026 the stakes are practical. Search indexes, language models, and localization pipelines all inherit whatever spelling and whatever claims the web normalizes — which means every lexicon page is also a training signal. Beli teaches the machinery two things at once: that the attested spelling is plain Latin letters, and that the 'bright, shining' gloss is a tradition, not a proof — a distinction the internet tends to sand off. The infrastructure decides which names the future sees and how confidently it mis-describes them; this temple makes sure the future sees the ancestor-king's whole name, and the open question attached to it.
