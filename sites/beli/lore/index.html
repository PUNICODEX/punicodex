The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Beli

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Beli is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Bright, shining”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

beli

The plain beli form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Beli

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Beli. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Beli.com → beli.com

The non-ASCII characters in Beli are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Beli.

Pronunciation

How Beli was spoken

/ˈbɛlɪ/ Modern Welsh

Modern Approximation

BEL-ee — two syllables, stress on the first; the e is the open e of 'bed', never the long ay of 'bailey'.

Etymological Kin

  • Middle Welsh Beli mawr uab Manogan, 'Beli the Great, son of Manogan' — the Mabinogion form
  • Latin Heli — Geoffrey of Monmouth's form of the name in the Historia Regum Britanniae
  • Proposed cognate Gaulish Belenos / Latin Belinus, the 'bright one' of the continental inscriptions — the link is traditional, not proven
  • Attested form Beli — plain ASCII, identical to the manuscript spelling

The Accent / Script Rule

Middle Welsh Beli (Beli mawr uab Manogan, 'Beli the Great, son of Manogan') is already written in the Latin alphabet, so the ASCII form loses nothing: the name is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule, since the restoration preserves no diacritic or distinctive letter that ASCII drops. The traditional gloss 'bright, shining' reaches the name through its long-proposed connection with the Celtic god Belenos — conventionally derived from the root *bhel-, 'to shine' — but the etymology of Belenos itself is debated, and the handbooks record the link as a possibility, not a proof.

05

The Root of the Pedigrees

The Island of Britain, the Four Sons, and the Question of the Bright One

Beli mawr — Beli the Great, son of Manogan — is the deep ancestor of British kingship: the king of the Island of Britain whom the Mabinogion sets at the head of its dynasties, father of Lludd, Caswallawn, Nynniaw, and Llefelys, and the name to which the medieval Welsh royal pedigrees climb. Behind the king, scholarship has long glimpsed a god — the continental Belenos, the 'bright, shining' one — but the texts give us the ancestor, and the ancestor is what the record can defend.

The Island of Britain

Beli's realm in Lludd a Llefelys: the whole island, 'the crown of London' its seat — the deep past of a single British kingship.

The Four Sons

Lludd, Caswallawn, Nynniaw — and, 'as the story tells', a fourth, Llefelys: the branches of the house the whole tradition descends from.

The Pedigree

The Harleian genealogies carry the royal houses of Wales back to Beli Mawr — the name at the root of the British family trees.

The Bright One

The proposed god behind the king: Belenos, 'bright, shining' — the continental name Tertullian and the Aquileia altars attest, linked to Beli by a long scholarly tradition.

Sacred Symbols

The Island of Britain The single realm Beli held and his sons inherited — kingship imagined whole before it was ever divided
The family tree The pedigrees of the Welsh dynasties growing back to one root — ancestry as the ground of legitimacy
The veil of illusion Caswallawn's invisible cloak in Branwen — the house of Beli taking the crown unseen
The walled city Lludd's rebuilding of London — Lud's town, the memory of the dynasty fixed in stone and street-names

06

Mythology

Stories of Beli

Beli has no adventure of his own: his story is his house. The Mabinogion knows him as the dead king whose four sons inherit Britain, France, and the quarrels of the next generation; the pedigrees know him as the root; and Geoffrey of Monmouth knows him as Heli, father of the builder of London.

Mabinogion (Lludd a Llefelys)

The Four Sons of Beli

The tale of Lludd and Llefelys opens with the settlement of the island: Beli the Great, son of Manogan, was king of this island, and he had three sons — Lludd, Caswallawn, and Nynniaw — and, as the story tells, a fourth son called Llefelys. When Beli died, the kingdom of the Island of Britain fell to Lludd as the eldest, and Lludd ruled it prosperously, rebuilding the walls of London and living there for most of the year. Llefelys, the youngest, went to France, won the love of the old king's only daughter, and inherited that kingdom in his turn.

What follows — the three plagues that fall on Britain, and Llefelys's counsel from the middle of the Channel — belongs to the sons' temples. Beli's part is finished in the opening lines, and that is precisely his function: he is the point from which legitimacy flows, the dead father whose reign makes his sons' divided world one family.

Mabinogion (Branwen ferch Llŷr)

Caswallawn's Veil

The Second Branch remembers Beli's house at the moment it takes the crown. While Brân the Blessed marched into Ireland to avenge Branwen, he left seven men as regents in Britain, his son Caradog at their head. Caswallawn son of Beli marched against them wearing a veil of illusion, so that no one could see him — only his sword killing. He slew the seven, and Caradog died of a broken heart at the sight of the slaughter and the knowledge of who held the island now.

Caswallawn son of Beli became king in London. The branch never explains or excuses the usurpation; it simply registers that while the children of Llŷr spent themselves overseas, the children of Beli took the island — the two great houses of Welsh tradition passing the crown between them without a battle anyone could see.

Harleian Genealogies

The Root of the Royal Trees

Outside the tales, the record that made Beli matter was parchment of a drier kind. The Harleian genealogies — the collection of Welsh royal pedigrees preserved in British Library, Harley MS 3859 (c. 1100) — carry the lines of the British kingdoms back generation by generation until they reach Beli mawr, who stands near the root of the trees. To claim a throne in early Wales was, in part, to publish a pedigree, and the pedigrees converge on him.

The convention was old and tenacious: centuries later, the genealogical tracts still counted descent from Beli the Great as the deepest legitimacy a Welsh royal house could print. He is the standing proof that in the British tradition, ancestry is not decoration on kingship — it is the thing itself.

Geoffrey of Monmouth

Heli, Father of Lud

Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) took the Welsh Beli into Latin and made him a chapter of world history. As Heli he reigns after King Cligueillus, a man of forty years' rule, and fathers three sons: Lud, Cassibelaunus, and Nennius. Lud succeeds him and rebuilds the city of Trinovantum with noble walls, surrounding it with countless towers and renaming it Kaerlud, Lud's City — whence, Geoffrey says, its later name London, and the memory of the king in the gate called Ludgate.

Through Geoffrey, Beli's house entered the bloodstream of European letters: the Historia was one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages, and the sons of Heli — Lud the city-builder, Cassibelaunus the enemy of Caesar — carried their father's ancestry wherever Latin was read.

Tertullian / Herodian

The Bright One Behind the King

Older than any Welsh manuscript is a continental witness the scholars keep setting beside Beli's name. Around the year 200, Tertullian lists the local gods of the provinces and gives the Noricans Belenus (Apologeticum 24); a generation later, Herodian tells how, at the siege of Aquileia in 238, the citizens credited their deliverance to Apollo, the god the people of that region worshipped under the name Belis. The altars agree: dedications Belino Augusto sacrum cluster at Aquileia and across the eastern Alpine provinces.

Whether the Welsh ancestor-king descends from this 'bright, shining' god is the standing question of Beli scholarship. The names are close, the traditional gloss fits, and a euhemerized god makes a plausible root for a genealogy — but no text performs the identification, and the honest verdict is the one the handbooks give: a venerable possibility, unproven. This temple holds the question open.

Extended Lore

Beli is the patron of everything that matters most and does least: the dead father, the founding reign, the name at the root of the tree. He fights no monster, wins no bride, casts no spell; the Mabinogion finishes him in a sentence, and then builds a world on him. His sons inherit his island and quarrel over it; his house takes the crown under a veil of illusion; the pedigrees spend a thousand years climbing back to him. That is how ancestry actually works — the founder is never the hero of the story, only its premise. Beli asks the genealogist's hard question of every institution and every family: not what have you done, but what are you descended from, and does the root still hold. And the name itself holds its own question: whether the bright one, the shining god of the continental altars, lies behind the sober Welsh king. Some roots run deeper than the record can follow. Beli is the place where Britain's goes dark.
