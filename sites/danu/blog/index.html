Mythology

Danu’s mythology is peculiar: she is everywhere in the name and nowhere ​in the stories. The Tuatha Dé Danann — her peoples — fill the Mythological Cycle’s battles and sorceries, but their mother never takes the stage. Her biography is her ​children’s: the coming from the northern islands, the four treasures, the battles of Mag Tuired, and the fading into the sídhe, the hollow hills where the divine race retired ‌when the sons of Míl took Ireland.

The Peoples of Danu Arrive (Lebor Gabála)

The Book of Invasions tells it as history: the Tuatha Dé Danann came from the northern ​islands in dark clouds, burned their ships behind them on the Irish shore, and contended for the island against the Fir Bolg and the Fomoiri. From each of the ‌four cities they brought a treasure — the stone of Fál, the spear of Lugh, the sword of Nuadu, the cauldron of the Dagda — the four regalia of ​the divine race, each a gift of Danu’s line.

The Battles of Mag Tuired (Cath Maige Tuired)

The race’s great war-cycle pits them against the Fomoiri, the sea-demons of ‌chaos: Nuadu’s silver arm, Lugh’s many skills, the Dagda’s bottomless cauldron, and the Morrígan’s prophecy carry the day. Danu’s name stands over the whole cycle — the mother’s eponym on ​every battle-line, though she herself never draws a sword in the telling.

The Retirement into the Hills (Sídhe Tradition)

When the sons of Míl defeated the Dé Danann, the ‌race did not die but withdrew — into the sídhe, the hollow hills of Ireland, where they keep their beauty and their treasure and their grievance, and from where ​they still interfere, bless, and curse. Danu’s peoples became the fairies of Irish tradition; their mother’s name contracted into the fairy-mounds’ oldest title.

The Danube’s Sister (River-Etymology)

The philology ‌is the mythology here: *dānu, ‘the flowing one’, names rivers from the Danube to the Don to the Dnieper — the Indo-European river-mother’s name scattered across the map like water-systems. ​Irish Danu is the westernmost daughter of the family; her son the Dagda’s name has been read as ‘the good god’, the river’s generous flood made divine.

Across Cultures

Danu is the Celtic branch of the Indo-European river-mother ‌— kin to the Vedic Danu, mother of the demon Vṛtra in the Rigveda’s oldest combat, across the very language-family the names share. The Tuatha Dé Danann were euhemerized ​by the medieval Irish scholars into a people of sorcerers; the modern Celtic revival restored them to goddess-worship, with Danu as the primordial name.

Within the Celtic tradition, closely ‌related names in the corpus include Aengus, Annwn, Arawn, Badb, Balor, and Bedwyr.

Cultural Legacy

Her name survives in the Tuatha Dé Danann’s — the divine race of every Irish ​legend of consequence — and in the river-network of Europe, the Danube most of all. The modern Druidic and Celtic-revival movements take Danu as the mother-goddess of their pantheon; ​and the sídhe-tradition she eponymizes is the ancestor of the world’s entire fairy-canon.
