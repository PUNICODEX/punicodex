The name Makemake reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the polynesian tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Hawaiian — makemake, 'to want, to desire, to wish' — the exact Polynesian cognate, still the ordinary Hawaiian verb (Pukui & Elbert, Hawaiian Dictionary, s.v. makemake)
  • Rapa Nui — make, reduplicated as a divine name; the lexicon's gloss 'to see, to know' follows the meaning recorded for the god in Rapa Nui tradition (Englert, Diccionario Rapanui–Español, s.v. make)
  • Tradition pair — Tangaroa — the sea-god named beside Makemake in the recorded Rapa Nui pantheon (Métraux, Ethnology of Easter Island)

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Makemake in Later Traditions

Makemake had no classical syncretism — there was no neighboring pantheon on the loneliest island in the world. His transformation came with catastrophe: after the Peruvian slave raids and epidemics of the 1860s, the survivors embraced Catholicism, and the creator god was absorbed into the image of the Christian God or quietly retired to the petroglyphs. Comparison with other Polynesian traditions remains the useful lens: Tahiti's Ta'aroa and the wide Tangaroa-complex of the eastern Pacific give the intellectual frame for understanding how a high creator-god sits above a crowd of specialist spirits and ancestor-deities. In the modern world the identification has run in reverse — the dwarf planet Makemake, named in 2008 after the Easter Island god, has carried the name of a Polynesian creator into every astronomy textbook, a rare case of a colonial-era suppressed deity colonizing the heavens.

Modern Legacy

The birdman's egg became the island's flag: the Tapati Rapa Nui festival, held each February, stages canoe races and dance competitions in which the old birdman legend is the centerpiece, and the winner's crown echoes the tangata manu's year of rule. Orongo, restored as a ceremonial village, anchors the UNESCO-listed Rapa Nui National Park. In 2008 the International Astronomical Union named the dwarf planet Makemake — one of the largest objects in the Kuiper belt — after the god, explicitly honoring Easter Island's cultural heritage. Meanwhile the Rapa Nui language, once nearly extinct, is taught again in island schools, and with it the old names return to living mouths. A tradition recorded from the last survivors of a catastrophe has become the founding charter of a cultural renaissance.

In the Earth

The material evidence is concentrated at Orongo, the ceremonial village on the Rano Kau crater rim: some fifty stone houses and over a thousand petroglyphs, including the wide-eyed Makemake faces and birdman figures documented by Routledge (1914–15) and catalogued by Georgia Lee (1992). Several carved slabs were removed to museums in the 1970s after weathering and vandalism; the village was consolidated under William Mulloy's restoration program. The islets of Motu Nui, Motu Iti and Motu Kao Kao below the cliffs preserve the contest's arena; Rano Raraku's quarry and the ahu platforms (notably Ahu Tongariki) form the wider sacred landscape. The Rapa Nui National Park has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1995.

The Meditation

What Makemake means — beyond the dictionary

Makemake is the god of the watching face: on an island of 165 square kilometers, where every horizon ends in ocean, creation was a matter of sight — the god who sees, the god who knows. His cult turned that metaphysics into sport: each year the whole society wagered its fertility on who could first see, and bring back, a single egg. The birdman contest is among the most beautiful institutions in the ethnographic record — an election without ballots, a kingship without war, legitimacy conferred by a seabird's arithmetic. And the lesson of the silence matters as much as the contest: traditions recorded from the last elders, like the chants Métraux wrote down in 1934, are not museum pieces but threads — cut, they take whole cosmologies with them. Makemake's staring eyes at Orongo are a reminder that being watched is the oldest form of being known.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Makemake, Creator, Fertility, Bird-Man, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Makemake?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Makemake is /makemake/ — approximately Say 'makemake' with the stress and length implied by the scholarly restoration 'Makemake'.

02What does Makemake mean?

Makemake means To see, to know in the polynesian tradition.

03What are the symbols of Makemake?

Makemake is associated with The sooty tern's egg (The unbroken egg carried from Motu Nui by the swimmer: Makemake's annual sign, the seed of the birdman's year of sacred rule.), The wide-eyed face (Makemake's carved visage at Orongo, eyes hugely enlarged: the god as watcher — seeing and knowing everything that happens below, the lexicon's gloss made stone.), Frigatebird (manu) (The great sea-bird of the contest islets; manu, 'bird', is the root of tangata manu, the birdman himself, and of the god's lordship over the flocks.), Rano Kau crater (The great volcanic bowl above Orongo whose rim is the ceremonial stage of the birdman cult — the island's sacred theater, open to the sea.), Aku aku (The guardian spirits of the caves in the recorded lore — attendants of Makemake's hidden realm, keepers of what the island buried.).

04Why restore Makemake in Unicode?

Makemake is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Makemake?

“The Creator in the Chants” — Makemake is the creator god of Rapa Nui (Easter Island): father of fertility, lord of the seabirds, and the hidden face behind the island's birdman cult. The sources are late — the tradition was recorded in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, after the population's collapse — and they must be read as the memory of a wounded tradition. The full telling lives on the lore page.
