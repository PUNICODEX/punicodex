Mythology

Stories of Makemake

Makemake is the creator god of Rapa Nui (Easter Island): father of fertility, lord of the seabirds, and the hidden face behind the island's birdman cult. The sources are late — the tradition was recorded in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, after the population's collapse — and they must be read as the memory of a wounded tradition.

Creation

The Creator in the Chants

When the first ethnographers asked the islanders who made the world, the answer was Makemake. Alfred Métraux, working on Rapa Nui in 1934–35, recorded chants in which Makemake appears at the beginning of things as the great god, the maker of humankind, attended by the aku aku, the guardian spirits of the caves; Katherine Routledge heard related accounts during the Mana expedition of 1914–15. The surviving material is fragmentary — Peruvian slave raids and smallpox had shattered the population in the 1860s, carrying off the initiated experts, and much of the theology survived in the mouths of a handful of elders. In the recorded lore Makemake stands at the head of the pantheon, paired with the sea-god Tangaroa. What can be said with confidence is this: on the most isolated inhabited island on earth, creation was not a distant cosmic event but a local one, governed by a god whose staring face was carved into the living stone of the island itself.

Tangata Manu

The Birdman Contest

Each spring the men of Rapa Nui gathered at Orongo, the stone village on the rim of the Rano Kau crater, to compete for the title of tangata manu — the birdman. Swimmers called hopu descended the sea cliffs and crossed shark-filled water to the islet of Motu Nui, there to await the sooty tern's first egg of the season. The sponsor whose hopu returned first with an unbroken egg won: he was shaved, painted red and black, given the egg to wear against his body, and seated for a year in sacred seclusion as Makemake's chosen — the year's lord of fertility, whose fortune was read in the fortunes of land and sea. Métraux reconstructed the full ritual cycle from informants' testimony, and Routledge saw the last performances in 1914. The contest means that on an island without kingship, legitimacy came not from conquest but from a god-given sign: the birdman's egg was the island's crown, and Makemake alone conferred it.

Iconography

The Eyes of Orongo

The village of Orongo is itself Makemake's great icon. Its flat lava slabs are covered with petroglyphs — birdmen with human faces and birds' bodies, frigatebirds, Komari fertility symbols, and above all the staring face of Makemake himself, the eyes hugely enlarged, peering from the rock beside the contest grounds. Georgia Lee's survey, The Rock Art of Easter Island (1992), catalogued some twelve hundred designs and showed the wide-eyed faces clustered at the very village where the tangata manu contest was held: the god carved as witness. Métraux had already recorded that the islanders named these faces as Makemake's. The image means that knowledge is sight — the lexicon's gloss of the name, 'to see, to know', is literally carved at Orongo, where the god's whole function is to watch, and where being seen by Makemake is the entire power of the ceremony.

Survival

The Silence and the Return

The cult did not survive contact intact. Peruvian slavers carried off around a thousand islanders in 1862–63, including the last initiated experts; smallpox followed; by 1868 the survivors stood for mass baptism. Métraux heard the birdman cycle from men who had never seen it performed, and Routledge found Orongo already standing empty, its sacred houses abandoned. Yet the name never died. In the later twentieth century the Tapati Rapa Nui festival began staging the birdman legend again — canoe races, body-painting, and the choosing of a festival queen — and the tangata manu story became the emblem of the islanders' revived identity. In 2008 the astronomers who found the bright dwarf planet beyond Pluto gave it Makemake's name. The history means that a god once reduced to a stone face watched over the slow return of the people who had made him: the watcher outlasted the silence.

Makemake

Creator

Makemake is the creator god of Rapa Nui — Easter Island: father of fertility, lord of the seabirds, and the hidden face behind the island's birdman cult. His carved eyes watch from the stone village of Orongo, where each spring a contest chose the tangata manu, the man who bore the god's sign for a year.

Tangata Manu

The birdman of the Orongo contest — the year's chosen lord, raised by Makemake's sign: the first sooty-tern egg brought unbroken from Motu Nui.

Creator of Rapa Nui

Head of the island's recorded pantheon: maker of humankind, father of fertility, lord of the manu, the birds on which the island's life depended.

Orongo

The stone village on the rim of the Rano Kau crater, where hundreds of petroglyphs carry Makemake's enormous staring face.

Tapati Rapa Nui

The island's annual festival, where the birdman legend is staged again — the tradition's living return after the nineteenth-century silence.

Sacred Symbols

The sooty tern's egg The unbroken egg carried from Motu Nui by the swimmer: Makemake's annual sign, the seed of the birdman's year of sacred rule.
The wide-eyed face Makemake's carved visage at Orongo, eyes hugely enlarged: the god as watcher — seeing and knowing everything that happens below, the lexicon's gloss made stone.
Frigatebird (manu) The great sea-bird of the contest islets; manu, 'bird', is the root of tangata manu, the birdman himself, and of the god's lordship over the flocks.
Rano Kau crater The great volcanic bowl above Orongo whose rim is the ceremonial stage of the birdman cult — the island's sacred theater, open to the sea.
Aku aku The guardian spirits of the caves in the recorded lore — attendants of Makemake's hidden realm, keepers of what the island buried.

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Makemake

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Makemake is the standard Polynesian romanisation, documented in academic sources — “To see, to know”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual polynesian names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

The plain makemake form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Extended Lore

Makemake is the god of the watching face: on an island of 165 square kilometers, where every horizon ends in ocean, creation was a matter of sight — the god who sees, the god who knows. His cult turned that metaphysics into sport: each year the whole society wagered its fertility on who could first see, and bring back, a single egg. The birdman contest is among the most beautiful institutions in the ethnographic record — an election without ballots, a kingship without war, legitimacy conferred by a seabird's arithmetic. And the lesson of the silence matters as much as the contest: traditions recorded from the last elders, like the chants Métraux wrote down in 1934, are not museum pieces but threads — cut, they take whole cosmologies with them. Makemake's staring eyes at Orongo are a reminder that being watched is the oldest form of being known.
