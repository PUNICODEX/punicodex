The Creator in the Chants (Creation)

When the first ethnographers asked the islanders who made the world, the answer was Makemake. Alfred Métraux, ​working on Rapa Nui in 1934–35, recorded chants in which Makemake appears at the beginning of things as the great god, the maker of humankind, attended by the aku aku, the guardian spirits ‌of the caves; Katherine Routledge heard related accounts during the Mana expedition of 1914–15. The surviving material is fragmentary — Peruvian slave raids and smallpox had shattered the population in the 1860s, carrying ​off the initiated experts, and much of the theology survived in the mouths of a handful of elders. In the recorded lore Makemake stands at the head of the pantheon, paired with the ‌sea-god Tangaroa. What can be said with confidence is this: on the most isolated inhabited island on earth, creation was not a distant cosmic event but a local one, governed by a ​god whose staring face was carved into the living stone of the island itself.

The Birdman Contest (Tangata Manu)

Each spring the men of Rapa Nui gathered at Orongo, the stone village on ‌the rim of the Rano Kau crater, to compete for the title of tangata manu — the birdman. Swimmers called hopu descended the sea cliffs and crossed shark-filled water to the islet of ​Motu Nui, there to await the sooty tern's first egg of the season. The sponsor whose hopu returned first with an unbroken egg won: he was shaved, painted red and black, given the ‌egg to wear against his body, and seated for a year in sacred seclusion as Makemake's chosen — the year's lord of fertility, whose fortune was read in the fortunes of land and ​sea. Métraux reconstructed the full ritual cycle from informants' testimony, and Routledge saw the last performances in 1914. The contest means that on an island without kingship, legitimacy came not from conquest but from ‌a god-given sign: the birdman's egg was the island's crown, and Makemake alone conferred it.

The Eyes of Orongo (Iconography)

The village of Orongo is itself Makemake's great icon. Its flat lava slabs ​are covered with petroglyphs — birdmen with human faces and birds' bodies, frigatebirds, Komari fertility symbols, and above all the staring face of Makemake himself, the eyes hugely enlarged, peering from the rock ​beside the contest grounds. Georgia Lee's survey, The Rock Art of Easter Island (1992), catalogued some twelve hundred designs and showed the wide-eyed faces clustered at the very village where the tangata manu ‌contest was held: the god carved as witness. Métraux had already recorded that the islanders named these faces as Makemake's. The image means that knowledge is sight — the lexicon's gloss of the ‌name, 'to see, to know', is literally carved at Orongo, where the god's whole function is to watch, and where being seen by Makemake is the entire power of the ceremony.
