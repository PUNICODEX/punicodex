Pronunciation

How Mꜥ was spoken

/'maːʕ/ Egyptian Reconstruction
m- Voiced bilabial nasal — the m of Egyptian, the closed lips that hum before the sound breaks free. It is the sound of closed eyes in meditation, the moment before sight — a soft, resonant hum that vibrates in the chest like the first note of a hymn.
-ꜥ Egyptian alef — a glottal stop or voiced pharyngeal fricative depending on period and dialect. In Old Egyptian, the ꜣ (vulture) represented a liquid or fricative sound. It opens the throat, clears the airway, prepares the mouth for the final, deepest sound. The breath before the vision.
-ꜥ Pharyngeal fricative — the ayin, a voiced sound produced deep in the throat, where the root of the tongue presses against the pharynx. No modern European language has this sound. It is the hum of creation, the vibration of the primordial waters, the voice of the god who spoke the world into being. To say it correctly is to feel the throat open like an eye toward the light.

Modern Approximation

"MAH-ah" — with the second syllable produced deep in the throat, almost a growl, a vibration that you feel rather than hear clearly. The m is soft, resonant. The ꜥ is the challenge — it is the sound of clearing your throat while voicing, of humming from the chest. In practice, most scholars simply say "maa" with a throaty final vowel.

Mythology

Stories of Mꜥ

The Eye

The Eye of Ra

The Eye of Ra — the sun's disk — is a powerful, independent being. It is not merely an organ of sight but a goddess in its own right: Wadjet, the cobra, the flame that strikes from above. One day, Ra grew weary of mankind's plots and whispers. He sent his Eye to punish them. The Eye left him in anger, blazing across the sky, and Ra could not call it back. It had its own will, its own hunger, its own judgment. He had to send Thoth — the god of wisdom, the tongue that speaks true names — to find the Eye and persuade it to return. When it returned, it brought transformation and renewal. The eye that sees is the eye that changes. It does not merely observe. It acts. It judges. It transforms.

The Journey

The Distant Goddess

The Eye of Ra wandered far — to Nubia, to the southern lands, to the desert beyond the borders of Egypt. It took the form of a lioness: Tefnut in some tellings, Sekhmet in others. The lioness of the sun, burning with rage and divine fire, far from home, far from the god who had sent her forth. Ra sent Shu, the air, and Thoth, the tongue, to bring her back. The journey was long. The desert was vast. And in the traveling, something changed. The lioness's rage cooled into benevolence. Her destructive fire became protective warmth. She returned not as a weapon but as a guardian. Perception matures through experience. The eye that wanders far sees more than the eye that stays at home. Distance brings clarity. Journey brings wisdom. The goddess who returns is not the goddess who left.

The Return

The Wedjat's Return

Horus, the sky god, the divine falcon whose eyes are the sun and moon, faced Set in their eternal battle. Set tore out Horus's left eye — the eye of the moon, the eye of perception, the eye of — and scattered it into pieces. Six pieces. Or fourteen. Or sixty-four, in some tellings. The eye was shattered. Sight was broken. The cosmos trembled. But Thoth came. Thoth, the ibis-headed god of wisdom and writing, the measurer and restorer, gathered every piece. He reassembled the eye, piece by piece, fraction by fraction. He made it whole. The restored eye — the wedjat — became the most powerful symbol in all Egypt. It was the amulet worn by the living and placed upon mummies. It was the measure of offerings, the standard of value, the symbol of healing and wholeness. Perception can be shattered. But perception can be restored. And the restored eye sees more clearly than the eye that was never broken.
