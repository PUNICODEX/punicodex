The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

伊邪那岐

The name in its original Japanese form. Izanagi (伊邪那岐) is attested in the source tradition — “He who invites”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

izanagi

The plain izanagi form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Izanagi

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Izanagi. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Izanagi.com → izanagi.com

The non-ASCII characters in Izanagi are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Izanagi.

Pronunciation

How Izanagi was spoken

/i.za.na.ɡi/ Modern Standard Japanese (Hepburn)
I- Close front [i]
-za- Voiced alveolar fricative [z] plus open central [a]
-na- Alveolar nasal [n] plus open central [a]
-gi Voiced velar stop [ɡ] plus close front [i]; from 岐 'invite'

Modern Approximation

'EE-zah-nah-gee' — the 'g' is hard, and the 'r' of Western transcriptions is absent in Japanese.

Etymological Kin

  • Japanese 伊邪那岐 (Izanagi), 'He-Who-Invites,' creator kami
  • Consort Izanami, 'She-Who-Invites'
  • Children Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, Susanoo, and countless other kami

05

He-Who-Invites

Creator of the Islands, Father of the Gods

Izanagi is the male half of the primordial creator pair. With his sister-wife Izanami, he stirred the primordial ocean with the spear Ame-no-Nuboko and raised the islands of Japan. His later journey to the underworld and ritual purification gave birth to the most powerful kami of the Japanese pantheon, including Amaterasu herself.

Creator of Japan

With Izanami, he stirred the ocean and created the archipelago.

Spear of Heaven

Ame-no-Nuboko, the jeweled spear with which he stirred the brine.

Father of the Sun

His purification produced Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo.

Visitor to Yomi

He descended to the underworld to retrieve his dead wife Izanami.

Sacred Symbols

Ame-no-Nuboko The jeweled spear used to stir the primordial ocean and create land
Yomotsu-hirasaka The slope that separates the land of the living from Yomi, sealed by Izanagi
Misogi purification The river ritual that cleansed him after Yomi and produced the great kami
Eight-forked road The crossroads in Yomi where Izanami sent demons to pursue him
06

Mythology

Stories of Izanagi

Izanagi's mythology is the creation narrative of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: the making of the world, the founding of death, and the birth of the gods who rule heaven and earth.

Kojiki / Nihon Shoki

The Creation of the Islands

Standing on the Floating Bridge of Heaven, Izanagi and Izanami lowered the jeweled spear into the formless ocean. When they lifted it, brine dripped from the tip and congealed into the island of Onogoroshima. They descended to the island, circled the pillar of heaven, and began to create the Japanese archipelago.

Kojiki

The Birth and Death of Izanami

As Izanami gave birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi, she was burned so badly that she died. Izanagi pursued her to Yomi, the underworld, and asked her to return. She had already eaten the food of Yomi and could not leave, but she asked him to wait. When he broke his promise and lit a comb to see her decaying body, she sent the eight thunder demons and the hag of Yomi after him. He escaped and sealed the entrance with a great boulder.

Kojiki

The Purification of Izanagi

After escaping Yomi, Izanagi performed purification (misogi) in a river. As he washed, new kami were born from his discarded garments and from his body: Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose. These three became the rulers of heaven, night, and sea.

Extended Lore

Izanagi is the one who could not bring back the dead. His grief produced the separation of life and death, and his purification produced the gods. His story suggests that creation and loss are inseparable: to make the world, something must be left behind.
