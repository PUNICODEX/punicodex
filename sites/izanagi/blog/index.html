Overview

Izanagi (izanagi) — ​Creator God, Shintō — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "He who invites".

Izanagi is the creator god who, with Izanami, gives birth to the Japanese islands and the great ​kami.

PuniCodex restores the name as Izanagi. The plain ASCII form izanagi is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive ​visual form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is treated as a plain form. The Unicode form ​remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 伊邪那岐. Etymologically it means "He who ‌invites".

Izanagi's name means "he who invites," from the verb izanau (to invite, to welcome). Together with his spouse Izanami he stands the kami of creation in Japanese myth. His journey to ​the land of the dead (Yomi) to retrieve Izanami is one of the foundational narratives of Shinto cosmology.

The ASCII form izanagi survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese ​characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Izanagi gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name ‌in romanized Japanese. The name is a simpler restoration because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

‌The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 伊邪那岐 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written ​top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Izanagi (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //i.za.na.ɡi//.

The characters 伊邪那岐 are ateji for the divine name. ‌The final graph 岐 is read gi here. The full name is Izanagi-no-Mikoto, with the honorific suffix.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /i.za.na.ɡi/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For ​the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'IZANAGI' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Izanagi is a simpler restoration because ​the ASCII form izanagi already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short syllable ​receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as separate morae. The name Izanagi therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English speaker stresses ​one syllable heavily. The Hepburn romanization used here records the modern standard pronunciation; it does not preserve the historical pitch accent of the word, which differs across dialects. When reading the name ​aloud, keep the vowels pure and avoid English diphthongs: the restoration Izanagi is the conventional scholarly citation form, not a phonetic transcription with added diacritics. Mythology

Commanded by the primordial kami to consolidate the drifting land, Izanagi and Izanami stood on the Floating Bridge of Heaven ​and stirred the sea with a spear. The drops that fell from the spear formed Onogoro Island, where they descended and married. Izanami died giving birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi. Izanagi pursued ‌her to Yomi but broke the taboo of looking at her decomposing body; enraged, she sent demons after him. He escaped and sealed the entrance with a boulder, then purified himself in ​a river, begetting Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanō from his nose.

The narrative of Izanagi participates in the larger mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon ‌Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles were compiled in the eighth century under ​imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of izanagi illustrates how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from ‌heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred history.

The Spear in the Brine (The Stirring)

When heaven and earth had parted but the land below floated like oil ​and drifted like a jellyfish, the heavenly kami commanded Izanagi and Izanami to make it solid. Standing upon the Floating Bridge of Heaven, they lowered the jeweled spear Ame-no-Nuboko and stirred; when they ‌drew it up, brine dripped from the tip and piled up, congealing into the island of Onogoroshima (Kojiki 1). Descending, they built a heavenly pillar and a hall eight fathoms long and prepared ​to beget the land. The image is the Japanese creation at its most elegant: the cosmos is not spoken or fought into being but stirred — order precipitating out of the world-ocean ​like salt from brine. Everything that follows, from the imperial line to the boundary of death, crystallizes from that single drip.

The Ceremony Spoken Ajar (The Wrong Word)

To seal their union, Izanagi ‌and Izanami were to circle the heavenly pillar, he from the left and she from the right, meeting on the far side. But Izanami spoke first in greeting — a breach of ritual ‌order — and though they coupled, their first child was Hiruko, the leech-child, boneless and adrift, whom they set loose in a reed boat (Kojiki 1). Consulting the heavenly kami, they learned the ‌error: the man's voice must come first. They redid the circuit in the correct order, and Izanami bore the eight great islands of Japan. The myth encodes a whole ritual theory — ​words spoken out of sequence pollute outcomes — and plants cosmic law inside the smallest unit of ceremony: who speaks first at the pillar. No other creation story makes grammar itself a generative ​force.

The Pursuit Beyond the Slope (Yomi)

Giving birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi, Izanami was burned so badly that she died. Grieving, Izanagi followed her to Yomi and found her at the ‌castle-hall, where she warned him not to look until she had consulted the gods of that land. He waited — but broke his promise, lit a tooth of his comb, and saw ​his beloved rotting, maggot-crowned, with eight thunder-kami raging on her body. Horror-struck, he fled; the hags of Yomi and the thunders pursued him to the slope Yomotsu-hirasaka, where he hurled peaches at the ​pursuers and finally rolled a great boulder into the pass, crying: henceforth let no living cross to the dead (Kojiki 1). Behind the stone, Izanami pronounced the curse of daily death. The myth ‌does not flinch: grief violates the boundary it cannot abolish, and from that violation the world gains both its funerals and its peaches.

  • Misogi purification — The river ritual that cleansed Yomi's pollution and generated the three sovereign kami; the ancestor of ​every Shinto ablution

  • Peaches — The fruits he hurled at Yomi's pursuing hags, from which peaches ‌took their power against evil in Japanese folk ritual

Archaeology & Evidence

The Izanagi Shrine on Awaji Island and the Taga Taisha in Shiga are the principal sites associated with Izanagi. Archaeological ​and architectural studies of these shrines illuminate the regional development of creation myths and the cult of the founding kami.

Realm & Domain

Izanagi is the creator of the Japanese islands and the ​progenitor of the major kami. His domain spans birth, death, purification, and cosmic order. After his return from Yomi he performed the first great purification (misogi) at Awaji, giving birth to Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, ​and Susanō from his washed body.

The domain of Izanagi is understood within the broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, ​buddhas, and local spirits. Shrines, temples, and household rites all invoke the figure in ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Izanagi therefore names not only a mythic ‌person but a living field of ritual activity: pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. This combinatory or "syncretic" context is essential for interpreting the ‌domains listed above.

Across Cultures

Izanagi and Izanami were identified in medieval shinbutsu-shūgō with various Buddhist dyads, and Izanagi was sometimes equated with the Buddha Vairocana or with the gongen of esoteric Buddhism. ​The Taga Taisha and other shrines preserve traditions that link the creative couple to local founding myths.

Cultural Legacy

Izanagi is one of the most important figures in Shinto cosmogony. His purification at ​Awaji is the mythic origin of the misogi and harai rituals still performed at Shinto shrines. The Kojiki account of his descent to Yomi has influenced Japanese literature, art, and conceptions of death ‌and pollution down to the present.
