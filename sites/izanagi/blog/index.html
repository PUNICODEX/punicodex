Why Izanagi belongs in your address bar

Every address bar is a choice. When you type Izanagi, you are not typing a novelty; you are restoring a name that the early DNS, built for English typewriters, could not carry. The plain ASCII form izanagi is a leftover of that constraint, not the name itself. This post is the long version of the restoration: where the name comes from, how the Japanese characters tradition wrote it, how it is pronounced, what the myths and the material record preserve, and why its Unicode form now lives as a working domain. The claim throughout is simple — the original spelling is not decoration. It is the name.

Overview

Izanagi (izanagi) — Creator God, Shintō — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "He who invites".

Izanagi is the creator god who, with Izanami, gives birth to the Japanese islands and the great kami.

PuniCodex restores the name as Izanagi. The plain ASCII form izanagi is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 伊邪那岐. Etymologically it means "He who invites".

Izanagi's name means "he who invites," from the verb izanau (to invite, to welcome). Together with his spouse Izanami he stands the kami of creation in Japanese myth. His journey to the land of the dead (Yomi) to retrieve Izanami is one of the foundational narratives of Shinto cosmology.

The ASCII form izanagi survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Izanagi gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is Tier 2 because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 伊邪那岐 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Izanagi (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //i.za.na.ɡi//.

The characters 伊邪那岐 are ateji for the divine name. The final graph 岐 is read gi here. The full name is Izanagi-no-Mikoto, with the honorific suffix.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /i.za.na.ɡi/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'IZANAGI' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Izanagi is Tier 2 because the ASCII form izanagi already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Mythology

Commanded by the primordial kami to consolidate the drifting land, Izanagi and Izanami stood on the Floating Bridge of Heaven and stirred the sea with a spear. The drops that fell from the spear formed Onogoro Island, where they descended and married. Izanami died giving birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi. Izanagi pursued her to Yomi but broke the taboo of looking at her decomposing body; enraged, she sent demons after him. He escaped and sealed the entrance with a boulder, then purified himself in a river, begetting Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanō from his nose.

The Creation of the Islands (Kojiki / Nihon Shoki)

Standing on the Floating Bridge of Heaven, Izanagi and Izanami lowered the jeweled spear into the formless ocean. When they lifted it, brine dripped from the tip and congealed into the island of Onogoroshima. They descended to the island, circled the pillar of heaven, and began to create the Japanese archipelago.

The Birth and Death of Izanami (Kojiki)

As Izanami gave birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi, she was burned so badly that she died. Izanagi pursued her to Yomi, the underworld, and asked her to return. She had already eaten the food of Yomi and could not leave, but she asked him to wait. When he broke his promise and lit a comb to see her decaying body, she sent the eight thunder demons and the hag of Yomi after him. He escaped and sealed the entrance with a great boulder.

The Purification of Izanagi (Kojiki)

After escaping Yomi, Izanagi performed purification (misogi) in a river. As he washed, new kami were born from his discarded garments and from his body: Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose. These three became the rulers of heaven, night, and sea.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Spear Ame-no-nuboko — the heavenly spear with which he and Izanami stirred the primordial ocean and created Onogoro Island.
  • Purification wand — used in the misogi rite that produced the three great heavenly children.
  • Pillar — the Ame-no-mihashira pillar around which Izanagi and Izanami circled in the first marriage rite.
  • Black robes of Yomi — the pollution of the underworld from which he fled.
  • Yomotsu-hirasaka — The slope that separates the land of the living from Yomi, sealed by Izanagi
  • Misogi purification — The river ritual that cleansed him after Yomi and produced the great kami
  • Eight-forked road — The crossroads in Yomi where Izanami sent demons to pursue him

Archaeology & Evidence

The Izanagi Shrine on Awaji Island and the Taga Taisha in Shiga are the principal sites associated with Izanagi. Archaeological and architectural studies of these shrines illuminate the regional development of creation myths and the cult of the founding kami.

Realm & Domain

Izanagi is the creator of the Japanese islands and the progenitor of the major kami. His domain spans birth, death, purification, and cosmic order. After his return from Yomi he performed the first great purification (misogi) at Awaji, giving birth to Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanō from his washed body.

Across Cultures

Izanagi and Izanami were identified in medieval shinbutsu-shūgō with various Buddhist dyads, and Izanagi was sometimes equated with the Buddha Vairocana or with the gongen of esoteric Buddhism. The Taga Taisha and other shrines preserve traditions that link the creative couple to local founding myths.

Cultural Legacy

Izanagi is one of the most important figures in Shinto cosmogony. His purification at Awaji is the mythic origin of the misogi and harai rituals still performed at Shinto shrines. The Kojiki account of his descent to Yomi has influenced Japanese literature, art, and conceptions of death and pollution down to the present.
