01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Frigg (frigg) ​— Marriage, Motherhood, ‌Queenship · Queen ‌of the Æsir, ​the Knower Who ​Speaks Not — ​is the ​foremost of the ​ásynjur: daughter of ‌Fjörgynn, wife of ​Óðinn, mother of ​Baldr, and the ‌one figure in ​the Norse corpus ‌said to know ​all fate while ‌prophesying none of ​it.[1]

Her dossier ​is short and ​dense. Snorri ​gives her rank ​and household — ‌her hall Fensalir, ​her handmaid Fulla, ‌her messenger Gná ​on the horse ‌Hófvarpnir, her protector-aspect ​Hlín — and ‌the prose fact ​that she knows ‌men's fates though ​she says ​nothing of them.[2] ‌The Poetic Edda ‌gives her the ‌doctrine in verse: ​Freyja's answer to ​Loki in Lokasenna, ‌that Frigg knows ​all fate though ​she does not ‌speak it; and ​the heaviest single ​line of Völuspá, ‌'then Frigg ​wept in Fensalir ‌over Valhöll's woe'.[3] ​Her one great ​act is the ​oath: when Baldr ​dreams of death, ‌she exacts from ‌all creation the ​promise not to ‌harm him, omitting ​only the mistletoe ​as too young ‌to swear ‌— the gap ‌through which the ‌doom enters.[4]

PuniCodex ​restores the name ‌as Frigg — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, because ​the normalized Old ‌Norse form is ​itself plain ASCII: ​no diacritic, no ​distinctive letter, ‌nothing for the ​ASCII alphabet to ​lose. The name ​is therefore Tier ‌2 by the ​project's mechanical rule ‌— a property ​of its orthography, ‌not of the ​goddess's standing. The ‌plain-ASCII domain frigg.com ‌is registered to ​a third ​party outside the ​collection and is ​watched for release; ‌the project claims ‌no domain for ​this temple.[5]

02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ‌Frigg (nominative; genitive ​Friggjar). Its etymology ​is among the ​more secure in the ​pantheon: Proto-Germanic Frijjō, ​'the beloved one', ‌from the verbal ​root frijōn 'to ​love' — the ‌same Indo-European stock ​(priH-) that gives ‌Sanskrit priyā́ 'beloved' ​and, on the ‌Germanic side, English ​friend and free.[1] ​The double-g of ​the Old Norse form ​is the assimilated ​survival of the ‌stem plus the ​old nominative ending: ‌Frijjō-r* contracted into ​the geminate.

The ‌continental record keeps ​the name's family. ‌Paul the Deacon, ​writing the Langobard ‌origin legend in ​the eighth century, calls ​the wife of ‌Godan Frea — ‌the Langobardic form ‌of the same ​name; Old High ​German knows Frija; ‌and Old English ​preserves Frīg, chiefly ​as a fossil ‌in the weekday ​Frīgedæg, Friday, the ​Germanic translation of ‌dies Veneris.[2]

The ASCII ​form is not ‌a compromise here: ​normalized Old Norse ​Frigg is already ​written in plain ​ASCII letters. There ‌is no diacritic, ‌no thorn or ​eth, no vowel-length ‌mark for the ​early domain-name system ​to have stripped. ‌The name is classified ‌Tier 2 by ‌the project's mechanical ‌rule — the ​restoration preserves nothing ‌the ASCII form ​loses — which ​is a statement ​about orthography, never ‌about the goddess.

​The letter-by-letter transformation ​runs:

  • f ​→ F — Same, ‌capitalized
  • r ​→ r — ​Same
  • i ​→ i — ‌Same
  • g ​→ g — ‌Same
  • g ​→ g — ‌Same

The project ​holds no domain ‌for this name: ‌frigg.com is registered ​to a third party ​outside the PuniCodex ​collection and is ​watched for release. ‌The temple lives ‌at its canonical ​lexicon address.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈfriɡː/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Fr- — Voiceless ​labiodental [f] ‌and trilled [r]; ​the cluster ​is firm ‌at the head ​of the ‌word, stressed as ​the single ‌syllable of the ​name
  • ​-i- — ​Short close front ​[i], held ​brief; the vowel ‌of English ​'bit', not a ‌long ī

​- -gg — ‌Long voiced ​velar stop ‌[ɡː], the true ​geminate: the ‌consonant is held ​and released, ​as in modern ‌Icelandic Frigg; ‌the old ‌nominative ending is ​swallowed into ​this double-g

For ‌the modern ​speaker, the closest ​approximation is: ‌'FRIG' with ​the g held ​long — ‌rhymes with 'big', ​the r ‌lightly trilled, the ​double-g pressed ​and released.

​Kindred and historical ​forms of ‌the name:

  • ‌Old Norse ​— Frigg, wife ‌of Óðinn ​and foremost ​of the ásynjur

‌- Proto-Germanic ‌— *Frijjō, 'the ‌beloved one'[2]
‌- Langobardic — ​Frea, wife ‌of Godan ​in Paul the ​Deacon's Historia ​Langobardorum[3]

  • Old ‌English — ​Frīg, fossilized in ​Frīgedæg, Friday

​- Modern Icelandic ‌— Frigg, ​unchanged in ​spelling

Frigg is ​Tier 2 ‌because the Old ​Norse form ‌is already plain ​ASCII: nothing ‌distinctive is ​lost in transit. ‌The queen ‌of the gods ​carries a ​name so plain ​that only ​the capital ‌distinguishes the restored ‌form from ​the input.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved in ‌medieval Icelandic ‌manuscripts in the ​Latin alphabet as ​Frigg — ​Old Norse (North ​Germanic), attested 13th ​c. CE ‌in the Codex ​Regius of the ​Poetic Edda ‌and the Codex ​Regius and Uppsala ‌codices of ​the Prose Edda, ‌recording material centuries ​older.[1] Before ​the Latin bookhand, ​Old Norse names ​were carved ​in the Younger ‌Futhark; no runic ​attestation of this ‌theonym survives, ​so the manuscript ‌form is the ​primary witness.

‌The scholarly rendering ​proceeds step by ‌step:

  • ​The stem is ​Proto-Germanic Frijjō 'the ‌beloved one', ‌from the root ‌frijōn 'to love'; ​the old ​nominative ending assimilated ‌into the final ​geminate, giving ​Old Norse Frigg ‌(genitive Friggjar).[2]
  • ​Old Norse ​orthography writes the ‌name in plain ​Latin letters with ‌no diacritic: ​the double-g is ​ordinary text, not ​a special ​character.
  • In ‌the Younger Futhark ‌the name ​would be carved ‌ᚠᚱᛁᚴ (f-r-i-k, the ​k-rune writing ​the geminate gg) ‌— a normalized ‌reconstruction, since ‌no carved attestation ‌survives.
  • The ​Unicode restoration ‌Frigg is therefore ​identical to the ​ASCII input ​apart from the ‌capital; nothing was ​lost in transit, ​which is ​why the name ‌sits in Tier ​2.[3]

Because ​the name is ​pure ASCII, its ‌domain was ​registered decades ago ‌in the ordinary ​way: frigg.com ‌is held by ​a third party ‌outside the ‌PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for ​release — ​no form of ​the name is ‌currently owned, ‌and this temple ​claims none.

  2. Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, s.v. Frigg; Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Frigg.
  3. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. Frigg.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Frigg is ​catalogued under ‌Marriage, Motherhood, ‌Queenship — ​three provinces the ​sources assign ​her without ​ornament: she ​is Óðinn's wife ‌and equal ​in counsel, ​Baldr's mother ‌and the engineer ​of his ‌protection, and ​the pantheon's ‌silent keeper of ​fate.[1]

​Marriage

Gylfaginning ​numbers her ​first among the ​ásynjur as ‌Óðinn's wife, ​and the ‌Grímnismál prologue shows ​the marriage ‌as a ​working partnership ‌of wagers and ​stratagems — ‌the one ​myth where ​she outmaneuvers the ‌Allfather at ‌his own ‌game of ​disguises.[2]

Motherhood

​Her motherhood ‌defines the ​pantheon's central ​tragedy: the oath ‌of all ​things is ​a mother's ‌answer to a ​prophecy, and ‌Völuspá's single ​line — ​Frigg weeping in ​Fensalir — ​is the ‌tradition's image ‌of grief foreseen ​and powerless.[3]

‌### Foresight

​Lokasenna and ​Gylfaginning agree on ‌the fact: ‌she knows ‌all fate ‌and prophesies nothing. ​In a ‌mythology where ​knowledge is ​Óðinn's obsession, bought ​with an ‌eye and ​a hanging, ​Frigg possesses it ​without price ‌— and ​her power ​is exactly her ​silence.[1][4]

‌Queenship

Her ​household is ‌a court in ​miniature: Fulla ‌the confidante ​with the ‌golden snood, Gná ‌the messenger ​riding Hófvarpnir ​through air ​and sea, Hlín ​set to ‌guard those ‌Frigg wills ​to save.[2]

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Frigg's ​attributes are ‌domestic and ‌funerary rather ​than martial ​— the ​corpus fixes ​her by ​her hall, ‌her women, ​and the ​one unsworn ‌plant:[1]

  • ​Fensalir — ‌'The fen-halls', ​her dwelling ‌and the ​Edda's only ​named home ​for her; ​Völuspá makes ​it the ‌address of ​her weeping

‌- The ​unsworn mistletoe ‌— The ​one shoot ‌too young ​for ‌the oath: ​the symbol ​of the ‌gap in ‌all thoroughness, ‌slender and ​fatal[2]

  • ​Fulla's casket ‌— Fulla ​carries Frigg's ​eski, minds ‌her footwear, ​and shares ​her secret ‌counsels: the ​kept knowledge ‌of the ​queen made ​portable
  • ​Gná and ​Hófvarpnir — ‌Her messenger ‌and the ​'hoof-thrower' horse ‌that crosses ​air and ​sea: the ‌queen's word ‌carried between ‌worlds, ‌a female ​mirror of ‌Sleipnir's errands

​- The ​linen robe ​from Hel ‌— Nanna's ​gift, sent ​back with ​Hermóðr: a ‌wife's token ​to the ​mother-in-law, carried ​out of ‌the land ​of the ‌dead[2]

  • ​Frigga's distaff ‌— Orion's ​belt in ‌Scandinavian folk ‌astronomy, noted ​by Grimm: ​the later ​ages giving ​the queen ‌the spindle ‌the Eddas ​forgot[3]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Frigg's mythology is the ​mythology of the one who knew ‌first: the oath she almost completed, the ‌grief she almost prevented, the silence ​she keeps about everything else.

​The Oath of All Things (Gylfaginning 49)

​When Baldr dreams baleful dreams for ​his life, Frigg fights the future with ​an oath, exacted from all things ‌— fire and water, iron and every ​metal, stones, earth, trees, sicknesses, beasts, ​birds, poison, serpents — that they ‌will not harm her son. Only the ​mistletoe, growing west of Valhöll, seemed ‌too young to demand the oath of. ​The completeness of the list is ‌the point: Snorri enumerates creation itself, and ​the catastrophe enters through the one ​thing a mother's thoroughness could not ​imagine mattering.[1]

The Tears in Fensalir ​(Gylfaginning 49 · Völuspá 33)

Loki, ​in a woman's shape, comes to Fensalir ‌and draws the single omission out ​of Frigg herself. When blind Höðr's guided ‌throw falls and Baldr lies dead, ​it is Frigg who finds the first ‌voice in the silent hall and ​asks who among the Æsir will ‌ride to Hel and offer ransom — ​the grief-struck mother still doing strategy. ‌Völuspá gives her the briefest and heaviest ​line in the poem: 'Then Frigg ​wept in Fensalir over Valhöll's woe.' And ‌when Hermóðr rides back from the ‌dead, he carries a gift addressed ‌to her: from Nanna, her son's wife ​who died of grief on the ​pyre, a linen robe — one bereaved ‌woman remembering another, out of Hel ​itself.[1][2]

She Knows All Fate (Lokasenna ​26–30 · Gylfaginning 20)

In the ‌flyting, Loki turns his slander on ​the queen — his taunts are the ​poem's business, not the tradition's verdict ‌— and Freyja answers with the line ​that defines her: 'Frigg knows, I ‌think, all fate, though she does not ​speak it.' Snorri repeats the same ​fact as plain prose: she knows ​the fates of men, though she prophesies ​nothing.[3][4]

The Foster-Mother's Wager (Grímnismál, ‌prose prologue)

Óðinn and Frigg foster two ‌shipwrecked princes, Agnarr and Geirröðr; Óðinn ​backs Agnarr, Frigg backs Geirröðr, and Geirröðr ‌takes the throne. When Óðinn boasts ​of his foster-son's hospitality, Frigg calls ​it false, and the wager is laid. ‌She sends Fulla to warn the ‌king that a dangerous wizard is coming, ‌easily known because no dog will ‌leap at him; Óðinn arrives, is seized, ​and hangs tortured between two fires ‌for eight nights — outmaneuvered by ​his own wife, until the whole poem ​of Grimnir's sayings pours out of ​his captivity. It is the one myth ‌where the queen moves the plot ​against the Allfather, and she wins the ​move.[5]

Frea and the Naming ​of the Longbeards (Paul the Deacon 1.8)

‌The goddess's clearest continental appearance is ​Latin and eighth-century. Frea, Godan's wife ​— the Langobardic form of Frigg — ​counsels the outnumbered Winnili: at sunrise ‌the women are to let down their ​hair around their faces like beards ‌and stand with their husbands. At dawn ​she turns Godan's bed toward the ‌east and wakes him; he sees ​the bearded women and asks, 'Who are ‌these longbeards?' Frea answers: you have ‌given them a name — now give ​them the victory. Godan grants both, ​and the Winnili become the Langobardi. The ​tale is an origin-legend, but it ​proves the migration-age mainland knew a ‌goddess Frea at Godan's side, counsellor and ‌kingmaker, centuries before the Icelandic Eddas ​wrote her as Frigg.[6]

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The Roman ​interpretatio fixed ‌Frigg's identity for ‌posterity through ​the calendar: when ​the Germanic ​peoples translated the ​seven-day week, ​dies Veneris — ‌Venus's day ​— became Frigg's ​day, Old ‌English Frīgedæg, Old ​Norse frjádagr, ‌whence Friday. The ​equation is ‌Venus-as-consort: the wife ​of the ​chief god takes ​the day ​of the goddess ​of love.[1] ‌Greek comparison ​runs to Hera, ‌the queen ​and goddess of ‌marriage, but ​the structural fit ‌is imperfect ​— Frigg keeps ‌a seeress's ​knowledge Hera never ​claims.

The ‌deepest syncretic debate ‌is internal. ‌Since Jacob Grimm, ​scholars have ​asked whether Frigg ‌and Freyja ​were originally one ​goddess: both ‌wear falcon-cloaks, both ​are sought ​by giants, both ‌weep for ​absent mates, and ‌their husbands' ​names (Óðinn, Óðr) ​look like ​doubles. The names ​themselves, however, ‌are different words ‌— Frijjō ​'beloved' against frawjǭ ‌'lady' — ​and the handbooks ​(Simek, Orchard, ‌Lindow) print the ‌question open ‌rather than settled.[2]

‌Her firm ​continental witnesses are ‌two. Paul ​the Deacon's Frea ​stands at ​Godan's side in ‌the Langobard ​origin legend, ​counselling the stratagem ​of the ‌beards.[3] Saxo Grammaticus, ​euhemerizing in ​the Gesta Danorum, ​gives a ‌Queen Frigga who ​strips gold ‌from Óðinn's statue ​to gild ‌herself and disgraces ​the marriage ‌— a scandal-story ‌that belongs ​to Saxo's literary ​workshop, instructive ​about thirteenth-century Danish ​readings of ‌the goddess but ‌not about ​her cult.[4]

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Frigg's ​most durable ‌legacy is ‌the fifth ​weekday: Friday ​carries her ​name in ​every Germanic ​language, the ‌Germanic answer ​to the ​day of ‌Venus — ​a ‌goddess fossilized ​in the ‌calendar of ​half the ​modern world.[1]

​The Victorian ​revival made ​her the ‌weeping mother ​of the ‌North: Matthew ​Arnold's ‌'Balder Dead' ​(1855) gives ‌her long ​grieving speeches ‌the Eddas ​only imply.[2] ​The same ‌century's folklore ‌collectors recorded ‌'Frigga's distaff' ​(Swedish Friggerock) ​as the ‌folk ​name of ​Orion's belt, ‌and from ​that spindle ​the romantic ‌image of ​the cloud-weaving ‌queen descends ​— an ​image the ​Eddas themselves ​never ‌draw, and ‌one this ​temple presents ‌as the ​later, lovely ​accretion it ‌is.[3] Wagner ‌recast her ‌as Fricka ‌in the ​Ring, the ‌severe guardian ​of ​marriage and ​the operatic ‌portrait most ​moderns know ​first.

Modern ​Heathenry honors ‌her as ​goddess of ​hearth, household, ​and the ‌keeping of ​frith ‌— the ​peace-bond of ‌the hall ​— and ‌modern fantasy ‌keeps her ​at Óðinn's ​side. Her ​name never ​needed restoring: ‌it never ‌left the ​week.

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Frigg ​is an ‌essentially textual ‌goddess: no ​runestone invokes ​her, no ​votive deposit ​names her, ​and the ‌Scandinavian place-name ​evidence sometimes ​claimed ‌for her ​is contested ‌by the ​place-name scholars.[1] ‌Her material ​footprint is ​calendrical and ​linguistic — ​the weekday ​itself. The ‌substitution ​of Frigg ‌for Venus ​in the ‌Germanic translation ​of the ‌seven-day week ​(Old English ‌Frīgedæg, Old ​Norse frjádagr) ​is the ‌fossil ‌of the ‌interpretatio romana, ​preserved in ​every modern ‌Germanic language.[2]

​The narrative ​witnesses are ‌the Codex ​Regius of ​the Poetic ‌Edda ​and the ‌Codex Regius ​and Uppsala ​manuscripts of ​Snorri's Edda, ​thirteenth- and ‌fourteenth-century Icelandic ‌codices recording ​far older ‌material. Her ​continental ​attestation, Paul ‌the Deacon's ‌Historia Langobardorum, ‌descends in ‌a rich ​manuscript tradition ‌from the ​late eighth-century ​original.[3] Scandinavian ​folk astronomy, ‌recorded ​by nineteenth-century ​collectors and ​noted by ‌Grimm, kept ​the name ​Friggerock — ​'Frigga's distaff' ‌— for ​the three ‌stars of ​Orion's ‌belt: the ​nearest thing ‌to a ‌living material ​tradition attached ​to her, ​and the ​honest root ‌of the ‌later cloud-weaver ​image.[4]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Poetic Edda ‌gives Frigg ‌three registers: ​doctrine, grief, ​and guile. ​The doctrine ​is Lokasenna's: ​when Loki's ‌flyting reaches ​the ​queen, Freyja ‌answers for ​her with ‌the corpus's ​defining sentence ‌— 'Frigg ​knows, I ​think, all ​fate, though ​she ​does not ‌speak it.'[1] ​The grief ‌is Völuspá's, ​twice: 'then ‌Frigg wept ​in Fensalir ‌over Valhöll's ​woe' ‌at Baldr's ​fall, and ​at Ragnarök ‌the seeress ‌foretells 'the ‌second grief ​of Frigg', ​when Óðinn ‌goes against ​the ​wolf — ‌the poem ​counting her ​bereavements as ‌the world's ​losses.[2] The ‌guile is ​the Grímnismál ​prologue, the ​one ​prose frame ‌in the ‌Codex Regius ​where she ‌moves the ​plot: fostering ​Geirröðr, winning ‌the wager, ‌and sending ‌Fulla ‌to spring ​the trap ‌that hangs ​her own ​husband between ​the fires ‌until his ​wisdom pours ​out.[3]

​What the ‌Edda never ​gives her ​is the ​romantic loom. ‌The cloud-weaving ​queen of ‌the Victorian ​handbooks is ‌built ​on a ‌folk star-name ‌— Frigga's ​distaff, Orion's ​belt — ​and not ​on any ‌verse the ‌medieval poems ​know.[4]

Sources

  1. Lokasenna 26–30 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Völuspá 33 and 53 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Grímnismál, prose prologue (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  4. Grimm, Jacob, Teutonic Mythology, trans. Stallybrass (1882–88), on Frigga's distaff.

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Prose Edda ‌is Frigg's ‌systematic witness. ​Gylfaginning ​20 states ​her doctrine ​as plain ​fact ‌— she ​knows the ​fates ‌of men, ​though she ‌prophesies nothing ​— ‌and Gylfaginning ​35 builds ​her court: ​Fensalir ​her hall, ​Fulla the ‌golden-banded handmaid ​who ‌carries her ​casket and ‌shares her ​counsels, ‌Gná the ​messenger riding ‌Hófvarpnir through ​air ​and sea, ‌Hlín set ‌to ‌guard those ​Frigg wills ​to save.[1]

‌Gylfaginning ​49 makes ​her the ‌engineer of ​the ​myth's central ‌protection and ​the first ‌voice ​of its ​grief: she ​exacts the ​oath ‌from all ‌things, omits ​the mistletoe ‌as ​too young, ​unwittingly betrays ‌the ‌omission to ‌the disguised ‌Loki, and ​after ‌the fall ​asks who ​will ride ​to ‌Hel with ​ransom — ​receiving, by ​Hermóðr's ‌return, Nanna's ​linen robe ​from the ​dead.[2] ‌Skáldskaparmál's kennings ​keep her ‌in the ​poet's ‌stock as ​'wife of ‌Óðinn, ‌mother of ​Baldr', her ​identity carried ​by ​the two ‌relationships the ‌tragedy binds ​together.[3]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 20 and 35, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Skáldskaparmál (kennings for Frigg), trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no ​invocation of ​the ​goddess ​Frigg. No ​runestone ‌asks ​her favour, ​no ‌amulet ​bears her ‌name, ​and ‌no inscription ​yet ​found ​links a ​dedicant ​to ‌her cult ​— ‌a ​silence she ‌shares ​with ‌most of ​the ‌ásynjur, ​whose presence ​in ‌the ‌record is ‌literary ​rather ​than epigraphic.[1]

‌What ​the ​runes do ‌fix ​is ​the name's ‌shape. ​In the ‌Younger ​Futhark ​the name ​would ​have ‌been carved ‌ᚠᚱᛁᚴ ​— ‌f-r-i-k, the ​single ​k-rune ‌writing the ‌geminate ‌gg ‌into which ​the ‌old ​nominative ending ​was ​assimilated. ‌No Viking-Age ​carving ​of ​the theonym ‌is ​known; ​the rendering ​is ‌a ​normalized reconstruction, ‌and ​the ‌primary witness ​remains ‌the ‌manuscript spelling ​of ​the ​Eddas — ​which ‌is ‌itself plain ​ASCII.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Frigg; Simek, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, s.v. Frigg.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, Frigg's ‌medieval afterlife ‌runs through ​Latin historiography ​rather than ​Icelandic saga. ​The weightiest ​witness is ‌Paul the ​Deacon's Historia ​Langobardorum (late ‌8th c.), ​which makes ‌Frea the ​strategist of ‌the Langobard ​origin: the ​counsel of ​the beards, ​the turning ​of Godan's ‌bed toward ​the sunrise, ‌the pun ​that names ‌a people ​and wins ‌its battle. ​Behind Paul lies ‌the seventh-century ​Origo Gentis ​Langobardorum, which ‌carries the ‌same tale ‌in shorter ​form — ​proof that ‌the goddess ​was mainland ​property long ‌before the ​Icelandic codices.[1]

​Saxo Grammaticus, ‌euhemerizing the ​gods as ‌ancient Danish ​royalty, gives ​Book 1 ​of the ​Gesta Danorum ‌a Queen ‌Frigga who ​strips the ‌gold from ​Óðinn's statue ​and disgraces ‌the marriage ‌— a ‌scandal-narrative in the ‌classical manner, ​valuable as ‌evidence of ​how a ​thirteenth-century Christian ​literatus read ‌the queen, ​and not ​as myth.[2] ​The fornaldar- ‌and riddarasögur ​know her ​chiefly as ​an oath-name ‌and a ​poetic memory; ‌her living ​saga is ‌the weekday ​— Frīgedæg, ‌frjádagr, Friday ‌— which ​has carried ​her name ​without interruption ​from the ‌migration age ‌to the ​present calendar.[3]

Sources

  1. Paul the Deacon, Historia Langobardorum 1.8, trans. Foulke (1907); Origo Gentis Langobardorum, ed. and trans. in Foulke's appendix.
  2. Saxo Grammaticus, Gesta Danorum, Book 1, trans. Peter Fisher, ed. Hilda Ellis Davidson (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1979).
  3. Grimm, Jacob, Teutonic Mythology, trans. Stallybrass (1882–88), on the day-name.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Frigg ​is the ‌patron ‌of the ​knowledge that ​cannot ​be used. ​She ​knows all ‌fate and ​speaks ​none of ‌it ​— not ‌from coldness, ​but ‌because the ​Edda ​understands what ​foresight costs: ​she ​sees the ‌mistletoe ​coming and ‌still ​must live ‌the days ​before ‌it falls.[1]

​Her ‌one permitted ​weapon is ​thoroughness, ‌and she ‌wields ‌it to ​the limit ​of ‌the world ​— ​fire, water, ‌iron, stone, ​sickness, ​serpent, all ‌sworn ​— and ‌the ​future enters ​through the ​single ​twig too ‌young ‌to swear.[2] ​What the ‌tradition ​leaves her ​is ‌not failure ‌but a ‌harder ‌thing: the ​discipline ‌of the ​one who ​knows. ​Every keeper ‌of ​unwelcome certainty ​— ​the parent ‌who sees, ​the ​friend who ​cannot ‌say, the ​expert whose ‌warning ​would only ‌spread ​the panic ‌— works ‌in ​Fensalir, keeping ​counsel ​with a ​knowledge that ‌has ‌nowhere to ​go.

Sources

  1. Lokasenna 29, trans. Larrington (2014); Gylfaginning 20, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 49, trans. Faulkes (1987).
