Overview

Pazuzu (pazuzu) — Wind Demon, Protection · Unknown — belongs to the Mesopotamian tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Wind Demon, Protection". The name means "Unknown".

Pazuzu is the best-known demon of ancient Mesopotamia: the king of the lilû wind-demons, bringer of pestilential winds from the west and southwest, and—paradoxically—the apotropaic power invoked to keep worse demons away from house, mother, and child. In Assyrian and Babylonian thought he is not merely evil; he is useful evil, a terrifying force turned against other terrifying forces.

The Original Script

The name is preserved in Cuneiform as 𒀭𒉺𒍪𒍪 — Sumero-Akkadian cuneiform, attested Neo-Assyrian / Neo-Babylonian, c. 900–550 BCE, in Mesopotamia. The script is written left-to-right / top-to-bottom.

The scholarly transliteration is ᵈpa-zu-zu (Akkadian scholarly), giving the normalized reading /paˈzuːzu/.

The rendering proceeds step by step:

  • The standard spelling on Pazuzu heads and statues is ᵈpa-zu-zu, from Incantation A: anāku ᵈpa-zu-zu mār Ḫanbi, 'I am Pazuzu, son of Ḫanbi'
  • A variant pa-su-su is also attested; the romanization 'pà.zu.zu' in some literature uses other signs and is not followed here

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /paˈzuːzu/ — Akkadian Reconstruction.

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Pa- — Voiceless bilabial stop [p] followed by open vowel [a]; the first syllable is short and percussive.
  • -zū- — Voiced alveolar fricative [z] plus long close back rounded vowel [uː]; the macron marks a reconstructed length in the Akkadian name.
  • -zu — Final voiced alveolar fricative [z] and close back rounded vowel [u]; the name ends with the same syllable it began, giving it a drum-like ring.

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'pah-ZOO-zoo' — stress the long middle syllable and let the final syllable fall like a dry gust.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Akkadian — 𒀭𒉺𒍣𒍣 (dPazuzu) — the wind-demon's name written with the divine determinative dingir
  • Assyrian amulet formula — 'I am Pazuzu, son of Ḫanbi, king of the evil wind-demons' — standard self-presentation on protective plaques

Pazuzu is Tier 2 because the Unicode restoration preserves only the plain ASCII letters of the Akkadian name; there is no distinctive diacritic or non-ASCII letter that the ASCII form loses. The macron in /paˈzuːzu/ is a scholarly reconstruction of vowel length, not a feature of the written Unicode restoration.

Mythology

Pazuzu has no long narrative epic; his 'mythology' is the archive of incantations, amulets, and bronze images that give him a precise job description. He is the demon you invoke when you need a worse demon driven off.

Son of Ḫanbi, King of the Evil Wind-Demons (Incantation)

Assyrian and Babylonian amulets introduce Pazuzu in the first person: 'I am Pazuzu, son of Ḫanbi, king of the evil lilû-demons.' The formula identifies him as the head of a class of wind spirits and gives him a father, Ḫanbi, whose own name marks demonic ancestry. The incantations ask him to rise up and pursue malevolent forces, using his authority over the winds rather than fighting them directly.

The Warden Against Lamashtu (Apotropaic)

Lamashtu was the demoness who attacked pregnant women and newborns. To stop her, families hung Pazuzu amulets and engraved plaques at doorways and bedsides. The logic is sympathetic magic at its most pragmatic: a demon powerful enough to rule the winds is powerful enough to intimidate a child-killer. Images show Pazuzu grasping snakes and scorpions while Lamashtu is bound or sent back to the underworld.

The Bronze Head from Assyria (Iconography)

The most famous Pazuzu image is a bronze head in the Louvre (AO 19931), found in the region of ancient Assyria and dated to the 8th–7th century BCE. It combines a leonine muzzle, bulging eyes, a scaly body, a serpent penis, scorpion tail, and eagle talons. Every feature is chosen for maximum fright-value; the head was meant to be seen, not hidden, because its visibility was the protection.

The Southwest Wind in Mesopotamian Thought (Legacy)

In Mesopotamian meteorology, certain winds were not weather but agents of disease. The west and southwest winds could bring fever, crop-blight, and sand-storms. Pazuzu concentrates that dangerous wind into a single persona: when the air itself seems hostile, it helps to name the hostility. He is, in effect, the personification of an ecological anxiety that is still familiar in hot, dry climates today.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Pazuzu concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Serpents and scorpions — Attributes of the demon and the poisons he commands; on amulets they also bind Lamashtu.
  • Leonine face — The snarling mask of the wind-demon, meant to out-terrorize any spirit that approaches the threshold.
  • Eagle talons — The grasping, predatory power of the storm-wind and its capacity to snatch life away.
  • Right hand raised — The apotropaic gesture of warding, common on Mesopotamian plaques and demon images.

Archaeology & Evidence

Pazuzu's material record consists chiefly of bronze heads, terracotta plaques, and engraved amulets from Assyria, Babylonia, and surrounding regions, dated especially to the 8th–7th centuries BCE. The Louvre bronze head (AO 19931) is the most complete example. Amulets depicting Pazuzu opposite Lamashtu were intended for domestic use, often near women and children. Cuneiform incantation tablets give the verbal counterpart to these images, preserving the formulae in which Pazuzu names himself and accepts his apotropaic commission.

Apotropaic Warden

Pestilential West Wind

The southwest wind that carried fever, drought, and famine; meteorology and demonology were not separate sciences.

Icon of Terror

Leonic face, bulging eyes, serpents, scorpions, and eagle talons — a body designed to be more frightening than the demons it repels.

Across Cultures

Pazuzu sits at the intersection of demonology, medicine, and household religion.

His apotropaic role places him alongside other Mesopotamian protective figures such as the apkallu sages and the lion-demon who guards doorways. Later Near Eastern and Mediterranean traditions continued the idea that a demon's own power could be turned against other demons — a logic visible in Jewish and Christian amulets, Gnostic charitēs, and Arabic jinn lore. The modern horror film use of Pazuzu is a 20th-century reinvention; the ancient evidence is overwhelmingly protective, not purely malevolent.

Within the Mesopotamian tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Adad, Anû, Apsû, Aššur, Dumuzid, and Ēa.

Cultural Legacy

Pazuzu is probably the most visually recognizable Mesopotamian demon in the modern world, but for reasons ancient worshippers would not recognize.

The Louvre bronze head has become an icon of ancient Near Eastern art, reproduced in textbooks, museum merchandise, and popular media. Among Assyriologists, Pazuzu is a case-study in the logic of apotropaic religion: how a culture confronts danger by naming, embodying, and redirecting it. His amulets are early examples of protective technology — a material object designed to keep invisible harm at bay.

A Meditation

Pazuzu teaches that protection and peril can wear the same face. The demon who brings the pestilential wind is the same figure hung over a cradle to guard an infant. This is not contradiction; it is strategy. When the threat is invisible — a fever on the air, a sudden infant death, a miscarriage in the night — the defender must be equally uncanny.

To restore Pazuzu in Unicode is to keep visible the plain, wind-bitten sound of that strategy. There is no glittering diacritic here, no recovered long vowel in the display form. The name is as stark as the desert wind it names. That plainness is itself the point: some powers do not need ornament to be remembered.

A Closer Look at the Marks

A restored name is a small map. In the case of Pazuzu, the map does not lead through diacritics or special letters, because the source tradition wrote the name in Cuneiform rather than in the Latin alphabet. The ASCII form pazuzu and the restored form share the same letters; the restoration is a decision about which conventional spelling should serve as the public reference.

That decision is not cosmetic. The original script attestation carries semantic and phonetic information that no romanization can fully reproduce.
