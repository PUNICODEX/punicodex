Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Pazuzu (pazuzu) ​— Wind ‌Demon, Protection ‌· Unknown ​— belongs ​to the ​Mesopotamian tradition, ​where it ​is catalogued ‌under the ​domain "Wind ​Demon, Protection". ‌The name ​means "Unknown"[1].

‌Pazuzu is ​the best-known ‌demon of ​ancient Mesopotamia: ​the king ​of the ​lilû wind-demons, ​bringer of ‌pestilential winds from ​the west ‌and southwest, ​and—paradoxically—the apotropaic ‌power invoked ​to keep ‌worse demons ​away from ‌house, mother, ​and child. ​In Assyrian ‌and Babylonian ‌thought he ‌is not ​merely evil; ​he is ‌useful evil, ​a terrifying ​force turned ‌against other ​terrifying forces.

​This is ‌a domainless flagship: ​the ASCII ‌pazuzu.com is ​unregistrable, so ​the temple ​presents the ​canonical form ‌without claiming ‌domain ownership. ​The name ‌itself—plain, compact, ​and phonetically ​direct—is the ‌entire restoration.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ‌the name ‌as Pazuzu ​and serves ‌its temple ​at its ​temple. The ​restoration needs ‌no distinctive letters ​or diacritics ​its ASCII ​form would ‌lose, which ​places the ​name in ​Tier 2. ‌The plain ​ASCII form ‌pazuzu survives ​as a ‌modern convenience ​imposed by ‌the early ‌domain-name system; ​the restoration, ​not the ​fallback, is ​the form ‌the project ‌defends as ​philologically complete[3].

Sources

  1. Wiggermann, 'Pazuzu' in Reallexikon der Assyriologie.
  2. Black & Green, Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia.
  3. Louvre AO 19931 — bronze head of Pazuzu.

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name ‌is attested ‌in ​Cuneiform ​as 𒀭𒉺𒍪𒍪. ​Etymologically ​it ​means "Unknown"[1].

‌The ​ASCII ​form ‌pazuzu survives ​only ‌because ​the early ‌domain-name ​system ​could ​not carry ​diacritics; ​it ‌is a ​technological ‌compromise, ​not an ‌ancient ​spelling. ‌The ​Unicode restoration ‌Pazuzu ​recovers ​the full ‌diacritic ‌detail ‌of ​the scholarly ​transliteration ‌directly ​in the ​address ‌bar. ​The restoration ​needs ‌no ​distinctive ‌letters or ​diacritics ​its ​ASCII form ​would ‌lose, ‌which places ​the ‌name ​in ​Tier 2.

‌The ‌letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

‌- ​p ‌→ ​P — ​Same, ​capitalized
‌- a ​→ ​a ​— Same
‌- ​z ​→ ​z — ‌Same
​- ‌u → ​u ‌— ​Same
‌- z ‌→ ​z ​— Same
​- ​u ‌→ u ‌— ​Same

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name ‌is /paˈzuːzu/ — ​Akkadian Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Pa- — Voiceless ​bilabial stop ‌[p] followed ​by open vowel ​[a]; the ‌first syllable ​is short and ‌percussive.
  • ​-zū- — ‌Voiced alveolar fricative ​[z] plus ​long close ​back rounded ​vowel [uː]; the ​macron marks ‌a reconstructed ​length in the ‌Akkadian name.

​- -zu ‌— Final voiced ​alveolar fricative ‌[z] and ​close back rounded ‌vowel [u]; ​the name ​ends with ‌the same syllable ‌it began, ‌giving it ​a drum-like ring.

​For the ‌modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ​is: 'pah-ZOO-zoo' ‌— stress ​the long middle ​syllable and ‌let the ​final syllable ‌fall like a ​dry gust.

​Kindred and ​historical forms of ​the name:

‌- Akkadian ‌— 𒀭𒉺𒍣𒍣 (dPazuzu) ​— the ‌wind-demon's name ​written with the ​divine determinative ‌dingir

  • ‌Assyrian amulet ‌formula — 'I ‌am Pazuzu, ​son of ‌Ḫanbi, king of ​the evil ​wind-demons' — ​standard self-presentation on ‌protective plaques

​Pazuzu is ​Tier 2 because ​the Unicode ‌restoration preserves ​only the ​plain ASCII letters ​of the ‌Akkadian name; ​there is no ‌distinctive diacritic ​or non-ASCII ‌letter that the ​ASCII form ‌loses. The ‌macron in /paˈzuːzu/ ​is a ​scholarly reconstruction ​of vowel ​length, not a ‌feature of ‌the written ​Unicode restoration.

Sources

  1. Wiggermann, 'Pazuzu' in Reallexikon der Assyriologie.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name ‌is ‌preserved ​in ​Cuneiform ​as ​𒀭𒉺𒍪𒍪 ​— ‌Sumero-Akkadian ​cuneiform, ​attested ‌Neo-Assyrian ​/ ‌Neo-Babylonian, ​c. ‌900–550 ​BCE, ​in ​Mesopotamia. ​The ​script ‌is ​written ‌left-to-right ​/ ‌top-to-bottom.[1]

​The ‌scholarly ​transliteration ‌is ​ᵈpa-zu-zu ​(Akkadian ‌scholarly), ‌giving ‌the ​normalized ​reading ‌/paˈzuːzu/.

​The ​rendering ‌proceeds ​step ​by ‌step:

  • ​The ‌standard ​spelling ​on ​Pazuzu ​heads ‌and ‌statues ​is ‌ᵈpa-zu-zu, ​from ​Incantation ‌A: ‌anāku ‌ᵈpa-zu-zu ‌mār ​Ḫanbi, ‌'I ​am ​Pazuzu, ​son ‌of ​Ḫanbi'

​- ​A ‌variant ​pa-su-su ​is ​also ‌attested; ​the ‌romanization ​'pà.zu.zu' ‌in ​some ‌literature ‌uses ​other ​signs ​and ​is ‌not ‌followed ​here

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Pazuzu is ​the best-known ‌demon of ‌ancient Mesopotamia: ​the king ​of the ​lilû wind-demons, ​bringer of ​pestilential winds ‌from the ​west and ​southwest, and—paradoxically—the ‌apotropaic power ​invoked to ‌keep worse ​demons away ‌from house, ​mother, and ​child. In ​Assyrian and ​Babylonian thought ​he is ‌not merely ​evil; he ‌is useful ​evil, a ‌terrifying force ​turned against ‌other terrifying ​forces.

This ‌is a ​domainless flagship: ​the ASCII ‌pazuzu.com is ‌unregistrable, so ‌the temple ​presents the ​canonical form ‌without claiming ​domain ownership. ​The name ‌itself—plain, compact, ​and phonetically ​direct—is the ‌entire restoration.[1]

​Lord of ‌the Lilû

​King of ​the evil ​wind-demons in ​Akkadian and ‌Assyrian incantations; ‌the moving ​air itself ‌made demonic.

​### Apotropaic ​Warden

His ‌image was ‌worn to ‌ward off ‌Lamashtu and ​other child-snatching ‌demons — ​a frightful ​guardian against ​frightful things.

‌### Pestilential ​West Wind

​The southwest ​wind that ‌carried fever, ​drought, and ​famine; meteorology ​and demonology ‌were not ​separate sciences.

‌### Icon ​of Terror

‌Leonic face, ​bulging eyes, ‌serpents, scorpions, ‌and eagle ​talons — ​a body ​designed to ​be more ‌frightening than ‌the demons ​it repels.

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

The ​iconography ‌associated ‌with ​Pazuzu ​concentrates ​in ​a ​small ‌set of ​recurring ​attributes, ‌each ​a ‌compressed ​statement ‌about ​the ​name:[1]

​- ​Serpents ​and ‌scorpions ​— ‌Attributes ​of ‌the demon ​and ‌the ​poisons ‌he ​commands; ​on ‌amulets ‌they ‌also ​bind ​Lamashtu.
‌- ​Leonine ​face ‌— ​The ​snarling ‌mask of ​the ‌wind-demon, ​meant ​to ​out-terrorize ​any ‌spirit ‌that ​approaches ‌the ​threshold.
​- ‌Eagle ‌talons ‌— ‌The ​grasping, ‌predatory power ​of ​the ​storm-wind ‌and ​its ​capacity ​to ‌snatch ​life ​away.
​- ‌Right ​hand ‌raised ​— ‌The ​apotropaic gesture ‌of ‌warding, ​common ​on ​Mesopotamian ​plaques ‌and ‌demon ​images.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Pazuzu has ​no long narrative epic; ‌his 'mythology' is the ‌archive of incantations, amulets, ​and bronze images ​that give him a ​precise job description. He ​is the demon you ​invoke when you need ‌a worse demon ​driven off.[1]

Son ​of Ḫanbi, King of ‌the Evil Wind-Demons (Incantation)

​Assyrian and Babylonian ‌amulets introduce Pazuzu in ​the first person: 'I ‌am Pazuzu, son of ​Ḫanbi, king of the ​evil lilû-demons.' The ​formula identifies him as ​the head of a ​class of wind spirits ‌and gives him ​a father, Ḫanbi, whose ‌own name marks demonic ​ancestry. The incantations ask ‌him to rise ​up and pursue malevolent ‌forces, using his authority ​over the winds rather ‌than fighting them directly.[2]

​### The Warden ​Against Lamashtu (Apotropaic)

Lamashtu ‌was the demoness who ‌attacked pregnant women and ‌newborns. To stop ​her, families hung Pazuzu ​amulets and engraved plaques ‌at doorways and bedsides. ​The logic is sympathetic ​magic at its ‌most pragmatic: a demon ​powerful enough to rule ​the winds is powerful ‌enough to intimidate ​a child-killer. Images show ‌Pazuzu grasping snakes and ​scorpions while Lamashtu is ​bound or sent ​back to the underworld.

​### The Bronze Head ‌from Assyria (Iconography)

The ‌most famous Pazuzu image ​is a bronze ‌head in the Louvre ​(AO 19931), found in ​the region of ancient ‌Assyria and dated ‌to the 8th–7th century ‌BCE. It combines a ‌leonine muzzle, bulging eyes, ​a scaly body, a ‌serpent penis, scorpion ​tail, and eagle talons. ​Every feature is chosen ​for maximum fright-value; the ‌head was meant ​to be seen, not ​hidden, because its visibility ​was the protection.

‌The Southwest Wind ​in Mesopotamian Thought (Legacy)

​In Mesopotamian meteorology, certain ​winds were not weather ‌but agents of disease. ​The west and ‌southwest winds could bring ​fever, crop-blight, and sand-storms. ‌Pazuzu concentrates that dangerous ​wind into a ‌single persona: when the ‌air itself seems hostile, ​it helps to name ​the hostility. He is, ​in effect, the ​personification of an ecological ‌anxiety that is still ‌familiar in hot, dry ​climates today.

Sources

  1. Black & Green, Gods, Demons and Symbols of Ancient Mesopotamia.
  2. Louvre AO 19931 — bronze head of Pazuzu.
08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Pazuzu ​sits ‌at ‌the intersection ​of ​demonology, ​medicine, ​and ​household religion.

‌His ​apotropaic ​role ‌places ​him ‌alongside other ​Mesopotamian ‌protective ​figures ​such ​as the ​apkallu ​sages ‌and ​the ‌lion-demon ​who guards ‌doorways. ​Later ‌Near ​Eastern ‌and Mediterranean ​traditions ​continued ‌the ‌idea ‌that ​a demon's ​own ‌power ​could ​be ‌turned against ​other ​demons ‌— ​a ‌logic ​visible in ​Jewish ​and ​Christian ‌amulets, ‌Gnostic charitēs, ​and ‌Arabic ​jinn ​lore. ‌The ‌modern horror ‌film ‌use ​of ‌Pazuzu ​is a ​20th-century ​reinvention; ‌the ​ancient ​evidence ​is overwhelmingly ‌protective, ​not ​purely ​malevolent.[1]

‌Within the ​Mesopotamian ‌tradition, ​closely ‌related ​names ‌in the ‌corpus ​include ​Adad, ​Anû, ​Apsû, Aššur, ‌Dumuzid, ‌and ​Ēa.

Sources

  1. Wiggermann, 'Pazuzu' in Reallexikon der Assyriologie.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Pazuzu ​is ‌probably ‌the ​most ​visually ​recognizable ​Mesopotamian ​demon ‌​in ​the ‌modern ​world, ‌but ​for ‌reasons ​ancient ​worshippers ​would ​not ​recognize.

‌The ​Louvre ‌bronze ​head ‌​has ‌become ​an ‌icon ​of ​ancient ‌Near ‌Eastern ‌art, ​reproduced ​in ‌textbooks, ​museum ​merchandise, ‌and ​popular ​media. ‌​Among ‌Assyriologists, ​Pazuzu ​is ​a ​case-study ‌in ‌the ​logic ‌of ​apotropaic ​religion: ‌how ‌a ‌culture ‌confronts ​danger ‌​by ​naming, ​embodying, ‌and ​redirecting ​it. ​His ‌amulets ​are ​early ​examples ‌of ​protective ‌technology ​— ‌a ​‌material ‌object ​designed ​to ​keep ​invisible ‌harm ‌at ​bay.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Pazuzu's ​material ‌record ‌​consists ​chiefly ​of ​bronze ​heads, ‌​terracotta ​plaques, ‌and ​engraved ‌amulets ​from ‌​Assyria, ​Babylonia, ​and ​surrounding ​regions, ‌​dated ‌especially ​to ‌the ​8th–7th ‌​centuries ‌BCE. ​The ​Louvre ‌bronze ‌head ‌​(AO ​19931) ‌is ​the ​most ‌​complete ​example. ‌Amulets ​depicting ‌Pazuzu ​​opposite ​Lamashtu ​were ‌intended ‌for ​‌domestic ​use, ​often ‌near ‌women ‌and ‌​children. ‌Cuneiform ​incantation ​tablets ​give ‌​the ​verbal ​counterpart ‌to ​these ​​images, ‌preserving ​the ‌formulae ​in ‌which ​‌Pazuzu ‌names ​himself ​and ​accepts ​‌his ‌apotropaic ​commission.[1]

Cuneiform Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​cuneiform ‌dossier ‌of ​Pazuzu consists ​of ​incantation ​tablets, ​bronze ‌statuettes, ​and ​apotropaic ‌plaques ​from Neo-Assyrian ‌and ​Neo-Babylonian ‌contexts. ​The ​so-called ​Standard ​Pazuzu ​Incantation ‌A introduces ​him ‌in ​the ‌first ​person—anāku ‌dPazuzu ​mār ‌Ḫanbi, ​šar rūḫī ​lemnūti, ‌'I ‌am ‌Pazuzu, ​son ​of ‌Ḫanbi, ​king ​of the ‌evil ​winds'—and ​is ‌preserved ​on ‌multiple ​amulets ​and tablets. ​The ​Utukkū ‌Lemnūtu ‌('Evil ​Demons') ‌series ​classifies ​wind-demons ‌and supplies ‌the ‌demonological ‌taxonomy ​in ‌which ​Pazuzu ​rules ​the ‌lilû. Bronze ​heads ​such ​as ‌Louvre ​AO ​19931 ​give ‌the ​visual counterpart ‌to ​these ‌formulae, ​while ‌Lamashtu ‌plaques ​pair ​his ​image with ​the ‌bound ‌child-snatching ​demoness.

Enūma Eliš

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Pazuzu ​is ‌not ‌a ​named ​actor ​in ​Enūma ​Eliš, ‌but ​the ​poem's wind ‌theology ​frames ‌his ​office. ‌In ​Tablet ​IV ​the ​storm-wind ​(imḫullu) ‌becomes ​Marduk's ‌weapon ​against ‌Tiāmat, ​and ‌Marduk's ​subsequent ‌ordering ​of ​the ‌winds (Tablets ‌IV–V) ‌claims ​cosmic ​control ‌over ​the ​very ‌meteorological ​forces ​Pazuzu ‌personifies. ​For ‌an ​Assyrian ​householder, ​the ​same ‌west ‌wind ​that ‌Marduk ​governs on ​high ‌could ‌still ‌carry ‌fever ​and ‌misfortune ​on ​earth; ​Pazuzu ‌is ​the ​local ​apotropaic ‌answer ​to ​that ​ambiguity. ‌The ​epic ‌therefore ​supplies the ‌cosmic ​warrant ‌for ‌the ​domestic ​terrors ​Pazuzu ​is ‌asked ‌to ​repel.

  3. Wiggermann, F. A. M. 'Pazuzu.' Reallexikon der Assyriologie und Vorderasiatischen Archäologie 10 (2004), 372–381.

Atra-Ḫasīs

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Atra-Ḫasīs ​describes ‌the ‌plague-winds ​and ​droughts ​sent ​by ​Enlil ‌to ​reduce ​human ‌noise, ​a ‌mythic ​economy of ‌disaster ​in ​which ​destructive ​air ​is ‌a ​divine ‌instrument. ​Pazuzu ‌appears ​nowhere ‌in ​the ‌preserved ​tablets, ​yet ‌the ‌text's ‌logic—that ​wind ​and ‌sickness ​travel ​together ‌from ​the ​gods—matches ‌the amulets ​that ‌invoke ​him ​against ​'the ​evil ‌wind.' ‌The ​figure ‌thus ​inhabits ​the ‌same ‌symbolic ‌weather-system ‌as ​the ‌Atra-Ḫasīs ​plagues: ​a ​realm ‌where ​meteorology, ​disease, ​and ‌demonology ​are ​not ​separate disciplines. ‌Reading ​the ‌two ​corpora ‌together ​shows ‌why ‌a ​wind-demon ​could ​be ​simultaneously ‌feared ‌and ​enlisted.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Pazuzu ​teaches that ‌protection ‌and ​peril can ​wear ​the ​same face. ​The ‌demon ​who brings ​the ‌pestilential ​wind is ‌the ​same ‌figure hung ​over ​a cradle ​to ​guard ​an infant. ‌This ​is ‌not contradiction; ​it ‌is ​strategy. When ‌the ​threat ‌is invisible ​— ​a ‌fever on ‌the ‌air, a ​sudden ​infant ‌death, a ​miscarriage ​in ‌the night ​— ​the ‌defender must ​be ‌equally ​uncanny.

To ​restore ​Pazuzu ​in Unicode ‌is ‌to ​keep visible ‌the ​plain, wind-bitten ​sound ‌of ‌that strategy. ‌There ‌is ​no glittering ‌diacritic ​here, ​no recovered ​long ‌vowel ​in the ​display ​form. ‌The name ​is ​as stark ​as ‌the ​desert wind ‌it ​names. ‌That plainness ​is ‌itself ‌the point: ​some ​powers ​do not ​need ‌ornament ‌to be ​remembered.[1]

Sources

  1. Wiggermann, 'Pazuzu' in Reallexikon der Assyriologie.
