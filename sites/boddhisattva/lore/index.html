01

Mythology

Stories of Bodhisattva

The bodhisattva is the Mahāyāna's answer to its own founder's stunning success: if one man woke, the door was never locked. Its mythology runs from the Pāli canon's bodhisatta — Gotama's pre-awakening selves — to cosmic vows that hold the door open for everyone else.

The Word

An Awakening-Being, Not Yet a Buddha

The word is a compound: bodhi, awakening, plus sattva, being — "one whose essence is awakening." In the Pāli canon bodhisatta is a technical term for the Buddha before his awakening: the Nidānakathā and the Mahāpadāna Sutta (DN 14) use it of Gotama's past lives, from the bodhisatta enthroned in Tuṣita down to the last birth at Lumbinī. The Mahāyāna inverted the lens: the bodhisattva is no longer the road one man walked but a vocation open to all — one who deliberately defers final nirvāṇa until the grass of beings is dry, in the Sanskrit formula of the Kāraṇḍavyūha. The compound thus carries an entire schism in six syllables: the earlier tradition's biography against the later tradition's program. An awakening-being is the answer of a religion to its own founder's success: if one man woke, the door was never locked.

The Vow

The Promise That Builds a Bridge

Everything begins with a vow. The Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra — the final book of the Avataṃsaka — sends the pilgrim Sudhana through fifty-three teachers and ends inside Maitreya's tower, where past vows stand visible as architecture, each floor a former life; the praṇidhāna, "may I not sit on awakening's seat until every being is freed," is the bodhisattva's founding act. Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra (8th c.) gives the vow its classic liturgy: "For as long as space endures, and for as long as beings remain, may I too abide, to dispel the misery of the world." The jātakas test the vow against the body itself — Prince Śibi giving his eyes to the hawk (Jātaka 499), Vessantara giving away his wife and children (Jātaka 547). The mythic kernel: a promise made not to a god but to the uncountable future — compassion as a contract written across lifetimes, signed before any power existed that could enforce it.

The Ten Stages

From Joyful to Dharma-Cloud

The Daśabhūmikasūtra maps the bodhisattva career as ten bhūmis, each a world of its own. Pramuditā, Joyful: the first glimpse, irreversible; Vimalā, Stainless, where the ten courses of action are purified; Prabhākarī, Luminous, where the factors of awakening blaze; Arciṣmatī, Blazing; Sudurjayā, Difficult-to-Conquer; Abhimukhī, Facing — the turn toward suchness; Dūraṅgamā, Gone-Afar; Acalā, Immovable, where nothing mundane can shake the vow; Sādhumatī, Good-Intelligence; and Dharmameghā, Dharma-Cloud, where Buddhas of the ten directions pour consecration from their crowns and the bodhisattva can rain the Dharma like a storm. The scheme seeded every later system — the five Maitreya treatises' summaries, the Tibetan graded paths, the Huayan's own rounds. Its meaning: enlightenment is not a moment but a landscape, surveyed in advance so that travelers can know exactly where they stand.

The Exemplars

Avalokiteśvara and the Great Vows

The bodhisattva ideal walks in person. In the Lotus Sūtra (ch. 25) Avalokiteśvara hears the cries of the world and appears in whatever form frees — to the drowning as a boat, to the burning as rain, "with these many bodies wandering in the samsaric world"; Kṣitigarbha takes the opposite route, his vow driving him down — "not until the hells are empty will I become a Buddha" (Kṣitigarbha Sūtra); Mañjuśrī carries wisdom, Samantabhadra practice, Maitreya the future. Tibetan hagiography pushes the idea to its limit: overwhelmed by the sight of unending suffering, Avalokiteśvara shatters into a thousand pieces, and Amitābha reassembles him with eleven heads and a thousand arms — compassion that had to be rebuilt to keep going. The exemplars make the abstract inspectable: a vow you can picture, walking through fire and down into hell on your behalf.

02

Bodhisattva

The Awakening-Being — the Vow That Refuses the Exit

A bodhisattva is a being who has vowed awakening for the sake of all beings — and who, by that vow, defers the exit: not final nirvāṇa while a single being remains unliberated. The Mahāyāna made the word its founding institution: the bridge from one man's awakening to a vocation open to everyone.

The Vow

Praṇidhāna — 'may I not sit on awakening's seat until every being is freed' — the founding act of the bodhisattva career.

The Ten Stages

The Daśabhūmikasūtra's bhūmis — from Joyful to Dharma-Cloud — enlightenment surveyed as a climbable landscape.

The Perfections

The six pāramitās — giving, morality, patience, vigor, meditation, wisdom — the curriculum every vow must pass through.

The Exemplars

Avalokiteśvara hearing the world's cries, Kṣitigarbha vowing the hells be emptied — compassion walking in person.

Sacred Symbols

The lotus Untouched by the mud it grows from — the bodhisattva in the world but not of its stain; also Padmasambhava's birthplace, the word made landscape
The sword of wisdom Mañjuśrī's blade cutting through ignorance — insight wielded not for oneself but to clear the road for others
The water flask and willow Guanyin's healing emblems — the nectar of compassion poured drop by drop onto specific suffering
The moon in water The bodhisattva's presence as reflection — appearing to beings in whatever form frees them, without ever leaving the empty sky
The staff that shakes the six realms Kṣitigarbha's iron khakkhara — its rings wake the dead; his vow keeps him in hell's doorway until it is empty

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

बोधिसत्त्व

The name in its original Buddhist form. Bodhisattva (बोधिसत्त्व) is attested in the source tradition — “'Awakening-being' — one who vows Buddhahood for the sake of all beings”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

boddhisattva

The plain boddhisattva form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Bodhisattva

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Bodhisattva. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Bodhisattva.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Bodhisattva and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Bodhisattva was spoken

/ˈboː.dʱi.sɐt̪.t̪ʋɐ/ Sanskrit Reconstruction
bo- Long o like 'bode' — the awakening (bodhi) opening the word, heavy and stressed.
-dhi- The aspirate dh, a voiced breath — bodhi, 'awakening,' the root of the whole compound.
-sattva Dental sattva, 'being, essence' — with the long ā lost even in the standard spelling: it is bodhi-sattva, an 'awakening-being.'

Modern Approximation

'BOH-dhi-sut-tuh' — stress on BOH; the dh is a voiced breath; the final va is a soft 'wuh.'

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Tier 2: 'Bodhisattva' is ASCII-canonical in spelling. The scholarly restoration adds the knowledge the spelling hides — the long ā of sattva and the compound's meaning: not 'enlightenment-being' in the abstract but a being whose essence is a specific, nameable awakening.

Extended Lore

The bodhisattva is the most daring idea in the history of ethics: a moral agent whose deadline is infinity. Ordinary virtue balances cost and reward; the vow sets the reward aside entirely — awakening withheld, deliberately, until the last stranger is free. Logically it is madness; existentially it is the only response to a world of uncountable suffering that does not end in despair or indifference.
