01

Mythology

Stories of Adámas

Adámas appears in Greek myth not as a character but as a substance that binds, wounds, or pivots the cosmos itself. Its hardness creates the drama: what can hold an immortal must be stronger than the divine. From the sickle that cuts the sky to the spindle that turns the heavens, the unbreakable metal marks the exact points where Greek imagination needed something stronger than god.

Theogony

The Sickle That Wounded the Sky

Gaia, weary of Ouranos pressing down upon her and hiding their children inside her depths, fashioned a plan and set her youngest, Kronos, to ambush his father. She made a great long sickle with jagged teeth — of adamant, ἅρπη ἀδάμαντος (Hesiod, Theogony 161) — and bade Kronos wait in a hidden place. When Ouranos came at night, 'stretched all over Gaia, desiring love,' Kronos reached out with his left hand and swung with his right, severing his father's genitals and hurling them behind him. From the blood that fell on the earth were born the Erinyes, the great Giants, and the Meliai; from the sea-foam where they landed, Aphrodite (Theogony 173–192). Adamant matters here because the myth needs a material harder than the sky: only the unbreakable can break the original order. The word appears at the exact hinge where cosmic succession — and all Greek generation — begins.

Prometheus Bound

Chains That Hold a Titan

Aeschylus opens Prometheus Bound at the edge of the world, where Hephaistos, ordered by Strength and Force, must rivet the rebel Titan to the crags of the Caucasus. His bonds are adamantine — the material whose name means 'unconquerable' — and Hephaistos grieves as he works: his own craft, the fire he gave the world, is being turned to chain the god who gave it to men. The cosmology behind the image is already in Hesiod (Theogony 521–616): Zeus bound Prometheus in indissoluble fetters and set an eagle to tear his liver, because the Titan had tricked him over the sacrifice and stolen the fire. But Aeschylus sharpens the metal's meaning: adamant is not merely strong — it is the substance of the new regime, the hardness of power that will not negotiate, set against a mind that will not yield. The chains hold. The argument, Prometheus insists, will outlast them.

Philosophy

The Spindle of Necessity

In the great myth that closes Plato's Republic (616b–617d), the soul journeys beyond the heavens and sees the cosmos from outside: a column of light binding the sky together, and Necessity — Anankē — enthroned, with her daughters the Fates, turning the spindle that carries the whorls of the planetary spheres. 'The shaft and the hook of the spindle were of adamant,' Plato writes, while the great whorl mixed adamant with other materials. Adamant is the chosen metal of the world's axis: harder than anything the heavens contain, it alone is fit to be the pivot on which every sphere, every fate, every mortal life visibly turns. The choice is philosophically deliberate. The cosmos is not held up by Zeus's strength but by something stronger — a fixed, unyielding order that even the gods must revolve around. Through the later Platonists, and through Boethius, the image became canonical: fate turns on an adamantine hinge.

Lapidary

The Stone That Breaks the Anvil

For the ancient naturalists adamas was not only a poetic metal but a real substance — and an infuriating one. Pliny devotes a long entry to it (Natural History 37.61–65): a small, dark, impregnable stone that no anvil survives, that resists fire and hammer alike, and that some said could be split only by soaking it in goat's blood. Pliny's account is a museum of marvels and errors, but its testimony is decisive for the word's history: by the first century CE, adamas had drifted from 'unbreakable metal' toward 'hardest known stone' — the ancestor, through Latin adamas, Vulgar Latin *diamantem, and Old French diamant, of the English diamond. The lapidary tradition then laid a moral on the mineral: like faith, the true stone cannot be crushed. The myth of the stone that breaks its tools outlived the tools — modern mineralogy still measures every material against diamond, adamas's direct heir, on the Mohs scale.

02

The Unbreakable

Invincible Substance, Obdurate Will, Philosophical Symbol

Adámas is the Greek word for the hardest, most untamable substance — the adamant. In Homer it is the metal of chains that bind even gods; in later philosophy it becomes a metaphor for an unyielding soul. The word carries no cult of its own, yet it cuts across Greek literature as the image of absolute resistance.

Adamantine Substance

The hardest material known to Greek poets; used for chains, weapons, and the unbreakable core of things.

Bonds of the Gods

In Homer and tragedy, adamantine fetters hold Prometheus, Ares, and other immortals fast.

Invincible Resolve

Philosophers from Plato to the Stoics use adámas as an image of an unconquerable mind.

Diamond Legacy

The medieval and modern word 'diamond' descends from this Greek root, preserving its brilliance and hardness.

Sacred Symbols

Adamantine sickle The weapon used by Kronos to castrate Ouranos, and by Zeus in some traditions
Chains of Prometheus The unbreakable bonds that fix the Titan to the Caucasus in Aeschylus
Diamond The later gemstone identified with adámas because of its incomparable hardness
Anvil and forge The context in which adamantine metal is worked, if at all, by divine smiths

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

ἀδάμας

The name in its original Greek form. Adámas (ἀδάμας) is attested in the source tradition — “Unbreakable; the adamant; the hardest substance; origin of "diamond"”. Its acute accents carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

adamas

Reduced to plain adamas, the name loses everything that made it specific: acute accents. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Adámas

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Adámas restores acute accents, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Adámas.com → xn--admas-yqa.com

The non-ASCII characters in Adámas are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Adámas.

Pronunciation

How Adámas was spoken

/a.dá.mas/ Attic Greek Reconstruction
a- Short alpha [a], unstressed first syllable in the adjective ἀδάμας.
-dá- Delta plus acute on short alpha [da] — the pitch peak of the word.
-mas Mu-alpha-sigma; the -ας ending marks a third-declension noun/adjective stem.

Modern Approximation

'ah-DAH-mahs' — stress falls on the middle syllable, with a crisp final -s.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Adámas is Tier 2 because the Greek ἀδάμας preserves the acute stress on the second syllable but lacks a long vowel. The word is not a deity but a material concept that became a philosophical emblem of invincibility.

Extended Lore

Adámas teaches that resistance has its own beauty. The adamant does not bend; it holds. In a world that often confuses flexibility with virtue, the concept reminds us that some things must not yield: integrity, memory, the truth. Yet adámas is also a warning. Chains of adamant bind Prometheus not because the Titan is wrong, but because power can harden against justice. The same substance that protects can imprison.
