Cult & Worship

How the sacred was kept — rites, sanctuaries, remains

The Cult

Ratatoskr received no cult, and nothing in the Norse world suggests anyone ever addressed a prayer to him. His entire medieval existence is two passages — one stanza of Grímnismál and one chapter of Snorri's Gylfaginning — and two passages are not the foundation of a sanctuary: no runestone invokes him, no votive deposit names him, no place-name or legal formula preserves his cult, and no saga reports a rite performed in his honour. The medieval North did not worship even Yggdrasill, on whose trunk he runs; the squirrels the skalds knew were real animals of the northern forests, pressed here into cosmic service as the one creature of the tree defined by speech rather than appetite. What later centuries have made of him is reverence of a wholly different order: modern Heathen and Ásatrú retellings keep him running as the emblem of malicious speech — a literary devotion to a literary creature, with no ancient or medieval rite standing behind it.

In the Earth

Ratatoskr is an entirely textual creature: no picture stone, runestone, amulet, or toponym preserves him, and the squirrel is otherwise invisible in Norse material culture. His two attestations hang on the great codices: Grímnismál in the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda (c. 1270), and Gylfaginning in the Codex Regius of the Prose Edda and its sister manuscripts (early 14th c.), with the poem's stanza also copied into the Codex Wormianus and the Uppsala Edda tradition. The world-ash itself has material echoes — the tree-and-beasts carvings sometimes compared to Yggdrasill's menagerie — but none can be tied to the squirrel by name. His evidence is the manuscript page alone, which is fitting: Ratatoskr was always made of words.

05

Iconography

How Ratatoskr was imaged across the ages

The Image

No image of Ratatoskr survives from any period before modern illustration, and the attribute-system is the whole picture. The texts fix three elements: the trunk of the world-ash as his road, the eagle in the crown and Níðhǫggr at the root as his two correspondents, and Snorri's cargo of öfundarorð — slander — as the load he carries up and down. The Viking-Age carvers who pictured world-tree menageries — the tree-and-beasts compositions of the Gotland stones sometimes compared with the Edda's ash — never, so far as any surviving stone shows, carved the squirrel by name or feature, and the medieval Edda manuscripts do not illustrate him. His visual tradition begins with the nineteenth-century handbooks of Norse myth, where Romantic illustrators set a small dark squirrel mid-trunk as the tree's one running line; that composition — eagle above, dragon below, squirrel between — has never since been improved upon, because it is simply Grímnismál drawn. Every later artist has kept the same three fixed points.

06

Reception & Transformation

The name after its own age — transmission, translation, survival

Afterlives

Ratatoskr's journey is a study in how little text a durable figure needs. His stanza — Grímnismál 32 — survives in the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, the vellum that surfaced in Iceland in 1643 and passed to Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson, who hailed it as the work of the sagacious Sæmundr; Peder Resen's Edda Islandorum of 1665 put both poem and squirrel into print for the first time, with a Latin crib for the learned of Europe. Snorri's fuller prose portrait travelled in the codices of the Prose Edda — the Codex Regius, the Codex Wormianus, the Uppsala codex — manuscripts copied and recopied in Iceland and preserved into the age of print. Nineteenth-century philology gave the name its double life: Cleasby–Vigfusson's dictionary printed the word and weighed its elements, the rati of the boring auger against rata, 'to travel' — a dispute still honestly open — while comparative mythology, from Grimm onward, set the eagle-and-serpent tree beside the Mesopotamian Etana myth and made the squirrel a standard exhibit in the long history of the world-tree motif. Every handbook since has kept him running; none has needed to add a word to his dossier.

Identifications Across Traditions

Ratatoskr belongs to a pattern far older and wider than Iceland: the tree that holds the worlds, the eagle in its crown, the serpent at its root. The famous ancient parallel is the Mesopotamian Etana myth, where the eagle and the serpent dwell in the same great tree and their broken pact drives the story — a resemblance scholars have noted for over a century without being able to trace a line of borrowing; Lindow and Simek treat the configuration as a widely distributed world-tree motif rather than a loan. Within the North, the squirrel has no counterpart at all: he is the Edda's own invention, the one member of the tree's menagerie defined by speech rather than appetite. His nearest functional kin are Óðinn's ravens, who likewise range the world and return with words — but the ravens gather news, and Ratatoskr manufactures a feud. Comparativists have also pointed to the squirrel as the classic trickster go-between: small, deniable, and indispensable to enemies who must not be seen to speak.

Modern Legacy

Ratatoskr's afterlife grew with the tree he runs. Padraic Colum's The Children of Odin (1920) carried the squirrel into English-language retellings for children, and every popular handbook since has kept him running. Modern readings have made him the patron of the poisonous go-between — the forwarder of insults, the courier of feuds — a role that has only sharpened in the age of the message-board and the screenshot. He has become a minor star of games and pop culture: a playable god in Smite (2015), and the scene-stealing squirrel of God of War Ragnarök (2022), where the ancient slander-courier is played, faithfully to Snorri, as the most insulting creature in the Nine Realms. In Iceland the name lives on as Ratatoskur, and modern Heathen and Ásatrú retellings keep him as the comic-dark emblem of how small tongues keep great hatreds fed.

The Original Script Note

What the written record preserves — and what it does not

Original Script

The normalized Old Norse form is Ratatoskr — already plain ASCII, with no diacritic or distinctive letter for the restoration to recover; in the Younger Futhark the name would have been carved ᚱᛅᛏᛅᛏᚢᛋᚴᛦ (r-a-t-a-t-u-s-k-R, the ᚢ rune writing o and ᛦ the final nominative -r), though no runic attestation survives and the manuscript spelling remains the primary witness. Because the name is pure ASCII, its domain was registered in the ordinary way: ratatoskr.com is held by a third party outside the PuniCodex collection and is watched for release — no form of the name is currently owned, and this temple claims none.

The Meditation

What Ratatoskr means — beyond the dictionary

Ratatoskr is the Edda's smallest cosmological fact and one of its sharpest. The universe is held in a tree; the tree is being eaten from crown to root; and through the middle of the disaster runs a squirrel, up and down, carrying the worst things the top and the bottom can think of to say to each other. Snorri's word for the cargo is exact: öfundarorð — not news, not warnings, but malice. The myth knows something the etiquette books miss, that feuds do not survive on hatred alone but on delivery — that between every great enmity runs some small, quick, indispensable creature who keeps the words fresh. Ratatoskr never appears in a saga, never wins a fight, never speaks a line the sources record. He only carries. And the Edda gives him eternity for it: while the ash stands, the squirrel runs, and the eagle and the dragon never lack for ammunition.
