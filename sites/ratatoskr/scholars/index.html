01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ratatoskr (ratatoskr) ​— Squirrel of ‌Yggdrasil · ‌Drill-tooth — is ​the squirrel of ​the world-ash Yggdrasill: ​the small, ​tireless runner who ​bears the eagle's ‌words down from ​the crown ​of the tree ‌and carries Níðhǫggr's ​answers back up ‌from the roots, ​keeping the ‌feud between the ​top and the ​bottom of the ​cosmos supplied ​with fresh insults.[1]

​His attestation is ‌as narrow as ​his road ‌is long. One ​stanza of the ‌Poetic Edda's Grímnismál ​names him ‌and his office ​— ‘Ratatoskr is ‌the squirrel who ​must run ​on Yggdrasill's ash; ‌the eagle's words ‌he must bear ‌from above and ​tell to ​Níðhǫggr below’ — ‌and one chapter ​of Snorri's Gylfaginning ​fills out ‌the scene, adding ​the hawk Veðrfölnir ​between the eagle's ‌eyes and ​naming the squirrel's ‌cargo as öfundarorð, ​‘malicious words’.[1][2] Nothing ​else in ​the Old Norse ​corpus mentions him.

‌The name is ‌a compound: ​rati, ‘borer, auger’ ‌— the very ​name of the ​gimlet Óðinn drives ‌through the ‌mountain in the ‌mead-myth — and ‌toskr, ‘tusk, tooth’, ​whence the ‌standard gloss ‘drill-tooth’; ​a rival derivation ​from rata, ‘to ​travel, find ‌one's way’, yields ​‘travel-tooth’, and the ​dispute is genuinely ​open.[3] PuniCodex ‌restores the name ​as Ratatoskr — ​identical to the ​ASCII input, ‌since normalized Old ​Norse writes no ‌diacritic in it ​— and the ‌mechanical rule ​therefore places it ‌in Tier 2. ‌No domain is ​claimed: the ​plain-ASCII ratatoskr.com is ​registered to a ​third party outside ‌the collection ‌and is watched ​for release.

  1. Grímnismál 32 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 16, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  3. Cleasby, Richard, and Guðbrandur Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. ratatoskr, toskr; Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr.
02

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in Old ‌Norse as Ratatoskr ‌(nominative), a compound ​of two ordinary words. ​The second element ​is secure: toskr, ‘tusk, ​tooth’, cognate with ​Old English tusc. ‌The first is the ​philologist's problem. The ​standard reading takes ‌rati, ‘borer, auger’ — ​the name of ‌the drill with which ​Óðinn bores into ‌the mountain Hnitbjǫrg ​in the mead-of-poetry myth ​(Hávamál 106; Skáldskaparmál) ​— giving ‘drill-tooth’, a ​tooth that bores: ​exactly the instrument ‌a squirrel carries in ​its face.[1][2]

The ‌alternative derives the ​first element from the ‌verb rata, ‘to ​travel, to find one's ‌way’, yielding ‘travel-tooth’ ​— a derivation ‌the handbooks register without ​settling, and one ​whose fit for the ‌tireless courier of ‌the world-tree has ‌tempted more than one ​commentator.[3] What is ​not in dispute ‌is the word-class: Ratatoskr ​is a mythological ​coinage, never an ordinary ‌human anthroponym, and ​appears nowhere outside ​the two Eddic witnesses.

‌The ASCII form ​survives unchanged because normalized ‌Old Norse writes ​the name in ​plain Latin letters; the ​Unicode restoration Ratatoskr ​differs from the input ‌only by its ‌capital. Because the ​restoration preserves nothing the ‌ASCII form loses ​— no diacritic, ​no distinctive letter, no ‌vowel length — ‌the name is classified ‌Tier 2, a ‌statement about orthography ​and never about the ‌creature.

The letter-by-letter ​transformation runs:

  • r ​→ R — ​Same, capitalized
  • ‌a → a — ​Same
  • t ​→ t — ​Same
  • a → ‌a — Same

​- t → t ​— Same

  • ​o → o ‌— Same
  • s ​→ s — ‌Same
  • k → ​k — Same

‌- r → ​r — Same

The ‌project holds no ‌domain for this ​name: the plain-ASCII ratatoskr.com ​is registered outside ​the collection and watched ​for release, and ‌this temple stands ‌without an address of ​its own.

03

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈrɑ.tɑ.toskr/ — ​Old Norse Reconstruction.[1]

​Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • Ra- ​— Trilled ​[r], short ‌open [ɑ]; the ​stress falls ​here, on the ‌first syllable, ​as in every ‌Old Norse ​word
  • -ta- ‌— Voiceless ​dental stop [t], ​short open ​[ɑ]
  • -toskr ​— Voiceless ​stop [t], short ‌rounded [o], ​then the ‌cluster [skr]: sibilant, ​velar stop, ‌and the final ​nominative [r], ‌lightly syllabic as ​in modern ‌Icelandic

For the ​modern speaker, ​the closest approximation ‌is: ‘RAH-tah-toskr’ ‌— three even ‌beats with ​the weight on ​the first, ‌all vowels ​clipped short, and ​the final ‌-r sounded as ​a light ​trill, as in ‌Icelandic Ratatoskur.

​Kindred and historical ‌forms of ​the name:

  • ​Old Norse ​— Ratatoskr, the ​íkorni (squirrel) ‌of the ‌world-ash
  • Name ​elements — ‌rati ‘borer, auger’ ​(Óðinn's gimlet) ​+ toskr ‘tusk, ‌tooth’ (cf. ‌Old English tusc)[2]

‌- Younger ‌Futhark — ᚱᛅᛏᛅᛏᚢᛋᚴᛦ, ​the normalized ‌Viking-Age runic rendering ​(reconstructed; unattested)
​- Modern Icelandic ​— Ratatoskur, ‌the living ​form

  • Owned ​form — ​none; the temple ‌claims no ​domain

Ratatoskr is ​Tier 2 ​because the restoration ‌preserves nothing ​the ASCII alphabet ‌cannot write: ​no diacritic, no ‌distinctive letter, ​no length mark. ‌The name ‌travels from ​manuscript to address ​bar without ​losing a single ​feature — ‌the tier rule ‌records that ​fact mechanically.

Sources

  1. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), phonology and grammar.
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. ratatoskr, toskr.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts ​in the ​Latin alphabet ​as Ratatoskr ​— Old ​Norse (North ‌Germanic), attested ​13th c. ​CE in ‌the Codex ​Regius of ‌the Poetic ​Edda (Grímnismál) ‌and the ​early 14th-c. ​Codex Regius ​of the ​Prose Edda ​(Gylfaginning).[1] Before ‌the Latin ​bookhand, Old ‌Norse names ​were carved ‌in the ​Younger Futhark; ‌no runic ​attestation of ‌this name ​survives, so ​the manuscript ‌form is ‌the sole ‌witness.

The ​scholarly rendering ​proceeds step ‌by step:

​- The ​compound is ‌rati ‘borer, ​auger’ + ​toskr ‘tusk, ‌tooth’, with the ​nominative singular ‌ending -r.[2]
​- Old ​Norse orthography ​writes the ​name in ‌nine plain ‌Latin letters; ​no length ‌marks or ​special characters ​occur.

  • ‌In the ‌Younger Futhark ‌the name ‌maps to ​ᚱᛅᛏᛅᛏᚢᛋᚴᛦ (r-a-t-a-t-u-s-k-R): ‌the ᚢ ​rune writes ​the short ​o, and ‌ᛦ writes ​the final ​nominative -r ​— a ‌normalized reconstruction, ​not an ​attested carving.[3]

​- The ‌Unicode restoration ​Ratatoskr therefore ‌differs from ​the ASCII ‌form ratatoskr ​only by ‌its capital; ‌nothing distinctive ​is lost ​in transit, ​and the ​name is ‌Tier 2 ‌by the ​mechanical rule.

Sources

  1. Larrington, Carolyne, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014), note on Grímnismál; Faulkes, Anthony, Edda: Prologue and Gylfaginning (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1982), manuscript history.
  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. ratatoskr, toskr.
  3. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ratatoskr is ​the courier ‌of the ‌world-ash — ​the one ​creature in ​Yggdrasill's great ​menagerie defined ​by an ‌errand rather ​than by ​appetite, and ‌the only ​messenger in ‌the corpus ​whose cargo ‌is mischief ​itself.[1]

​The World-Tree's ​Courier

Grímnismál ​sets him ​his route ‌with the ​modal skal ‌— he ​must run: ‌from the ​eagle in ‌the crown ​to Níðhǫggr at ‌the root, ​the full ​height of ‌the cosmos ‌in a ‌squirrel's stride.[1]

​### Mischief

​Snorri names ‌the cargo: ​öfundarorð, ‘words ​of malice’ ‌— the ​slander-tidings that ​keep the ‌eagle above ​and the ‌dragon below ​forever at ​enmity.[2]

​The Vertical ​Axis

His ‌road is ‌the trunk ​of Yggdrasill ‌itself, the ​axis on ​which the ‌Nine Worlds ‌hang; no ‌other creature in ‌the Edda ​is given ‌the whole ​height of ​the universe ​as a ‌daily commute.[2]

​### The ​Tree's Menagerie

​He runs ‌in company: ​the unnamed ​eagle and ​the hawk ‌Veðrfölnir above, ​the four ‌harts in ​the branches, ‌the serpents ​and Níðhǫggr ‌beneath the ‌roots in ​Niflheimr — ​a household ​of gnawers ​and watchers ‌among whom ‌the squirrel ​alone speaks.[1][2]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 32-35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 15-16, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

Ratatoskr's ​iconography is ‌fixed ‌by two ​passages and ​nothing ​else — ​but ​those passages ‌equip ​him completely:[1]

​- ‌The world-ash ​— Yggdrasill, ‌‘the ​greatest and ‌best ​of all ​trees’: ​his road, ​his ​world, and ‌the axis ​of ‌everyone else's[2]
​- ‌The malicious ​message ‌— öfundarorð, ​Snorri's ‌exact word ​for the ​cargo: ‌not news ‌but ‌malice, carried ​between ​powers too ‌proud ​to speak ​directly[1]

  • ‌The ​drill-tooth — ​the ‌name itself, ​rati ‌+ toskr: ​the ​boring auger ​and the ​tusk, ‌a tooth ‌that ​drills and ‌a ​tongue that ​bores[3]

‌- The ‌hawk between ‌the ‌eyes — ​Veðrfölnir, ‌perched in ​the ​eagle's brow ​above ‌the squirrel's ​route: the ​unblinking ​audit over ‌every ​errand[1]

  • ​The ​four harts ‌— ​Dáinn, Dvalinn, ‌Duneyrr, Duraþrór, ​biting ‌the buds ​of ‌the ash ‌he ​runs: the ​tree's ​other tenants, ​eaters where ‌he ‌is a ​talker[1]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ratatoskr's mythology is ​two passages long — ‌one stanza and one prose ‌chapter — and it ​is complete: a road, a ​cargo, a character. No saga, ​þula, or skaldic verse ​adds a word to him.

​### The Squirrel's Stanza ‌(Grímnismál 32)

In Grímnismál, Óðinn ​— masked as Grímnir ​and bound between the fires ‌of King Geirrøðr — recites ​the inhabitants of the ‌world-ash, and the squirrel receives ​his stanza: Ratatoskr is ‌the íkorni who must run ​on Yggdrasill's ash; the ​eagle's words he must bear ​from above and tell to ​Níðhǫggr below. The verb ​is skal, ‘shall, must’: the ‌running is an office, ​written into the tree's economy ‌as fixedly as the ​dew that falls from its ‌branches.[1]

The Suffering Ash ​(Grímnismál 33-35)

The surrounding ‌stanzas build the tree's full ​menagerie and its misery. ‌Four harts bite the new ​shoots; more serpents lie ​beneath the ash than any ‌unwise ape would think — ‌Góinn and Móinn, Grábakr ‌and Grafvölluðr, Ófnir and Sváfnir ​— and gnaw the ​roots forever. Then the poem's ‌most famous sentence: the ​ash of Yggdrasill suffers agony ​more than men know — ‌the hart bites from ​above, the trunk rots at ​the side, and Níðhǫggr ‌gnaws from below. Ratatoskr runs ​his messages through a ‌tree being eaten alive at ​every level.[1]

Malice ​Up and Down the Trunk ​(Gylfaginning 16)

Snorri fills the ​stanza into a portrait. ‌An eagle sits in the ‌limbs of the ash ​and has knowledge of many ‌things, and between its ​eyes sits a hawk called ​Veðrfölnir; the squirrel called Ratatoskr ‌springs up and down ‌the ash and bears öfundarorð ‌— words of malice ‌— between the eagle and ​Níðhǫggr. Where Grímnismál described ‌a messenger, Snorri describes an ​agent provocateur: the squirrel does ​not merely deliver the ​correspondence of crown and root, ‌he keeps the feud ​alive.[2]

The Dragon at ​the Root (Gylfaginning 15 ​· Völuspá 66)

The lower ‌terminus of the squirrel's road ​is the worst address ​in the cosmos: the third ​root of the ash, ‌over Niflheimr and the spring ​Hvergelmir, where Níðhǫggr gnaws ‌from below among countless serpents.[2] ​Völuspá gives the Malice-Striker his ‌other aspect — sucking ​the corpses of the dead ‌at Nástrǫnd, and rising ‌at last from Niðafjǫll with ​dead men on his ​wings — so that every ​word Ratatoskr carries down lands ​in the ear of ‌the world's undertaker, and every ‌word he carries up ​rises from it.[3]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 32-35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 15-16, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  3. Völuspá 66 and the Nástrǫnd stanzas (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Ratatoskr belongs ​to a ‌configuration far ‌older and ​wider than ​Iceland: the world-tree ​with an ​eagle in ​its crown ‌and a ​serpent at its ​root. The ‌famous ancient ​parallel is ‌the Mesopotamian ​Etana legend, ‌in which an ​eagle and ​a serpent ​dwell in ​a single ​great tree and ‌their broken ​pact drives ‌the plot ​— a ‌resemblance noted by ​comparativists for ‌over a ​century. No ‌line of ​borrowing has ever ​been demonstrated, ‌and the ‌standard handbooks ‌treat the ​eagle-and-serpent tree as ​a widely ‌distributed motif ​rather than ​a loan ‌into the North.[1]

​Within the ​Germanic world ‌the squirrel ​has no ‌counterpart at ​all: he is ​the Edda's ​own addition ​to the ‌tree-menagerie, and ‌the only member ​defined by ‌speech. His ​nearest functional ​kin are ‌Óðinn's ravens Huginn ‌and Muninn, ‌who likewise ‌range the ​world and ‌return with words ​— but ​the ravens ​gather news, ‌while Ratatoskr, ​on Snorri's telling, ​manufactures a ​feud.[2] Related ‌figures in ​the corpus ​include Yggdrasill (the ​tree he ‌runs), Mímir ​(whose well ‌stands beneath ​another of ‌the tree's roots), ​and Jǫrmungandr ‌(the pantheon's ‌other great ​serpent, though ​bound in the ​sea and ​no correspondent ‌of the ‌dragon at ​the root).

Sources

  1. Dalley, Stephanie, Myths from Mesopotamia (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1989), the Etana legend; Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Yggdrasill, Ratatoskr.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr, Huginn and Muninn.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Ratatoskr's ​afterlife grew ‌with the ‌tree he ​runs. ​Padraic Colum's ​The Children ​of Odin ​(1920) ‌carried the ​squirrel into ​English-language retellings, ‌and every ​popular ‌handbook of ​Norse myth ‌since has ​kept ​him running ​up and ​down the ​ash.[1] ‌Twentieth- and ​twenty-first-century readings ‌sharpened Snorri's ​hint ‌into an ​emblem: the ‌squirrel as ​the ‌patron of ​the poisonous ​go-between — ‌the forwarder ‌of ‌insults, the ​courier who ​keeps enemies ‌supplied ​— a ​role that ‌has only ​gained ​force in ‌the age ​of the ‌forwarded ​message.

He ​has become ​a minor ​star ‌of games ‌and popular ​culture: a ‌playable ​god in ​Smite (2015), ‌and the ‌scene-stealing squirrel ‌of ‌God of ​War Ragnarök ‌(2022), where ​the ​ancient slander-courier ​is played, ‌faithfully to ​Snorri, ​as the ​most insulting ‌creature in ​the ​Nine Realms. ​In Iceland ‌the name ​lives ‌on as ​Ratatoskur, and ‌modern Heathen ​and Ásatrú ‌retellings ‌keep him ​as the ​comic-dark emblem ​of ​how small ‌tongues keep ‌great hatreds ​fed.[2]

Sources

  1. Colum, Padraic, The Children of Odin: The Book of Northern Myths (New York: Macmillan, 1920).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr (on the figure's reception and interpretive history).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

Ratatoskr ​is an ‌entirely ‌textual creature: ​no ​picture ​stone, runestone, ​amulet, ​or toponym ‌preserves ​him, and ​the ‌squirrel is ​otherwise ‌invisible ​in Norse ‌material ​culture.[1] His ​two ​attestations hang ​on ​the great ‌codices. ​Grímnismál ‌survives in ​the ‌Codex Regius ​of ‌the Poetic ​Edda ‌(c. 1270), ​and ​Gylfaginning ‌in the ‌early ‌fourteenth-century Codex ​Regius ​of the ‌Prose ​Edda and ​its ‌sister ​manuscripts, with ​the ‌Grímnismál stanzas ​on ‌the world-ash ​also ​copied ​into the ​Codex ‌Wormianus and ‌the ​Uppsala Edda ‌tradition.[2]

​The world-ash ​itself ‌has ‌material echoes ‌— ‌the animal-and-tree ​compositions ‌of Scandinavian ​and ​Insular art ​are ‌sometimes ​compared to ​Yggdrasill's ​menagerie — ‌but ​none can ​be ​tied to ‌the ​squirrel ‌by name. ​His ‌evidence is ​the ‌manuscript page ‌alone, ​which is ​fitting ​for ​a figure ‌made ‌entirely of ​words.[1]

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda owns ‌Ratatoskr outright: ‌his one stanza ​is Grímnismál ​32, spoken ​by the masked ​Óðinn in ​the catalogue ‌of the world-ash's ​inhabitants. ‘Ratatoskr ​is the squirrel ‌who must ​run on ‌Yggdrasill's ash; the ​eagle's words ‌he must ​bear from above ​and tell ​to Níðhǫggr ​below’ — the ​modal skal ‌makes the ​running an office, ‌not a ​habit.[1]

The ‌stanzas that frame ​him matter ‌as much as ​his own. ‌Grímnismál 33 ​names the four ​harts that ‌bite the ‌shoots; 34 counts ‌the serpents ​beneath the ​ash — more ‌than any ​unwise ape ​would think — ‌and 35 ​delivers the ​poem's most famous ‌sentence: the ​ash suffers agony, ‌the hart ​bites from ​above, the trunk ​rots at ​the side, ‌Níðhǫggr gnaws from ‌below. The ​squirrel is ‌the only member ​of this ​menagerie who ‌carries anything.[1]

Elsewhere ‌the Edda ‌supplies his ‌name's raw material ​and his ‌correspondent's dossier. Hávamál ​106 names ​Rati, the ​auger Óðinn drives ‌through the ​mountain for ​the mead of ​poetry — ‌the first ​element of ‘drill-tooth’.[2] ​And Völuspá ​gives Níðhǫggr ‌his full horror: ​sucking corpses ‌at Nástrǫnd, ​and at the ‌end rising ​from Niðafjǫll with ‌dead men ‌on his ​wings — the ​ear into ​which the ​squirrel has poured ‌the eagle's ‌words for ​all time.[3]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 32-35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Hávamál 106 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Völuspá, the Nástrǫnd stanzas and stanza 66 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is ‌the squirrel's ‌portraitist. Gylfaginning ​16 turns ​Grímnismál's bare ​stanza into ​a scene: an ​eagle sits ‌in the ​limbs of ​the ash ‌and has ​knowledge of ‌many things; between ​its eyes ‌sits the ​hawk Veðrfölnir; ​and the ​squirrel called ​Ratatoskr springs up ​and down ‌the ash ​and bears ‌öfundarorð — ​words of ‌malice — ​between the eagle ‌and Níðhǫggr. ​Four harts, ‌named there ​as Dáinn, ​Dvalinn, Duneyrr ‌and Duraþrór, ‌run among the ‌branches and ​bite the ​buds.[1]

Snorri's ‌contribution is ​the single ​word that ‌defines the figure: ​öfundarorð. Grímnismál's ​squirrel is ‌a messenger; ​Snorri's is ‌an instigator, ​a courier whose ​deliveries exist ​to keep ​crown and ‌root at ‌war. Interpreters ​have long ‌debated how much ​of this ​is Snorri's ‌own sharpening ‌of the ‌stanza — ‌the poem ​says only that ‌the eagle's ​words are ​carried down ​— but ‌the slander-reading ​has governed ​the figure's whole ​afterlife, from ‌the handbooks ​to the ​games.[2] Gylfaginning ​15 supplies ‌the geography of ​his road: ‌the three ​roots of ‌the ash ​over Asgardr, ‌Jǫtunheimr and ‌Niflheimr, the wells ​Urðarbrunnr, Mímisbrunnr ​and Hvergelmir ​beneath them, ​and Níðhǫggr ‌gnawing the ‌Niflheimr root ​from below.[1]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 15-16, trans. Faulkes (1987).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr.

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌is ‌silent on ​Ratatoskr, ​and the ​silence ​is expected: ​the name ‌is ​a mythological ​coinage ‌attested only ​in ‌the two ​Eddic ‌witnesses, never ​an ​anthroponym, and ​no ​stone, amulet, ​or ‌graffito yet ​found carves ‌it.[1] ​In this ‌he ​differs from ‌the ​great gods, ‌whose ​names or ​bynames ‌surface in ‌the ‌epigraphic record; ​the ​squirrel belongs ‌entirely to ​the ​world of ‌the ​parchment page.

​What ‌the runes ​can ‌supply is ​a ​normalized rendering. ​In ​the sixteen-rune ‌Younger Futhark ‌of ​the Viking ‌Age ​the name ​would ‌have been ‌carved ‌ᚱᛅᛏᛅᛏᚢᛋᚴᛦ — ‌transliterated ​r-a-t-a-t-u-s-k-R, with ‌the ​ᚢ rune ​writing ​the short ‌o and ​ᛦ ​carrying the ​final ‌nominative -r. ​The ​rendering is ​a ‌reconstruction in ​the ‌standard convention, ​not ‌an attestation: ​it ‌shows only ‌how the ​manuscript ​name maps ​onto ​the runic ‌row, ‌underspecifications and ​all.[2]

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957), on the runic rows and their transliteration.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond ​the Eddas, ‌the medieval ‌record is ​empty, and ​it is ​worth saying ​plainly. Ratatoskr ​appears in ‌no Íslendingasaga, ​no fornaldarsaga, ​no ‌king's saga; ​Saxo Grammaticus, ‌whose Gesta ​Danorum retells ‌so much ​of the ​divine mythology ​in euhemerized ​form, has ​no squirrel ‌at ​the world-tree; ‌and the ​skaldic corpus ‌never uses ​his name ‌as a ​heiti or ‌in a ​kenning.[1] Even ​the þulur ‌— ‌the versified ‌name-lists that ​preserve the ​Old Norse ‌vocabulary of ​creatures — ​add nothing ‌to his ​dossier beyond ​the Grímnismál ‌stanza ​some of ‌them copy.

​The absence ​is itself ​the datum. ​Ratatoskr is ‌a cosmographic ‌figure, not ​a narrative ‌one: he ​exists ​to populate ‌a description ‌of the ‌universe's plumbing, ‌and once ​the description ‌was given, ​the tradition ​never asked ​him to ‌do ​anything else. ​His entire ​story is ‌the office ​Grímnismál assigns ​him — ​run, bear, ‌tell — ​and the ‌character Snorri ​reads ‌into it. ​Everything after, ‌from Colum's ‌children's book ​to the ​modern games, ​is reception ​of those ‌two passages ‌and no ​more.[2]

Sources

  1. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Ratatoskr.
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ratatoskr.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Ratatoskr ​is the ‌Edda's smallest ‌cosmological fact ​and ​one of ​its sharpest. ​The ​universe is ‌held in ​a tree; ​the ‌tree is ​being eaten ‌from crown ​to ‌root; and ​through the ​middle ​of the ​disaster runs ​a squirrel, ‌up ​and down, ‌carrying the ​worst things ‌the ​top and ‌the bottom ​can ‌think of ​to say ​to each ‌other.[1]

‌Snorri's word ‌for the ​cargo is ​exact: ‌öfundarorð — ​not news, ​not ‌warnings, but ​malice. The ​myth knows ‌something ​the etiquette ‌books miss: ​that feuds ​do ​not survive ​on hatred ‌alone ‌but on ​delivery — ‌that between ​every ​great enmity ‌runs some ‌small, quick, ‌indispensable ‌creature who ​keeps the ‌words ​fresh. Ratatoskr ​never fights, ​never schemes ‌on ​his own ​account, never ​speaks a ‌line ​the sources ​record. He ​only ‌carries. And ​the Edda ‌gives him ​eternity ‌for it: ​while the ‌ash stands, ‌the ​squirrel runs, ​and the ​eagle ​and the ‌dragon never ‌lack for ​ammunition.[2]

Sources

  1. Grímnismál 32-35 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 16, trans. Faulkes (1987).
