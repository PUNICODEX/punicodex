Overview

Hwanin (hananim) — Supreme God, Heaven · Lord of Heaven — belongs to the Korean ​tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Supreme God, Heaven". The name means "Lord of Heaven".

Hwanin is the supreme celestial figure who rules from heaven in the Dangun founding myth in ​Korean tradition. The name means "Lord of Heaven" and belongs to the domain "Supreme God, Heaven." In the Samguk Yusa, Hwanin is the ruler of heaven who answers his son Hwanung's wish ​for the earth by choosing the sacred site and sending him down with three thousand retainers and the heavenly seals of authority. Mythology

The mythology of Hwanin is preserved in Korean oral ​tradition, shamanic ritual texts, and classical sources such as the Samguk Yusa. The narratives are not merely stories; they encode social values, ritual obligations, and relationships between humans, nature, and the sacred. ‌Some details vary by region and by shamanic lineage.

The Request from Heaven (Samguk Yusa)

According to Iryeon's Samguk Yusa, the Buddha asked Hwanin whether there was a land on earth where his ​teachings could flourish. Hwanin looked down and saw the Korean peninsula, then offered his son Hwanung to descend with heavenly attendants and sacred regalia.

The Descent of Hwanung (Foundation myth)

Hwanin granted Hwanung ‌three heavenly seals and three thousand retainers. Hwanung descended beneath a sandalwood tree on a high mountain, bringing laws, grains, and the arts of civilisation to the human world.

Hananim and Korean ​Religion (Living usage)

The vernacular title Hananim became the standard Korean Christian name for God, while in folk and shamanic contexts Hwanin remains the celestial ancestor of the Korean people. The two usages ‌live in parallel, one theological and one mythological.

The Lord of Heaven Sends His Son (Samguk Yusa)

Iryeon's Samguk Yusa opens the Korean story with Hwanin, 桓因, whom the text itself glosses as ​Indra — the Buddhist Śakra, lord of the thirty-three heavens. When his son Hwanung, 桓雄, gazed down upon the human world and asked to rule it, Hwanin looked down in turn, chose ‌the fairest spot under heaven, and fixed there the sacred altar. He gave his son three heavenly seals and a retinue of three thousand, and Hwanung descended beneath a sandalwood tree on Taebaeksan, ​bearing the tokens of rule, the arts of law, grains, and medicine. (Iryeon, Samguk Yusa, 'Gojoseon' chapter, trans. Ha and Mintz, pp. 32–41.) The myth is striking because heaven is bureaucratic as well ​as holy: authority travels downward from father to son, sealed and documented, so that Korean kingship arrives on earth already carrying heaven's warrant.

The Tiger and the Bear (Transformation myth)

Living beneath ‌the sandalwood tree, Hwanung was approached by a tiger and a she-bear who begged him to make them human. The god gave them twenty cloves of garlic and a bundle of mugwort and ‌set a single condition: eat only this and shun the sunlight for one hundred days. The tiger could not endure the discipline and fled back to the wilds; the bear persevered, and on ‌the twenty-first day she shed her fur and became a woman, Ungnyeo — the bear-woman. Childless, she prayed beneath the tree, and Hwanung took her as his wife. She bore a son, ​Wanggeom, 'the prince of the sandalwood,' whom heaven chose as the first king of the Korean people: Dangun. (Samguk Yusa, 'Gojoseon.') The tale ranks patience above strength: the beast that submits to law ​and time becomes the ancestress of a nation, while the stronger animal is forgotten.

Dangun's Fifteen Centuries (Foundation myth)

Dangun Wanggeom, child of the bear-woman and the son of heaven, founded Gojoseon — ‌'Old Joseon' — in 2333 BCE by the myth's traditional dating, and ruled for fifteen hundred years. When his time on earth was done he did not die as mortals die: he ​returned to the mountains and became a god of the peaks, Asadal, the spirit who still presides over the land he governed. Iryeon adds that Dangun's priests, the Jeceon, 'worship heaven' at his ​shrine, tying the ancestral cult directly to the heaven that sent him. The myth's grand claim is easy to miss: Korean kingship is not a human invention or a conquest but a family ‌inheritance, carried down through Hwanin and Hwanung and never surrendered — its founder still holds the mountains in trust for his descendants.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Hwanin concentrates in ​a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • The heavens — The celestial realm from which Hwanin dispenses authority and blessing.
  • The sandalwood tree — The axis linking ​heaven and earth in the Dangun myth; the place where Hwanung alighted.
  • The heavenly seal — The token of delegated authority that Hwanin gives to Hwanung.
  • Sun and moon — Celestial emblems of ‌the luminous, ordering presence of heaven.
  • Mugwort and garlic — The hundred-day food of the bear-woman — the austere diet that turns a beast into the ancestress of the Korean people.

Across Cultures

Hwanin entered Korean Christian vocabulary as Hananim, the name for the biblical God, while in shamanic ‌and nationalist discourse he remains the mythic ancestor of the Korean people. The two traditions interpret the name differently—one as a personal deity, the other as a celestial title—but both preserve the sense ​that Korea's legitimacy flows from heaven. Confucian and Buddhist interpreters have also read Hwanin through their own categories, sometimes as a personification of cosmic principle and sometimes as a devotional figure.

Within the ​Korean tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Baekdusan, Chilsong, Dangun, Halmoni, Hwanung, and Jowangshin.

Cultural Legacy

Hwanin survives in Korean public life chiefly through the Dangun myth, which is taught ‌in schools, celebrated on National Foundation Day (Gaecheonjeol), and invoked in discussions of Korean identity. The name Hananim remains one of the most common words for God in Korean Christianity, while Hwanin continues ‌to appear in scholarly reconstructions of early Korean religion, in museum exhibitions on Gojoseon, and in cultural diplomacy that presents Korea as a nation with ancient, heaven-blessed foundations.

A Meditation

Hwanin is a god known almost entirely through his absence from the story he frames. He never ‌descends; he appoints, seals, and sends. The active divine work of the Korean foundation myth is done by his son and grandson — Hwanung who rules beneath the sandalwood tree, Dangun who ​founds the state — while Hwanin remains above, the unmoved source of a moved world. That structure is the myth's theology in miniature: heaven does not administer the earth directly but delegates, authorizes, ​and watches, and legitimacy on the peninsula is real only insofar as it can trace that delegation back upward to its source.

The restoration of the name matters in the same register. 'Hananim' ​in ASCII flattens a title — the Lord of Heaven, 환인, 桓因 — into a string a registrar can process; Hwanin restores the capital, the characters, and the centuries-old claim that a ‌Korean word can carry the highest meaning in the language: the Christian God, the shamanic ancestor, and the bureaucratic father of kings all live inside it at once.
