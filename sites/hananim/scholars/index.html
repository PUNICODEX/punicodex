Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Hwanin (hananim) ​— Supreme ‌God, Heaven ‌· Lord ​of Heaven ​— belongs ​to the ​Korean tradition, ​where it ‌is catalogued ​under the ​domain "Supreme God, ‌Heaven". The ​name means ‌"Lord of ​Heaven"[1].

Hwanin ‌is the ​supreme celestial ​figure who ​rules from ​heaven in ​the Dangun ‌founding myth in ​Korean tradition. ‌The name ​means "Lord ‌of Heaven" ​and belongs ‌to the ​domain "Supreme ‌God, Heaven." ​In the ​Samguk Yusa, ‌Hwanin is the ‌ruler of ‌heaven who ​answers the ​Buddha's request ‌for a ​land fit ​for his ‌teachings by ​sending his ​son Hwanung ‌to earth. Hwanin ​is not ‌a creator ​in the ​biblical sense; ​he is ​the apex ‌of a ‌celestial hierarchy ​that authorises ‌the descent ​of culture and ​law to ‌the Korean ‌peninsula.[2]

PuniCodex ‌restores the ‌name as ​Hwanin and ‌serves its ​temple at ​its temple. ​The restoration ‌needs no distinctive ​letters or ​diacritics its ​ASCII form ‌would lose, ​which places ​the name ​in Tier ‌2. The ​plain ASCII ‌form hananim ​survives as a ‌modern convenience ​imposed by ‌the early ‌domain-name system; ​the restoration, ​not the ​fallback, is ​the form ‌the project ‌defends as ​philologically complete[3].

Sources

  1. Iryeon, Samguk Yusa (Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms), 1281; English trans. Ha Tae-Hung and Grafton K. Mintz (1972).
  2. Lee, Peter H., ed., Sourcebook of Korean Civilization, vol. 1 (Columbia University Press, 1993).
  3. Grayson, James H., Korea: A Religious and Cultural History (Routledge, 2002).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested ‌in Hangul ​as ​하나님. ​Etymologically it ​means ​"Lord of ‌Heaven"[1].

​The ASCII ​form ‌hananim survives ​only ‌because ​the early ‌domain-name ​system could ​not ​carry diacritics; ​it ​is a ‌technological ​compromise, ‌not an ​ancient ‌spelling. The ​Unicode ‌restoration Hwanin ​recovers ‌the full ​diacritic ​detail ‌of the ‌scholarly ‌transliteration directly ​in ​the address ‌bar. ​The restoration ​needs ‌no ​distinctive letters ​or ‌diacritics its ​ASCII ‌form would ​lose, ​which ​places the ​name ‌in Tier ‌2.

​The letter-by-letter ‌transformation ​runs:

  • ​h ‌→ ‌H — ‌Same, ‌capitalized
  • ​a ‌→ w ​— ​Special character

​- ‌n ​→ a ​— ​Special character
‌- ​a → ​n ​— Special ‌character
​- ‌n → ​i ‌— Special ​character
‌- i ‌→ ​n — ​Special ​character
​- m ‌→ ‌— — ​Dropped

Sources

  1. Iryeon, Samguk Yusa (Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms), 1281; English trans. Ha Tae-Hung and Grafton K. Mintz (1972).

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The ​name is ‌preserved ‌in Hangul ​as 하나님 ​— ​Korean alphabet, ​attested ​Modern Korean ‌(Hangul ​promulgated 1446 ​CE), ‌in Korea. ​The script ‌is ​written left-to-right.[1]

‌The ​scholarly transliteration ​is ​Hananim (Revised ​Romanization), ​giving the ‌normalized reading ​/ha.na.nim/.

‌The rendering ​proceeds ‌step by ​step:

‌- Standard ​modern ‌Hangul orthography ​for the ​shamanic ‌sky god
‌- ‌Also written ​하느님 ​(Haneullim); 하나님 ‌is ​shared with ​the modern ‌Christian ​usage

Korean ​names ‌may be ​written ‌in Hangul, ​the ​indigenous Korean ​alphabet, and ​sometimes ‌in Hanja, ‌the ​Sino-Korean character ‌set. ​For Hwanin, ​the ‌Hangul form ‌is 환인 ‌and ‌the Hanja ​form ‌is 桓因. ​Hangul ​‘환인’ and ​Hanja ‌‘桓因’, the ​forms used ​in ​the Samguk ‌Yusa; ​the name ​is ​also rendered ‌‘Hananim’ ​(하나님) in ‌Korean vernacular ​usage. ‌The ASCII ​form ‌hananim is ‌a ​technological compromise; ​the ​capitalised restoration ​Hwanin is ‌the ‌scholarly citation ​form.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Hwanin ​is the ‌supreme celestial ‌figure who ​rules from ​heaven in ​the Dangun ​founding myth ​in Korean ‌tradition. The ​name means ​"Lord of ‌Heaven" and ​belongs ‌to the ​domain "Supreme ‌God, Heaven." ​In the ​Samguk Yusa, ​Hwanin is ​the ruler ​of heaven ‌who answers ​the Buddha's ‌request for ​a ‌land fit ​for his ‌teachings by ​sending his ‌son Hwanung ​to earth. ​Hwanin is ‌not a ‌creator in ‌the biblical ​sense; he ​is the ‌apex ​of a ​celestial hierarchy ‌that authorises ​the descent ​of culture ‌and law ​to the ‌Korean peninsula.[1]

​### Lord ​of Heaven

​Hwanin sits ​at ‌the apex ‌of the ​Korean celestial ‌order, the ​source from ​which legitimate ‌authority descends.

‌### Celestial ‌Court

Heaven ‌is imagined ​as a ‌court with ​ministers, ​retainers, and ​an ethical ‌law that ​mirrors human ​government.

​Ancestor of ‌Hwanung

Hwanin ​authorises the ​descent that ​makes possible ‌the birth ​of ‌Dangun and ​the founding ‌of Korea.

​### Moral ‌Order

The ‌myth encodes ​the belief ​that rulers ​and peoples ​are accountable ‌to a ‌higher heavenly ​standard.

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

The mythology ​of Hwanin ‌is preserved ‌in Korean ​oral tradition, ​shamanic ritual ​texts, and ​classical sources ​such as ‌the Samguk ​Yusa. The ​narratives are ‌not merely ​stories; they ‌encode social ​values, ritual obligations, ‌and relationships ​between humans, ​nature, and ​the sacred. ​Some details ​vary by ‌region and ​by shamanic ‌lineage.[1]

​The Request ‌from Heaven ​(Samguk Yusa)

‌According to ​Iryeon's Samguk Yusa, ‌the Buddha ​asked Hwanin ​whether there ‌was a ‌land on ‌earth where ​his teachings ​could flourish. ‌Hwanin looked ​down and ​saw the ‌Korean peninsula, ​then offered ​his son ‌Hwanung to descend ​with heavenly ‌attendants and ​sacred regalia.[2]

​### The ​Descent of ​Hwanung (Foundation ‌myth)

Hwanin ‌granted Hwanung ​three heavenly ‌seals and ​three thousand ​retainers. Hwanung ‌descended beneath ‌a sandalwood ‌tree on a ‌high mountain, ​bringing laws, ‌grains, and ​the arts ​of civilisation ​to the ‌human world.

​### Hananim ​and Korean ​Religion (Living ‌usage)

The ​vernacular title ​Hananim became ​the standard Korean ‌Christian name ​for God, ‌while in ​folk and ‌shamanic contexts ​Hwanin remains ‌the celestial ‌ancestor of ​the Korean ​people. The ​two usages ​live in ‌parallel, one ‌theological and ​one mythological.

Sources

  1. Lee, Peter H., ed., Sourcebook of Korean Civilization, vol. 1 (Columbia University Press, 1993).
  2. Grayson, James H., Korea: A Religious and Cultural History (Routledge, 2002).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Hwanin ​entered ‌Korean ‌Christian ​vocabulary ​as Hananim, ​the ​name ​for ‌the ​biblical ​God, ‌while ​in ‌shamanic ​and nationalist ‌discourse ​he ​remains ​the ​mythic ​ancestor ‌of ​the ‌Korean people. ​The ‌two ​traditions ‌interpret ​the ‌name ​differently—one ​as ‌a ‌personal deity, ‌the ​other ​as ‌a ​celestial ​title—but ‌both ​preserve ​the ‌sense that ​Korea's ‌legitimacy ​flows ​from ​heaven. ​Confucian ‌and ‌Buddhist ​interpreters ‌have also ​read ​Hwanin ‌through ‌their ‌own ‌categories, ​sometimes ‌as ​a ​personification of ​cosmic ‌principle ​and ​sometimes ​as ‌a ​devotional ​figure.[1]

​Within the ‌Korean ​tradition, ‌closely ​related ‌names ​in ‌the ‌corpus ​include ​Baekdusan, Chilsong, ​Dangun, ​Halmoni, ‌Hwanung, ‌and ​Jowangshin.

Sources

  1. Iryeon, Samguk Yusa (Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms), 1281; English trans. Ha Tae-Hung and Grafton K. Mintz (1972).

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Hwanin ​survives ‌in ‌​Korean ​public ​life ​chiefly ​through ‌​the ​Dangun ‌myth, ​which ‌is ​taught ‌​in ​schools, ​celebrated ​on ​National ‌​Foundation ‌Day ​(Gaecheonjeol), ‌and ​invoked ‌​in ‌discussions ​of ​Korean ‌identity. ‌The ‌​name ​Hananim ‌remains ​one ​of ‌​the ​most ‌common ​words ‌for ​​God ​in ​Korean ‌Christianity, ‌while ​‌Hwanin ​continues ​to ‌appear ‌in ‌scholarly ‌​reconstructions ‌of ​early ​Korean ​religion, ‌​in ​museum ​exhibitions ‌on ​Gojoseon, ​​and ‌in ​cultural ‌diplomacy ​that ‌presents ​‌Korea ‌as ​a ​nation ​with ​‌ancient, ‌heaven-blessed ​foundations.[1]

Sources

  1. Iryeon, Samguk Yusa (Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms), 1281; English trans. Ha Tae-Hung and Grafton K. Mintz (1972).
