The Evidence

Exhibit 1 — the attestation. The tradition's record preserves this name in its romanized scholarly form: Ogma — no older script survives to transcribe, so the canonical content is the exactness of the spelling itself.

Exhibit 2 — the flattened form. The plain form ogma differs from the canonical Ogma only by convention of display — case, not content. It is acceptable everywhere: in legacy systems, in plain-text citation, in the DNS itself. What is not acceptable is what follows in the register: respellings that change the letters, not just their case.

Exhibit 3 — the physical record. Ogma is a textual god: no ogham stone names him, no votive inscription invokes him, and the irony is the corpus's own — the script the medieval scholars fathered on him survives on some four hundred stones of roughly the fourth to seventh centuries, bearing personal names in formulaic genitives, none of them his. His manuscript witnesses are exact: Cath Maige Tuired survives uniquely in the sixteenth-century British Library MS Harley 5280, the basis of Gray's edition; the Lebor Gabála Érenn is preserved in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster and its sister codices; and the Auraicept na n-Éces, the tract that credits him with the alphabet, comes down in the great late-medieval compilations such as the Book of Ballymote. The nearest material witness to his figure is classical rather than Celtic: Lucian's second-century description of the Gaulish Ogmios, the old Herakles of eloquence, is the continental portrait of the god, and the Gaulish name is otherwise attested on a small number of defixiones (curse tablets) — comparative evidence, never identification.

Exhibit 4 — the continuing record. Ogma's afterlife runs on the two rails his name already laid down: strength and letters. Lady Gregory's Gods and Fighting Men (1904) carried Cath Maige Tuired into English and made the champion of the firewood years part of the revival's reading; the later romantic cycle, in Oidheadh Chloinne Tuireann, made him the father of Tuireann and so grandfather of the three sons whose blood-price Lugh exacted.

The Convention's Own History

Every tradition in this register has its own scholarly transliteration standard, built by its own specialists over decades or centuries, and the register follows each on its own terms: the tradition’s own standard. The principle that unifies them is the one the whole project stands on: a name is evidence, and evidence is not negotiable — not for keyboards, not for convenience, not for anyone.

The Protocol's Position

Can the canonical form live in the address bar? Almost always, yes. IDNA 2008 admits every mark this register defends — the DNS stores them as `xn--` punycode and modern browsers render them natively. The real frictions are practical, not legal: some registrars stock only part of the IDN space, so a theoretically valid name can be temporarily unbuyable; and stacked combinations (an acute riding a macron) remain awkward to type on mobile keyboards even where the protocol accepts them.

The project's answer is a ranked ladder, not a compromise: the full restoration first, then attested fallbacks in descending order of completeness. The ladder's lowest rung, plain ASCII, is a vehicle for legacy systems — sanctioned, but never presented as the scholarly form.

The Verdict

Case closed on Ogma: canonical, cited, and defended. The keyboard form ogma is the same name in another register of display — always acceptable. The register's files remain open: if a source surfaces that changes any line of this entry, the entry changes. Until then, the temple at /ogma/ holds the canonical form.

The docket for this ruling: no false forms on the docket; no contested forms attested; the attestation rests on 10 cited sources — Cath Maige Tuired (The Second Battle of Mag Tuired), ed. and trans. Elizabeth A. Gray, Irish Texts Society 52 (1982) — the firewood under Bres, the flagstone test, Indech slain, Orna the speaking sword, the recovery of the harp, Lebor Gabála Érenn, ed. and trans. R. A. S. Macalister (Irish Texts Society, 1938–56) — Ogma son of Elatha, brother of the Dagda, champion of the Túatha Dé Danann, Auraicept na n-Éces (The Scholars' Primer), ed. George Calder (1917) — Ogma, well skilled in speech and poetry, as inventor of the ogham; the father of the ogham is Ogma, the mother is his hand or knife.
