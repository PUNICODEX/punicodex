Name and Meaning (Etymology)

The name means "He who goes forth shining; the Nahuatl sun god" and reflects the figure's role ​in the tradition. Tōnatiuh is the Nahuatl sun god, depicted as a warrior ascending from the east. The Aztecs believed that the present sun, the Fifth Sun, was born when the gods ‌sacrificed themselves at Teotihuacan; Tōnatiuh now demands blood sacrifice to continue his daily journey. His face is shown in the center of the Aztec calendar stone. Tonatiuh's aged face and tongue of ​obsidian knife imagery stress that the sun is a warrior who must be nourished by human hearts to defeat the forces of darkness.

Nahui Ollin — The Sun of Movement (The Fifth ‌Sun)

Before our sky there were four suns, each destroyed: 4-Jaguar, when jaguars devoured the giants; 4-Wind, when hurricanes swept the people from the earth; 4-Rain of Fire, when the sky rained ​flames; 4-Water, when the flood came and the people became fish (Anales de Cuauhtitlan; Codex Vaticanus A). After the flood the gods gathered in darkness at Teotihuacan, the 'Place Where the Gods ‌Were Made,' and asked who would light the world. The proud Tecuciztecatl and the humble, pox-scabbed god Nanahuatzin both offered to leap into the great fire. Tecuciztecatl faltered at the flames and ​leapt only at the shaming of the others; Nanahuatzin, who had no pride to lose, plunged straight in — and rose as the new sun, the moon following ashamed behind him (Florentine ‌Codex, Book VII). Our age is therefore Nahui Ollin, '4-Movement,' and its sign is the earthquake: the sun of the present world moves, and everything that moves can end. The old gods ​did not make our sky to last — they made it to be paid for.

The Sun That Demands Hearts (The Price of Motion)

When the new sun rose it would not ​move. 'How shall I live?' it said. 'I cannot move — feed me, nourish me' (Anales de Cuauhtitlan). The gods understood at once: no sun moves on its own strength. Quetzalcoatl took ‌the obsidian blade, and the gods went willingly to be opened — their hearts fed the fire, and only then did the sun begin its road across the sky. The chronicles and codices ‌name this sun Tōnatiuh, 'he who goes forth shining,' the same deity the fate-books enthrone at 13-Olin in the tonalamatl (Codex Borbonicus; Codex Vaticanus A). The tellings differ on the fire-leaper's name ‌— Nanahuatzin in Sahagún's version, Tōnatiuh in the calendar books — but they agree completely on the economy: daylight is a loan, and the debt is paid in what is most alive. ​Aztec war, tribute, and temple sacrifice are not cruelty for its own sake in this theology; they are the interest on the world's first meal.

Carried by the Warrior Dead (The Sky-Road)

​All day Tōnatiuh must be carried. From dawn to noon he climbs on his own heat; at noon his strength wavers, and the souls of warriors who died in battle and of ‌those taken by the water take up the burden — the eagle-warriors lifting him on their wings toward the west (Florentine Codex, Books II and VII). Below, his light is warred over ​by the tzitzimime, the star-demons who wait at the edges of the sky for the world to end; when the sun darkens in eclipse they descend to devour humanity, and the people ​raise a din of war-cries and drumbeats to drive them back. At evening Tōnatiuh sinks into the west and the dead warriors set him down, their service done until tomorrow's climb (Florentine ‌Codex, Book VII). The myth makes the noon sky a battlefield: every noon is a held bridge, every dusk a narrow escape, and every eclipse the world's enemies testing the door. A ​sun that must be carried is a sun that can be dropped — and the whole apparatus of Aztec war exists, in this theology, to keep the bearers numerous and willing.
