Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the ​name is /səˈhɑːdɛvə/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Sahadeva).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • Sa- — Short a; the prefix sa- means “with” — Sahadeva, “god with god”.
  • -ha- — The h ​is a light aspirate; the compound honours the twin-born’s doubled divinity.
  • -deva — “God”: the name is a sentence — a god attended by gods.

For the modern speaker, ​the closest approximation is: “suh-HAH-deh-vuh”

Mythology

Sahadeva’s mythology is the epic’s study ‌of wisdom without power: the youngest brother whose knowledge was perfect and whose influence was nil. He knew the dice-game was ruin, the war’s timing, the omens’ meaning — ​and the epic lets him speak each truth once, then moves on. His death on the mountain was for the one pride knowledge permits: the pride of being ‌right.

The Younger Twin (Ādi Parvan)

Mādrī called the Aśvin twins and bore two sons — Nakula first, Sahadeva second — both radiant as the gods. Orphaned at the ​funeral pyre that took Mādrī, the twins were raised by Kuntī as her own: the youngest of five, the astronomer among warriors, born with the physician-gods’ second sight.

The ‌Counsel Against the Dice (Sabhā Parvan)

When the challenge to the dice-game came, Sahadeva — who had calculated the stars and the odds — advised refusal to the ​last. The epic notes that the advice was heard and overridden; the fall of the Pāṇḍavas into thirteen years of loss begins at the moment the youngest brother’s arithmetic ‌is outvoted.

Tantripala the Cowherd (Virāṭa Parvan)

In the incognito year he served Virāṭa as Tantripala, keeper of the cattle — and the herds multiplied under his accounting. ​The disguise-book’s joke runs deepest here: the astronomer disguised as a cowherd ran the kingdom’s true prosperity while the king praised his other servants.

The Pride of the Right ‌(Svargārohaṇika)

On the final mountain, Sahadeva fell second — and Yudhiṣṭhira named his sin: ‘he thought none his equal in wisdom’. The epic’s most quietly devastating verdict: even the ​love of being right is a weight, and the wisest of the five carried his wisdom as others carried vanity.

Across Cultures

Sahadeva ​is the Aśvins’ second son — the epic’s accounting keeps the physician-gods’ line in the heroic blood — and later tradition made him the type of the jyotiṣika, the ​court astrologer whose charts rule kingdoms. The twins together became the subcontinent’s archetype of matched excellence in different keys: Nakula the body’s arts, Sahadeva the mind’s.

Within the ‌Sanskrit tradition, closely related names in the corpus include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.

Cultural Legacy

The twins’ names remain a matched set in Indian naming — ​Sahadeva a standing boy’s name meaning ‘attended by gods’. His unheeded-counsel scene is the epic’s image of every expert ignored by power; and the jyotiṣa tradition claims him ‌as its founding hero, the astronomer inside the world’s longest poem.
