The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Original Script

𒀭𒄊𒀕

The name in its original Mesopotamian form. Nergal (𒀭𒄊𒀕) is attested in the source tradition — “Lord of the great city”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

nergal

The plain nergal form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Nergal

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Nergal. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Nergal.com → nergal.com

The non-ASCII characters in Nergal are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Nergal.

Etymological Kin

  • Sumerian 𒀭𒄊𒇽𒃲 (dGÌR.UNUG.GAL), 'lord of the great city' — the cuneiform writing used at his cult centre of Kutha
  • Akkadian Nergal / Nerigal, the warrior and underworld god of the first-millennium cult
  • Syncretic name Erra, the plague-warrior of the Akkadian Epic of Erra, often identified with Nergal

The Accent / Script Rule

Nergal is Tier 2 because the restored form contains no distinctive diacritic, stress mark, or non-ASCII letter. The cuneiform original is conventionally transliterated with plain Latin characters; PuniCodex preserves that transparent spelling while noting that any vowel length or Sumerian accent must be reconstructed from comparative evidence.

05

Lord of the Great City

War, Plague, and the Underworld

Nergal is the Mesopotamian god in whom three terrible jurisdictions meet: the battlefield, the epidemic, and the realm of the dead. His name means 'lord of the great city', and his great city is Kutha (modern Tell Ibrahim), the cult centre from which his authority radiated across Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia. Where other war gods personify the glory of victory, Nergal personifies the unstoppable force that cuts life down — the lion, the plague-wind, and the furnace-heat of the netherworld.

He is not merely a destroyer. In the myth Nergal and Ereshkigal he descends to the underworld and becomes the consort and co-ruler of its queen; in the Epic of Erra his violence is checked only by the counsel of the god Ishum. His mythology is therefore a meditation on the difference between raw destruction and the ordered power that even death requires.

The Lion of Kutha

The lion is Nergal's animal; he is the kingly predator whose roar announces war and whose jaws close on the dying.

The Plague-Arrow

Nergal sends pestilence as a warrior sends arrows; epidemics are his invisible campaign against the living.

The Underworld Throne

By marriage to Ereshkigal, Nergal becomes king of the dead; his throne stands at the lowest gate of the cosmos.

The Epic of Erra

In the poem of Erra, Nergal's burning fury almost consumes Babylon until the subordinate god Ishum stays his hand.

06

Mythology

Stories of Nergal

Nergal's myths are myths of descent and dominion. He begins as a god of the upper air — the son of Enlil — and ends as king of the underworld through violence that turns into sovereignty. His stories are preserved in Sumerian hymns, in the Akkadian Epic of Erra, and in the two major versions of the Nergal and Ereshkigal myth.

Descent

Nergal and Ereshkigal

In the Standard Babylonian version of Nergal and Ereshkigal, the queen of the underworld sends her messenger Namtar to complain that Nergal has refused to bow before her. The gods order Nergal to descend, but warn him not to accept any gift or pleasure offered in the netherworld. At each of the seven gates he strips away a divine attribute, enters the throne room, seizes Ereshkigal by the hair, and raises his sword to kill her. She offers him kingship over the underworld and her hand in marriage; he accepts, and the two rule the dead together. The older Amarna version tells a similar story of banishment, seduction, and shared sovereignty.

Plague

The Epic of Erra

The Akkadian Epic of Erra (also known as Erra and Ishum) opens with the warrior-god Erra, identified with Nergal, plotting to devastate Babylon. He persuades Marduk to leave his statue unadorned so that the city's protective power wanes, then unleashes war, fire, and plague across Mesopotamia. The god Ishum, 'the wise one', finally calms Erra by reminding him that excessive destruction is counterproductive and that his own reputation depends on the survival of civilization. The poem is a meditation on the limits of violence and the political theology of catastrophe.

Birth

Son of Enlil

The Sumerian myth Enlil and Ninlil explains Nergal's origin through the exile of the young Enlil from Nippur. After Enlil impregnates Ninlil by the canal and is banished to the underworld, he meets her three times in disguise — as the gatekeeper, the river-man, and the ferryman — begetting three underworld gods. Nergal is the first of these children, born at the gate of the netherworld. The story makes him a legitimate descendant of the king of the gods while also binding him permanently to the realm of the dead.

Cult

The Lion of Kutha

Nergal's principal cult centre was Kutha (Sumerian Ki-ut), where his temple Emeslam stood. Royal inscriptions from the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian kings record repairs to the temple and offerings to 'Nergal of Kutha'. In hymns he is praised as the lion who strikes down enemies, the lord who burns the wicked, and the god who opens the earth to receive the dead. His worship persisted into the Hellenistic period, when Greek writers identified him with Ares and the planet Mars.

Extended Lore

To name Nergal is to name the moment when destruction becomes authority. He is not a chaotic demon but a god who descends through every gate, strips away his own divine equipment, and still emerges as sovereign. That descent is not a fall; it is a claim. The underworld, in his theology, is not an absence of order but a realm with its own king, its own queen, and its own laws.
