The Authentic Name

Unicode restoration and ASCII comparison

Scholarly Transliteration

Ceridwen

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Ceridwen is the standard Celtic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “Bent or crooked woman”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual celtic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

ceridwen

The plain ceridwen form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Ceridwen

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Ceridwen. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Punycode Encoding
Ceridwen.com → ceridwen.com

The non-ASCII characters in Ceridwen are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Ceridwen.

Pronunciation

How Ceridwen was spoken

/kɛrˈɪd.wɛn/ Modern Welsh
Cer- Voiceless velar stop [k] — Welsh c is always hard — with short open [ɛ] and trilled [r]; the light, unstressed first syllable.
-id- Short close [ɪ] under stress with a voiced dental [d]; Welsh words stress the penultimate syllable, so the weight of the name lands here.
-wen Labial-velar [w] plus open-mid [ɛ] and alveolar [n]; the unstressed ultima closes quietly.

Modern Approximation

'keh-RID-wen' — every c is hard, the r is rolled, and the stress falls on the middle syllable, the Welsh penult.

Etymological Kin

  • Medieval Welsh verse Kerritwen, Kyrridven — the early spellings preserved in the poetry that names her
  • Ifor Williams (reconstruction) Cyridfen, 'crooked woman' — the original form the greatest editor of Welsh poetry proposed; the lexicon's gloss follows it
  • Traditional analysis cerdd 'song, craft, poetry' + gwen 'white, fair, blessed' — 'fair of song, blessed of poetry', the romantic reading
  • Modern Welsh Ceridwen, the unbroken living form — and the name of the awen's keeper

The Accent / Script Rule

Ceridwen is Tier 2 by the mechanical rule: the Welsh form is already plain ASCII — there is no diacritic and no distinctive letter for the restoration to recover, and only the capital distinguishes the restored form from the input. The etymology is a genuine scholarly debate: Ifor Williams derived the name from an original Cyridfen, 'crooked woman', while the traditional analysis reads cerdd 'poetry' + gwen 'fair, blessed'; the handbooks register both rather than settle the question.

05

Inspiration, Transformation

The Cauldron of Awen, the Three Drops, the Flight of Shapes

Ceridwen is the keeper of the cauldron of awen — the poetic inspiration of Welsh tradition. From her house by Llyn Tegid she brews a year and a day for the three drops of inspired knowledge, hunts the servant-boy who steals them through every shape in creation, swallows him as a grain of wheat, and gives him back to the world as Taliesin, the greatest poet Wales remembers. The early Welsh sources concur on the essential fact: she is the owner of the cauldron that is the source of poetic inspiration.

The Cauldron of Awen

Boiled a year and a day from the books of the feryllt, the alchemist's art, until three drops of inspired knowledge should rise from it.

The Three Drops

All the virtue of the brew gathered in three drops — meant for her ugly son, splashed instead on the finger of Gwion Bach, who put it to his lips.

The Flight of Shapes

Hare and greyhound, fish and otter, bird and hawk — the pursuit through every form until the grain of wheat meets the high-crested black hen.

Llyn Tegid

The lake of Tegid Foel — modern Bala Lake, the largest natural lake in Wales — beside which her household stood and which still keeps her husband's name.

Sacred Symbols

The cauldron Inspiration brewed by art and patience — knowledge as something cooked, not granted
The three drops The whole of awen compressed into an accident — inspiration that chooses its own vessel
The high-crested black hen The final shape of the hunt: the swallowing that is not death but the first cell of a poet
The hide bag The coracle-skin sewn round the unwanted child and given to the sea — rejection that becomes deliverance
The weir on the Dyfi Gwyddno's salmon-weir where the bag comes to rest — the threshold between the hunted boy and the radiant brow
Llyn Tegid The lake that still bears her husband's name — the story's anchor in real Welsh geography
06

Mythology

Stories of Ceridwen

Ceridwen's whole dossier is a single tale — Hanes Taliesin, the Tale of Taliesin — preserved in sixteenth-century Welsh manuscripts and customarily printed with the Mabinogion, though it is none of the Four Branches. Within that tale she owns its great engine: the cauldron, the hunt, and the rebirth.

Hanes Taliesin

The Household by Llyn Tegid

In the beginning, the tale says, Ceridwen lived beside Llyn Tegid in the land of Penllyn, the wife of Tegid Foel — Tegid the Bald — whose name the lake still carries. They had two children whose fortunes were weighed in opposite scales: a daughter, Creirwy, the fairest maiden in the world, and a son, Morfran, so dark and ill-favoured that he was called Afagddu, Utter Darkness. No god could have made the boy handsome; but Ceridwen was skilled in the arts, and she resolved to give him what nature had refused — knowledge and the gift of poetry, so that his birth might yet be honoured among men of culture.

Hanes Taliesin

The Cauldron of a Year and a Day

From the books of the feryllt — the Welsh name for the alchemist's art — Ceridwen compounded a cauldron of Inspiration and Science. It must boil a year and a day, ceaselessly, until three drops of the spirit of inspiration should be won from it. To keep the vigil she set a blind man, Morda, to kindle the fire beneath, and a servant-boy, Gwion Bach, son of Gwreang of Llanfair in Powys, to stir the pot. The design was exact and the patience enormous: everything in the tale that follows is the unmaking of this design by a single splash.

Hanes Taliesin

The Three Drops and the Flight of Shapes

At the year's end the three charmed drops leapt from the cauldron — and fell, not on Morfran, but on the finger of Gwion Bach. The boy put his burning finger in his mouth, and in that instant all the awen was his: he knew all that was past and all that was to come, and knew above all that Ceridwen would kill him. He fled. She pursued; and the pursuit is the oldest Welsh telling of the magic flight, the transformation chase of the folktales. He became a hare; she became a greyhound and gained on him. He leapt into a river and became a fish; she became an otter-bitch behind him. He rose into the air as a bird; she was a hawk on his wing. Every shape he took, she took its hunter.

Hanes Taliesin

The Grain of Wheat and the Black Hen

Spent at last, Gwion dropped into a barn and hid himself as one grain of winnowed wheat on the threshing-floor. Ceridwen became a high-crested black hen, went to the wheat, found the grain, and swallowed it. The swallowing is the tale's hinge: she carried him nine months, and when she bore him again he was so beautiful that she could not do what she had sworn — she could not kill him. So she sewed him in a hide bag and cast him into the sea, to the mercy of God, on the twenty-ninth of April. With that, Ceridwen passes out of her own story; she never appears again.

Hanes Taliesin

The Radiant Brow

The sea carried the bag to the salmon-weir of Gwyddno Garanhir, near the mouth of the Dyfi, and there on May Day Gwyddno's son Elffin found it among the fish. Opening it, he saw the boy's forehead and cried out what became a name: 'Behold the radiant brow' — tal iesin. 'Taliesin be I called,' the child answered, and as Elffin carried him home on horseback the boy sang his first poem, a song of consolation: Fair Elphin, cease your weeping. Years later, when Maelgwn Gwynedd imprisoned Elffin, the boy Taliesin went to the king's court, shamed every bard in the hall with poems they could not match, foretold the king's death, and freed his foster-father. The inspiration Ceridwen brewed for one son had become the making of another: the greatest poet of the Welsh tradition is, in the end, her child — swallowed, carried, born again, and set adrift by her own hands.

Extended Lore

Ceridwen is the only great figure of Welsh tradition whose myth is a failure, and whose failure is a gift. She plans everything: the books consulted, the fire kept, the boy set to stir, the year counted to its day. And the plan is perfect except for the one thing no plan commands — where the drops fall. The inspiration she cooked for the son she pitied goes to the son she hunts, and everything she does to recover it only completes the gift: the chase teaches him every shape in creation, the swallowing carries him through the longest transformation of all, and the bag she sews to drown him is the coracle that brings him to his name.
