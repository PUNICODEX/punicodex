Overview

Tjinimin (tjinimin) — Ancestor of the Peoples, Murrinh-Patha — belongs to the Aboriginal Australian tradition. The PuniCodex lexicon classes the figure as 'Trickster, Moth Man', but the published Murrinh-Patha sources associate Tjinimin with the ​bat or flying fox and with Kunmanggur, the great Rainbow Serpent.

Tjinimin is remembered as an ancestral being whose story encodes law, consequence, and transformation. After a quarrel with Kunmanggur over the Green Parrot-Girls, he hung ​upside down in a tree, vowed never to have sex again, and lost his nose — an event said to explain the short nose of the bat. The narrative is used in initiation teaching and is ​held by Murrinh-Patha custodians of the Daly River region, Northern Territory.

PuniCodex restores the name as Tjinimin. The plain ASCII form tjinimin is identical except for the lack of an initial capital; the Unicode restoration therefore ​supplies the conventional scholarly citation form rather than recovering a lost diacritic or original-script mark. Because no distinctive non-ASCII feature is added, the entry is a plain restoration. The capitalised form is the philologically complete reference; ‌the lowercase ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

Reflection

Tjinimin embodies the ancestor who is changed by his own transgression. He is not a hero but a cautionary figure: one who desired ​what was not his, lost, and made a vow that reshaped his body. The bat's short nose is the permanent sign of that vow.

The name's restoration is modest — only an initial capital is added ​— yet even this small mark matters. It distinguishes the proper name from a plain string and signals that the figure is a person in a scholarly citation, not merely a word. The ASCII form flattens ‌the name into a utilitarian label; the Unicode restoration returns the dignity of a capital letter, the conventional sign of a named being.

The Name

Etymology

The name Tjinimin is a Murrinh-Patha proper noun. No published source ​gives a transparent internal etymology, and it should not be analysed as a compound of separate common nouns without specialist linguistic evidence. The spelling follows the orthographic conventions used by W. E. H. Stanner and later ​ethnographers, in which tj represents a palato-alveolar affricate similar to English 'ch'.

The PuniCodex lexicon glosses the name as 'The moth man', but this meaning is not attested in the scholarly record. Stanner's account identifies Tjinimin ​as an ancestor associated with the bat and the Rainbow Serpent, not the moth. The 'moth' gloss may derive from a secondary source, a transcriptional confusion, or an outdated compendium entry. It is flagged here as ‌uncertain and should be verified with Murrinh-Patha language custodians before being treated as canonical.

Because the name is already rendered in basic Latin letters, the Unicode restoration does not recover a lost original script or diacritic. ​Its scholarly value lies in establishing a consistent, capitalised citation form.

The Original Script

The name is preserved as a Murrinh-Patha proper noun in scholarly transcription. No indigenous writing system is securely attested for Murrinh-Patha in ‌the pre-contact period; the language was transmitted orally and through ceremony, rock art, and material culture. The form Tjinimin is therefore a modern Latin transcription rather than a recovery of an original script.

The scholarly transliteration ​is Tjinimin, giving the normalised reading /tʃɪnɪmɪn/. The transcription tj represents a palato-alveolar affricate; the vowels are short and the stress falls lightly on the first syllable. Because the source language was unwritten in the pre-contact ‌period, the label 'Original Script' is not applicable; the appropriate label is 'Scholarly Transliteration'.

For the English speaker, the ​closest approximation is: 'CHIN-ih-min' — pronounce the initial 'tj' as 'ch', keep all vowels short, and place a light stress on the first syllable. Murrinh-Patha phonology includes contrastive consonants not fully captured by this transcription, but ‌the approximation is sufficient for scholarly citation.

Mythology

The central Murrinh-Patha narrative of Tjinimin, recorded by Stanner, is ‌an etiological myth of transformation and restraint. Tjinimin quarrelled with Kunmanggur, the Rainbow Serpent, because he desired Kunmanggur's consorts, the Green Parrot-Girls. Defeated in the dispute, Tjinimin withdrew and hung himself upside down in a tree. ​Looking at the stars, he vowed that he would never have sex again. Soon after, his nose fell off, and this is said to be the reason bats in the region have short noses.

The story ​functions as a charter for social and sexual law. The ancestor's transgression, defeat, and permanent bodily change make visible the consequences of overstepping kinship boundaries. Read comparatively, the narrative belongs to a wider Australian pattern in ‌which ancestral actions are inscribed in animal form and landscape. Because Murrinh-Patha sacred narratives are restricted, the published version should be understood as an ethnographic summary rather than a full ceremonial text.

The Quarrel with Kunmanggur ​(Murrinh-Patha Dreaming)

In the beginning-time, when the ancestral beings walked in their first forms, Tjinimin set his desire against Kunmanggur, the great Rainbow Serpent, the water-being whose body made the rivers and whose authority ordered the ​relations between all the ancestral peoples. He coveted Kunmanggur's consorts, the Green Parrot-Girls, and pressed his claim in the quarrel that the old men still tell. Stanner recorded the narrative in the mid-twentieth century from Murrinh-Patha ‌speakers at Port Keats (Wadeye), and it must be said that the published account is the publicly speakable layer of the story: senior custodians hold fuller versions that ethnography does not print. What the published telling ​makes plain is the myth's charge — desire aimed at what is not one's own, aimed, moreover, at the possessions of the most powerful law-giver in the country. The quarrel defines the boundary of the permissible ‌by showing what it costs to cross it.

The Vow beneath the Stars (Murrinh-Patha Dreaming)

Defeated in the quarrel, Tjinimin withdrew from the world in the most literal way the country allows: he hung himself upside ​down in a tree, head toward the earth, feet toward the sky, and there he gazed at the stars. In that suspension, between the ground of the living and the camp of the dead, he made ​a vow that the Murrinh-Patha tell as absolute: he would never lie with a woman again. No council imposed this sentence and no serpent demanded it — the old men emphasize that Tjinimin pronounced it himself, ​and that such a vow, once spoken beneath the stars, binds the speaker past recall. He hung there through the nights, looking down at the world he had forfeited and up at the order he could ​no longer join. The myth makes law interesting: its deepest form is not punishment from above but sentence passed on oneself, self-exile from the ordinary human sentence of desire and consequence.

The Short Nose of the ‌Bat (Murrinh-Patha Dreaming)

Soon after the vow, the story says, Tjinimin's nose fell off. The detail is the myth's signature: it is the reason, the old men explain, that the bats of the Daly country have ‌short noses — the ancestral being's bodily transformation became the permanent anatomy of the animal with which he is thereafter identified. In Murrinh-Patha understanding this is not merely an etiological curiosity. The bat, the flying fox ​that camps in the river trees, is kin: its face is the visible record of an ancestral choice, read every evening as the colonies rise at dusk. Tjinimin's punishment and his animal form are the same ‌event — to be changed into the bat is both the consequence of the transgression and its memorial. The myth means that ancestral law is not written anywhere but on the bodies of the country: in ​the short nose of the bat, in the course of the river, any child can read what happens to those who reach for what the law withholds.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Bat or flying fox — the ​animal with which Tjinimin is associated; the myth explains the bat's short nose as the result of his ancestral transformation.
  • Rainbow Serpent (Kunmanggur) — the great water-spirit and law-giver whose authority Tjinimin challenges; the serpent marks ‌the boundary between proper and improper desire.
  • The upside-down tree — the place where Tjinimin hung after his defeat, suspended between earth and sky while he contemplated the stars.
  • Stars — the night sky that Tjinimin ‌gazed upon during his withdrawal; a symbol of reflection, abstinence, and lasting consequence.

Archaeology & Evidence

No fixed archaeological site is publicly identified with Tjinimin. Murrinh-Patha sacred geography is held by custodians and is not published ‌in open scholarly form. The primary 'site' of the narrative is the Daly River country of the Northern Territory, where the story is maintained through initiation, ceremony, and oral transmission.

Realm & Domain

Tjinimin is first an ‌ancestor: in Murrinh-Patha tradition he is remembered as an ancestor of the Australian peoples. His mythic episode with Kunmanggur places him at the intersection of kinship law, sexual propriety, and transformation. The bat or flying fox ​is his animal emblem; the short nose of the bat is explained as the bodily trace of his vow.

The figure also carries trickster colouration. He overreaches, is defeated, and withdraws permanently from the reproductive order. ‌Yet the story is not a celebration of trickery; it is a cautionary narrative about the consequences of violating ancestral law. In this respect Tjinimin resembles other Australian ancestor-beings whose transgressions become object lessons encoded in ​species and landscape.

The domain of Tjinimin must be understood within the specific Murrinh-Patha religious landscape of the Daly River region. The story is not open public entertainment; it belongs to initiated custodians and is transmitted ​in appropriate ceremonial contexts. Any modern use of the name — in branding, education, or cultural commentary — should respect these protocols and seek guidance from Murrinh-Patha authorities.

Across Cultures

Tjinimin belongs specifically to Murrinh-Patha tradition ​rather than to the wider cross-cultural networks of the Kimberley Wandjina or Arnhem Land Rainbow Serpent complexes. Within Murrinh-Patha cosmology he is grouped with the ancestor-figures whose stories encode law, kinship, and the origins of animal ‌species. Comparative scholarship sometimes links him to the broader Australian theme of the Moon-Man or to nocturnal transformation myths, but these connections remain speculative; the primary attested context is the Daly River region.

Cultural Legacy

Tjinimin remains part of Murrinh-Patha oral tradition and initiation teaching in the Daly River region. Unlike the Wandjina imagery ​of the Kimberley, which has entered international art markets under strict custodial protocols, Murrinh-Patha sacred narratives such as that of Tjinimin are not reproduced as open visual art. The figure's legacy is therefore primarily linguistic, ceremonial, ​and ethnographic.

In broader Australian discourse, Tjinimin appears in reference works and mythological compendia as an example of an ancestral being whose story explains an animal feature. The story is cited in discussions of Aboriginal law ‌narratives, the Rainbow Serpent complex, and the cultural significance of the bat or flying fox.

Today Tjinimin appears in Aboriginal Australian art, storytelling, environmental education, and cultural tourism, always under the guidance of the communities who ​hold the name. Bark paintings, rock-art sites, dance cycles, and welcome-to-country ceremonies keep the figure present in public life, while restricted knowledge remains within appropriate kin and ceremonial contexts. A Meditation

Tjinimin is a difficult ancestor because his story turns on failure rather than triumph. He desires what is not his, loses, withdraws, and is transformed. The myth ‌does not celebrate his ambition; it records the cost of transgression and the permanent mark it leaves on the body. In this he is close to the trickster type, but he is also a cautionary figure: ​the ancestor whose own choice limits him.

For contemporary readers, Tjinimin raises questions about the relationship between desire and law. What does it mean to make a vow that cannot be undone? How does a community ‌remember the one who overreached and was changed? The bat's short nose is not merely an etiological detail; it is a material reminder that ancestral actions continue to shape the living world.
