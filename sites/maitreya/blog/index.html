Metteyya at the End of the Decline ​(The Prophecy)

The oldest prophecy of the future Buddha stands in the Cakkavattisihanāda Sutta (DN 26): after the wheel-turning king Daḷhanemi, greed returns, human life shrinks to ten years — and then, ‌slowly, morality climbs back until people live eighty thousand years. In that age a Buddha named Metteyya will arise, fully awakened, attended by thousands, holding three great assemblies beneath a pavilion ​— and the wheel will turn a fourth time in this eon. Later Pāli texts place him now in Tuṣita heaven, where Śākyamuni himself waited before his final birth. The myth is ‌the Buddhist answer to a hard fact — every Buddha's teaching decays — and its answer is not despair but schedule: the wheel turns down and up again, and the next teacher ​is already named, already enthroned, already waiting.

The Descent at Ketumatī (The Sanskrit Prophecy)

The Sanskrit tradition gives the future a city and a plot. The Maitreyavyākaraṇa — transmitted within the ‌Divyāvadāna and translated repeatedly into Chinese and Tibetan — describes Maitreya's birth in splendid Ketumatī, when the wheel-turning king Saṅkha rules, lifespans run to eighty thousand years, and the Dharma has been ​forgotten. Born to the brahmin Subrahma, he will leave his palace by the four gates, meet an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a renunciant — the very sights that ‌unseated Śākyamuni — and awaken beneath a nāga-puṣpikā tree in a single night. The sūtra's sharpest promise is addressed to its hearers: those who receive this prophecy will meet Maitreya face ​to face. The myth makes eschatology personal — salvation not as catastrophe but as a scheduled appointment with a named teacher, and a ticket any listener can hold.

The Earliest Future on ​Earth (The Cult)

Maitreya is among the very first bodhisattvas to receive cult — earlier than most of the Mahāyāna pantheon. Gandhāran art of the Kuṣāṇa centuries (2nd–3rd c. CE) already shows ‌him in the dress of a princely ascetic: stūpa in the crown, water flask in hand, sometimes teaching with a wheel at his feet. The emblem choice is theology in stone ‌— the stūpa names him successor, the flask the medicine of the age to come; inscriptions call him simply Metr, the Middle Indic form. Along the Silk Road his cult became geography: ‌caravan towns from Khotan to Dunhuang painted his descent, and devotion to his coming seeded messianic movements whenever the age grew dark — most famously in China's rebellions of the sixth and ​tenth centuries, when "Maitreya has descended" was a battle cry. The meaning of the images: a religion that put its future in human shape, standing where the caravans could see him.
