01

Mythology

Stories of Maitreya

Maitreya's mythology is unique in the Buddhist world: it is scheduled but unfulfilled — prophecy rather than biography. The canon names him, the Mahāyāna sūtras detail his future career, and Asia's art made him the most visible future the ancient world ever gave a face.

The Prophecy

Metteyya at the End of the Decline

The oldest prophecy of the future Buddha stands in the Cakkavattisihanāda Sutta (DN 26): after the wheel-turning king Daḷhanemi, greed returns, human life shrinks to ten years — and then, slowly, morality climbs back until people live eighty thousand years. In that age a Buddha named Metteyya will arise, fully awakened, attended by thousands, holding three great assemblies beneath a pavilion — and the wheel will turn a fourth time in this eon. Later Pāli texts place him now in Tuṣita heaven, where Śākyamuni himself waited before his final birth. The myth is the Buddhist answer to a hard fact — every Buddha's teaching decays — and its answer is not despair but schedule: the wheel turns down and up again, and the next teacher is already named, already enthroned, already waiting.

The Sanskrit Prophecy

The Descent at Ketumatī

The Sanskrit tradition gives the future a city and a plot. The Maitreyavyākaraṇa — transmitted within the Divyāvadāna and translated repeatedly into Chinese and Tibetan — describes Maitreya's birth in splendid Ketumatī, when the wheel-turning king Saṅkha rules, lifespans run to eighty thousand years, and the Dharma has been forgotten. Born to the brahmin Subrahma, he will leave his palace by the four gates, meet an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a renunciant — the very sights that unseated Śākyamuni — and awaken beneath a nāga-puṣpikā tree in a single night. The sūtra's sharpest promise is addressed to its hearers: those who receive this prophecy will meet Maitreya face to face. The myth makes eschatology personal — salvation not as catastrophe but as a scheduled appointment with a named teacher, and a ticket any listener can hold.

The Cult

The Earliest Future on Earth

Maitreya is among the very first bodhisattvas to receive cult — earlier than most of the Mahāyāna pantheon. Gandhāran art of the Kuṣāṇa centuries (2nd–3rd c. CE) already shows him in the dress of a princely ascetic: stūpa in the crown, water flask in hand, sometimes teaching with a wheel at his feet. The emblem choice is theology in stone — the stūpa names him successor, the flask the medicine of the age to come; inscriptions call him simply Metr, the Middle Indic form. Along the Silk Road his cult became geography: caravan towns from Khotan to Dunhuang painted his descent, and devotion to his coming seeded messianic movements whenever the age grew dark — most famously in China's rebellions of the sixth and tenth centuries, when "Maitreya has descended" was a battle cry. The meaning of the images: a religion that put its future in human shape, standing where the caravans could see him.

The Chinese Turn

Budai, the Laughing Maitreya

In China the austere future Buddha was re-imagined as a fat, laughing monk. From the tenth century onward, the eccentric wanderer Budai (布袋, "Cloth Sack") — a historical monk of the Wuyue kingdom, d. 917, who carried sweets and toys for children and was believed to be Maitreya in disguise — replaced the princely bodhisattva in the popular imagination. The Jingde Record of the Transmission of the Lamp (1004) preserves his sayings, and his image, belly bared at the temple gate, became the most reproduced Buddhist figure in the world after the seated Śākyamuni. The transformation is doctrinal as much as comic: Maitreya's age is one of abundance and eighty-thousand-year lives, so his sign is a belly, not an emaciated rib-cage — a prophecy of plenty arriving as laughter, standing at the threshold of every Chinese monastery to remind the poor that the next age will be fed.

02

Maitreya

The Benevolent One — Bodhisattva of the Age to Come

Maitreya is the Buddha of the future — now a bodhisattva enthroned in Tuṣita heaven, waiting for the Dharma's decline to run its course. His name is his program: maitrī, loving-kindness, the first of the four immeasurables — a promise that the wheel, having turned down, will turn up again.

The Future Buddha

The prophecy of DN 26: after the Dharma's long decline and renewal, Metteyya will awaken when lifespans reach eighty thousand years.

Maitrī, Loving-Kindness

The name made doctrine — the first of the four brahmavihāras, boundless friendliness toward all beings.

Tuṣita Heaven

The realm of delight where he waits — and where Śākyamuni waited before his own final birth.

The Descent at Ketumatī

The Maitreyavyākaraṇa names his future city, his royal patron Saṅkha, and the tree under which he will wake.

Sacred Symbols

The stūpa in the crown Gandhāran iconography crowns him with a small stūpa — Śākyamuni's relic-shrine marking Maitreya as the appointed successor
The water flask (kamaṇḍalu) The āmra-ghataka of the princely ascetic — the elixir of the future age, medicine for a world whose Dharma has faded
The wheel of Dharma Set beneath his feet on Kushan reliefs — the teaching he will turn again, three assemblies strong
The nāga-puṣpikā tree The tree under which he will awaken in one night at Ketumatī — the future's Bodhi tree, named in the prophecy
The cloth sack (Budai) His Chinese transformation: the laughing monk with his sack of sweets, the future Buddha as abundance and belly-laugh

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

मैत्रेय

The name in its original Buddhist form. Maitreya (मैत्रेय) is attested in the source tradition — “'The Benevolent One' — the Bodhisattva who will return as the future Buddha”. Its original diacritics and script distinctions carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

maitreya

The plain maitreya form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Maitreya

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Maitreya. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

ASCII-Clean Name
Maitreya.com

This restoration is written entirely in ASCII letters — no Punycode encoding stands between Maitreya and the DNS. The domain reads identically to machines and to human eyes.

Pronunciation

How Maitreya was spoken

/ˈmɛːɪ̯.tɾe.jɐ/ Sanskrit Reconstruction
mai- The long diphthong ai — classically a single open 'e'-like glide — carrying the stress: MAI-tre-ya, not mai-TRAY-a.
-tre- Dental t with a tapped r and light e — the center of the name, quick and bright.
-ya Palatal y plus short a, the patronymic ending that makes maitrī, 'friendliness,' into a person.

Modern Approximation

'MIGH-tray-yuh' — stress forward on the diphthong; the name is built on maitrī, 'loving-kindness,' so it should sound warm, not martial.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Tier 2: 'Maitreya' is the ASCII-canonical form, with no diacritic-bearing feature lost in plain spelling — the name's restoration lies in its meaning (maitrī, loving-kindness) rather than its letters. Pāli Metteyya preserves the same sounds.

Extended Lore

Every religion promises an end; Buddhism alone promises a sequel. Maitreya is the argument that decline is not the last chapter — that the Dharma, having dimmed, will be re-taught in full, by a teacher already named, already enthroned in the heaven of delight. The figure dissolves despair without denying decay: the wheel does turn down, and it turns up again.
