Kǒng (孔) the clan name; zǐ (子) 'master' — the honorific title of the philosopher Confucius (Kong Qiu).

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Chinese honorific — 孔夫子 Kǒng Fūzǐ, “Master Kong” — the fuller honorific from which the Jesuits of Matteo Ricci’s circle coined the Latin name Confucius (late 16th c.).
  • Courtesy name — 仲尼 Zhòngní, the Master’s courtesy name (zì 字), the form early texts prefer for addressing him.
  • Surname — 孔 Kǒng, the family name — the stock from which the Kong lineage of Qufu still descends.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Kǒngzǐ in Later Traditions

The Master resisted deification in life — “while you do not yet know life, how can you know death?” (Analects 11.12) — but the Han state deified him anyway. Emperor Gāozǔ offered the first imperial sacrifice at his tomb in 195 BCE; by the Tang his posthumous titles swelled toward “King” and “Perfect Sage,” and Dǒng Zhòngshū gave his teaching cosmological armor. From Sòng times the neo-Confucians — above all Zhū Xī — rebuilt it into the metaphysics of East Asia, and for thirteen centuries the civil examinations made his classics the gateway to office in China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. The West received him through the Jesuits: Ricci’s circle Latinized Kǒng Fūzǐ as Confucius, and Voltaire kept his portrait like a household saint. Modernity first dethroned him — the New Culture Movement denounced him as the face of feudalism — and then reinstated him. Few mortals have been so many things to so many regimes.

Modern Legacy

His living monument is Qufu: the Temple of Confucius — consecrated in 478 BCE, the year after his death, when Duke Āi of Lǔ turned his former residence into a shrine — the adjacent Kong Mansion, and the Kong Lin cemetery, together the largest classical building complex in China after the Forbidden City. His bloodline is on record longer than any other on earth: the Kong genealogy counts more than eighty documented generations. From 605 to 1905 the empire’s examinations were built on his classics, making him — by crib-book, temple sacrifice, and schoolhouse portrait — the single most studied author in human history. Taiwan still keeps Teachers’ Day on his traditional birthday, 28 September; and the everyday word rú 儒, “scholar,” is his school’s oldest name.

In the Earth

Qufu is the material anchor: the Temple of Confucius stands on the site of his residence, consecrated by Duke Āi of Lǔ in 478 BCE and rebuilt to its present Ming-era scale; beside it the Kong Mansion housed his descendants until 1949, and the Kong Lin cemetery holds over 100,000 lineage burials with thousands of stelae. Far older than any temple, the Guōdiàn bamboo slips — a Warring States library buried c. 300 BCE at Hubei and excavated in 1993 — preserve pre-imperial versions of texts in his tradition, proving the school was copying and teaching his books within three centuries of his death. And the Wǔ Liáng shrine stone reliefs (Shandong, 151 CE) already show the iconic scene of the young Kǒngzǐ asking Lǎozǐ about the rites: the myth cut in stone within three and a half centuries of the man.

The Meditation

What Kǒngzǐ means — beyond the dictionary

Kǒngzǐ is the rare founder who disclaimed founding anything. “I transmit, I do not create.” What he transmitted was a question rather than an answer: how should a human being live among other human beings? His answers were deliberately unheroic — learn, practice the rites, keep your word, honor your parents, govern by example — and the unheroism is their strength. He is the teacher as such, the man whose greatness is measured entirely in students.

The restoration of his name matters in a way he might have appreciated: drop a single tone-mark and the Master’s name dissolves into a string of letters. Orthography, he taught by every example of his life, is not decoration but the foundation of the Way. To write Kǒngzǐ is to keep the bell ringing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Kǒngzǐ, Confucius, Moral Teaching, Ritual, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Kǒngzǐ?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Kǒngzǐ is /kʰʊŋ˨˩˦ tsɨ˨˩˦/ — approximately “KOHNG-dzuh,” each syllable on a dipping-then-rising melody; said in one breath, the first tone lifts and the second dips.

02What does Kǒngzǐ mean?

Kǒngzǐ means Master Kong in the chinese tradition.

03What are the symbols of Kǒngzǐ?

Kǒngzǐ is associated with Qilin of the birth-omen (The peace-beast said to have appeared before his birth, spitting a jade tablet announcing a child whose teaching would rule all under Heaven though he sat on no throne (later legend built on Shiji 47).), The Apricot Altar (Xíngtán 杏壇) (The teaching ground at Qufu where tradition seats the Master among his disciples; the phrase became a synonym for the school itself.), The Analects (Lúnyǔ 論語) (Twenty books of short exchanges — question, answer, sometimes only a sigh: the irreducible core of the tradition, memorized by every educated East Asian for two millennia.), The Stone Classics (The canon carved into stone at the imperial academy (the Xīpíng stones, 175–183 CE) so that no scribal error could corrupt the Master’s text.), The cypress of Qufu (The ancient cypresses at his tomb in the Kong Lin forest cemetery — living links in the longest documented family line on earth.).

04Why restore Kǒngzǐ in Unicode?

Plain ASCII kongzi strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Kǒngzǐ?

“The Qilin’s Jade Tablet (551 BCE)” — Kǒngzǐ is a historical man — born 551 BCE in the state of Lǔ — but the tradition did with his biography what it does with all founders: it polished it into legend. The Shǐjì of Sīmǎ Qiān (ch. 47) supplies the framework, the Lúnyǔ the voice; between them stands the mythic Master, born under a portent, refused by every court, and vindicated only after death. The full telling lives on the lore page.
