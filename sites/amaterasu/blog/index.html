How Amaterasu got its accent back

The ASCII form amaterasu is missing something. Amaterasu restores the marks the source language used to distinguish this name from a thousand others — and those marks change how the name is read, pronounced, and understood. This post explains, with the full scholarly record behind it, what each restored mark preserves: the Japanese characters evidence, the reconstructed sound, the myths the name carries, and the classification logic that separates Tier 1 restorations from Tier 2. Overview

Amaterasu (amaterasu) — Sun Goddess, Shintō — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "Sun goddess in Shinto".

Amaterasu is the sun itself made kami — the great august kami who illuminates heaven and the ancestor of the imperial house. Her withdrawal into the Heavenly Rock Cave and her restoration by dance and persuasion form the central crisis of Shinto cosmology.

PuniCodex restores the name as Amaterasu. The plain ASCII form amaterasu is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is placed in Tier 2. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 天照. Etymologically it means "Sun goddess in Shinto".

The name is usually analyzed as ama-terasu, "shining in heaven," from ama (heaven) and terasu (to shine, to illuminate). The fuller cult name is Amaterasu-ōmikami, "the great august kami who illuminates heaven." She is the ancestral kami (sojin) of the imperial house and the central deity of the Shinto pantheon.

The ASCII form amaterasu survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Amaterasu gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is Tier 2 because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 天照 — Japanese logographic and syllabic script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Amaterasu (Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //a.ma.te.ɾa.sɯ//.

The two characters 天 (ama, heaven) and 照 (terasu, to illuminate) compose a classic Japanese ateji or semantic transcription of the divine name. The form 天照大神 (Amaterasu-ōmikami) is the full cult title used in ritual and imperial sources.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /a.ma.te.ɾa.sɯ/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'AMATERASU' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Amaterasu is Tier 2 because the ASCII form amaterasu already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Mythology

The central myth of Amaterasu is the episode of the Heavenly Rock Cave. After Susanō rampaged through her rice fields and desecrated her weaving hall, Amaterasu withdrew into a cave and sealed it with a boulder, plunging the world into darkness. The eight hundred myriad kami gathered outside; the goddess Ame-no-Uzume performed an ecstatic dance on an overturned tub, exposing herself and making the assembled gods laugh. Curious, Amaterasu peeked out; Tajikarao seized her hand and pulled her into the light. A rope (shimenawa) was stretched before the cave to prevent her return. The myth establishes kagura (sacred dance), divination, and communal ritual as the means by which cosmic order is restored.

Birth from Izanagi's Purification (Kojiki / Nihon Shoki)

After escaping the underworld where his wife Izanami had died, Izanagi performed ritual purification (misogi) in a river. As he washed his left eye, Amaterasu was born; from his right eye came Tsukuyomi, the moon kami; and from his nose came Susanoo, the storm kami. Amaterasu was given rule over the high plain of heaven, making her the senior sibling and source of celestial order.

The Cave of Heaven (Kojiki)

When Susanoo rampaged through heaven, destroying her rice-fields and terrifying her weaving-maiden, Amaterasu withdrew into the Ama-no-Iwato cave and sealed it with a great rock. The world fell into darkness. The eight hundred myriads of kami gathered and performed a raucous dance (kagura). The goddess Ame-no-Uzume danced on an overturned tub, exposing herself and making the assembly laugh. Curious, Amaterasu peeked out; a mirror was held before her, and the strong-handed Tajikarao pulled her into the light.

The Descent of Ninigi (Nihon Shoki)

Amaterasu sent her grandson Ninigi-no-mikoto to rule the terrestrial world, entrusting him with the Three Sacred Regalia: the curved jewel, the mirror, and the sword. Ninigi's great-grandson Jimmu became the first emperor of Japan, establishing the imperial house as descended from the sun.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Mirror (Yata no Kagami) — one of the three imperial regalia, said to have been used to lure her from the cave; it embodies the sun and the emperor's divine reflection.
  • Magatama curved jewel — another imperial regalia, associated with her mythic pact.
  • Sword (Kusanagi) — recovered from the tail of Yamata no Orochi by Susanō and presented to her, later passed to Ninigi.
  • Cock — the crowing bird that announces dawn and marks the successful lure from the cave.
  • Sakaki tree — the evergreen (Cleyera japonica) to which the shide streamers were fastened during the kagura dance.
  • Cave of Heaven (Ama-no-Iwato) — The rock-door of withdrawal and return, marking cosmic crisis and renewal
  • Sun-disc — The radiant disk she embodies, often rendered as a red sun
  • Magatama beads — Curved sacred jewels associated with her and imperial legitimacy

Archaeology & Evidence

The Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingū) in Mie Prefecture is the principal material cult site of Amaterasu. Rebuilt on adjacent plots every twenty years since at least the seventh century CE, the shrine preserves the shinmei-zukuri architectural style, thought to reproduce ancient raised-granary forms. The Shikinen Sengū cycle of 2013 was the sixty-second recorded rebuilding. Archaeological work in the Ise region has clarified the pre-shrine landscape and the historical development of the saikū, the imperial princesses' residence that administered the shrine.

Realm & Domain

Amaterasu is the sun itself made kami. Her realm is Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven, from which she bestows light and fertility upon the world. As ancestor of the imperial line, she also guarantees political legitimacy: the emperor rules as her direct descendant. Her withdrawal into the Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato) is the primal Shinto myth of cosmic crisis and restoration.

Sun Sovereign

Ruler of Takamagahara and source of light, warmth, and agricultural fertility.

Withdrawal and Return

Across Cultures

Amaterasu was historically identified with Buddhist solar deities, most notably Dainichi Nyorai (Mahāvairocana), in the shinbutsu-shūgō system of medieval Japan. At Ise, however, the outer shrine (Gekū) dedicated to Toyouke and the inner shrine (Naikū) dedicated to Amaterasu maintained a Shinto-centred cult that resisted complete Buddhist assimilation. The Meiji separation of kami and buddhas (shinbutsu bunri) restored her purely Shinto identity. In the modern period she remains the symbol of the Japanese state in imperial ritual and the national emblem of the rising sun.

Cultural Legacy

Amaterasu is the namesake of Japan itself — Nihon or Nippon, "origin of the sun." The Ise Grand Shrine, rebuilt every twenty years in the shikinen-zōtai ceremony, is her principal cult centre and one of the most important religious sites in Japan. The imperial regalia (sanshu no jingi) descended from her form the legitimating treasures of the Chrysanthemum Throne. She appears constantly in anime, manga, video games, and contemporary fiction as the archetype of the benevolent solar deity.
