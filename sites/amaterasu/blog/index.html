Overview

Amaterasu (amaterasu) — Sun Goddess, Shintō · Sun ​goddess in Shinto — belongs to the Japanese tradition, where it is catalogued under the domain "Sun Goddess, Shintō". The name means "Sun goddess in Shinto".

Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /a.ma.te.ɾa.sɯ/ — Modern Standard Japanese (Hepburn).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • ‌Ama- — Open front [a], labiodental [m], open central [a]; from 天 'heaven/sky'
  • -tera- — Alveolar tap [ɾ] between close-mid [e] and open central [a]; from 照 'to shine'
  • -su — Voiceless ​alveolar fricative [s] plus close back unrounded [ɯ]; the final vowel is soft

For the modern speaker, the closest approximation is: 'ah-mah-TEH-rah-soo' — the 'r' is a light tap, and the final ‌'u' is barely voiced.

Kindred and historical forms of the name:

  • Japanese — 天照 (Amaterasu), short for Amaterasu-ōmikami, 'Heaven-Illuminating Great August Deity' — 天 is the very character for sky
  • Siblings — Tsukuyomi ​(moon) and Susanoo (storms), born with her in Izanagi's misogi
  • Lineage — Grandmother of Ninigi; divine ancestress of the imperial line through Jimmu

Amaterasu is a plain restoration here: the ASCII form ​is identical to Hepburn romanization. Her authority is mythological and political, not phonetically distinctive.

Mythology

Amaterasu's mythology, told in the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, is a theology of light, legitimacy, and the ​restoration of order after violence. It moves from her birth in purification to her withdrawal from a wounded heaven and her dispatch of the imperial line to earth.

From the Left Eye ​of Izanagi (The Birth)

When Izanami died giving birth to the fire god and Izanagi failed to bring her back from Yomi, the grieving creator purified himself in a river. As he washed ​his face, three sovereign deities were born: Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose (Kojiki 1). Delighted, Izanagi removed his own necklace of magatama ‌and gave it to her, charging her with rule of Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven. Her full style, Amaterasu-ōmikami — the Heaven-Illuminating Great August Deity — is written with the very ​character for sky. The Nihon Shoki preserves several variant birth accounts, a reminder that these texts were compiled at court rather than received as fixed scripture. The myth makes the sun the eldest ‌child of purification: light is what emerges once pollution has been washed away.

Susanoo's Rampage in Heaven (The Outrage)

Susanoo wept so violently at the death of his mother that mountains shook ​and rivers swelled; when Izanagi expelled him from the land, the storm god climbed to heaven to bid his sister farewell. Wary, Amaterasu went out armed, but Susanoo protested his innocence and ‌proposed an ukei, a test by which the siblings exchanged ornaments and produced divine children (Kojiki 1). No sooner was the pledge sealed than he reversed the ridges of her rice-fields, filled the ​ditches, and flayed the heavenly piebald horse, hurling its carcass through the roof of her weaving hall. The shock killed her weaving-maiden. Enraged and grieving, Amaterasu withdrew into the rock cave and ‌sealed the door. The myth insists that the gravest danger to cosmic order is not foreign invasion but violence inside the family — the sun itself can be driven out by her ​own brother.

The Rock Cave of Heaven (The Withdrawal)

With Amaterasu hidden, the world went dark and the eight hundred myriad deities gathered on the bed of the tranquil heavenly river to devise ‌a remedy. The goddess Ame-no-Uzume overturned a tub, stamped upon it, and danced so bawdily that the assembled gods roared with laughter (Kojiki 1). Curious, Amaterasu opened the rock-door a crack: why ​did they rejoice in darkness? Uzume answered that they had found a deity more splendid than her — and held up the yata mirror. As the sun leaned out to look, the ​strong-handed Ame-no-Tajikarao, concealed behind the door, seized her wrist and drew her into the light; Ame-no-Futodama stretched the first shimenawa across the cave mouth so she could never withdraw again. The myth ‌is a charter for communal restoration: light returns not through force but through laughter, cunning, and a mirror that lets the goddess see her own radiance.

Ninigi and the Three Regalia (The Descent)

‌To bring order to the unruly land below, Amaterasu resolved to send her grandson Ninigi-no-mikoto to rule the earth (Nihon Shoki 2). The embassy had not been easy: her first envoy lingered ‌in heaven, the second was charmed by the earthly god Ōkuninushi, and a third chose marriage over mission. At last she entrusted Ninigi with the Three Sacred Regalia — the curved magatama ​jewel, the yata mirror, and the sword Kusanagi — instructing him to revere the mirror as if it were herself. Ninigi descended to Mount Takachiho, wed Konohanasakuya-hime, and from their line sprang Jimmu, ​first emperor of Japan. The myth is transparently political — a charter in which the ruling house descends from the sun — yet its mechanism is veneration: heaven does not rule the ‌earth directly; it sends a sign the earth must honor.

The Mirror Goes to Ise (The Journey)

Though born in heaven, Amaterasu did not remain in the palace. The Nihon Shoki (reign ​of Suinin) tells how the goddess longed for the land of Yamato, 'the land of fair rice-ears of the fertile reed-plain,' and commanded that a sanctuary be built for her there. In the ​Kojiki's account she had first taken up residence inside the imperial palace itself, troubling the emperor's house, until she declared her wish to leave. The emperor's daughter, the princess Yamato-hime, wandered the ‌provinces carrying the divine mirror, divining for a dwelling-place, until she received the oracle on the banks of the Isuzu river. There rose the Ise Grand Shrine, where the mirror is enshrined ​to this day and the sanctuary is renewed every twenty years. The myth moves the sun out of the palace and into a perpetual sanctuary: sovereignty becomes devotion, and devotion requires a building ​that never ages.

Symbols & Iconography

The iconography associated with Amaterasu concentrates in a small set of recurring attributes, each a compressed statement about the name:

  • Mirror (yata no kagami) — One ‌of the Three Imperial Regalia; the mirror hung outside the cave showed Amaterasu her own light, and its dwelling at Ise is her own
  • Cave of Heaven (Ama-no-Iwato) — The rock-door of ​her withdrawal; in ritual memory the sealed cave marks every crisis in which order must be coaxed back
  • Sun-disc — The radiant disk she embodies, echoed today in the red circle of ‌the Japanese flag
  • Magatama beads — The curved jewels Izanagi gave her; her own necklace generated the gods of the ukei rite with Susanoo
  • Shimenawa rope — The sacred rope Ame-no-Futodama stretched across ​the cave mouth — the first boundary between retreat and the common world

Archaeology & Evidence

The Ise Grand Shrine (Mie Prefecture) is Amaterasu's principal dwelling: its Inner Shrine (Kōtai Jingū), traditionally ​founded in the reign of Suinin (1st century BCE), is rebuilt every twenty years (shikinen-zōtai) on alternating adjacent plots, a rite documented from 690 CE and continued without break. Architectural and documentary ​study of the precincts — the Uji Bridge, the Shōden, the treasure halls — makes Ise the best-documented ritual complex in Japan, though the mirror itself is never displayed. Heian ritual compendia (Engishiki) ​and shrine records (Shintōshū) list the annual offerings of cloth and first fruits presented by the court.

‌Across Cultures

In the medieval honji-suijaku system Amaterasu was identified with the cosmic Buddha Vairocana (Dainichi Nyorai) — the sun of the Dharma itself — and Buddhist chapels and sutras stood within ​Ise's precincts until the Meiji separation edicts. State Shinto then stripped away these Buddhist layers, taught the descent of Ninigi as national history, and made her the theological center of imperial ideology; overseas ​shrines carried her cult into the empire. Her modern reception extends to the video game Ōkami, whose wolf-goddess is drawn from her iconography — evidence that the sun still needs a face.

Cultural Legacy

Ise Grand Shrine remains the supreme sanctuary of Shinto, its twenty-year rebuilding unbroken since the seventh century, and the ‌Daijōsai enthronement rite of each new emperor reenacts Ninigi's descent. The red disc of the flag keeps her sign on every public building, and pilgrims stream across the Uji Bridge at New Year. ‌In the countryside, folk religion still addresses her as the guarantor of the harvest sun. Abroad, Ōkami introduced her to millions who will never visit Ise — the newest link in an ​unbroken chain of sunlight made visible.

A Meditation

Amaterasu teaches that light is not automatic. Her withdrawal shows that even the sun can be wounded ​into silence, and her return shows what it takes to heal her: not argument, not force, but the laughter of Uzume and the honesty of a mirror. The community of gods succeeds ​precisely because each plays a part — the dancer, the strong-arm, the ritualist, the mirror-bearer.

To read the myth politically is correct but incomplete: it is also a psychology of withdrawal and return. ‌Everyone who has sealed themselves behind a rock door knows that the way back is not a plea but a celebration worth peeking at. And the mirror matters most of all: she ​returns not because others praise her, but because she is allowed to see her own radiance reflected.
