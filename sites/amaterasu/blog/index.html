Overview

Amaterasu (amaterasu) — Sun Goddess, ​Shintō — belongs to the Japanese tradition. The name means "Sun goddess in Shinto".

Amaterasu is the sun itself made kami — the great august kami who illuminates heaven and the ​ancestor of the imperial house. Her withdrawal into the Heavenly Rock Cave and her restoration by dance and persuasion form the central crisis of Shinto cosmology.

PuniCodex restores the name as Amaterasu. ​The plain ASCII form amaterasu is the modern Hepburn romanization; the restoration capitalizes the conventional spelling. Because the Japanese source script uses logographic characters whose distinctive visual form cannot be conveyed in ​the basic Latin alphabet, and because no diacritic or special letter is added in this restoration, the name is treated as a plain form. The Unicode form remains the philologically complete ‌reference, while the ASCII spelling is a practical convenience for the domain-name system.

The Name

The name is attested in Japanese characters as 天照. Etymologically it means "Sun goddess in Shinto".

The ​name is usually analyzed as ama-terasu, "shining in heaven," from ama (heaven) and terasu (to shine, to illuminate). The fuller cult name is Amaterasu-ōmikami, "the great august kami who illuminates heaven." ​She is the ancestral kami (sojin) of the imperial house and the central deity of the Shinto pantheon.

The ASCII form amaterasu survives only because the early domain-name system could not carry ‌Japanese characters or diacritics; it is a technological compromise, not an ancient spelling. The Unicode restoration Amaterasu gives the conventional Hepburn romanization with initial capitalization, the standard scholarly way to cite a ​proper name in romanized Japanese. The name is a simpler restoration because the restoration does not add a distinctive diacritic or special letter absent from the ASCII form.

The Original Script

The name is written in Japanese characters as 天照 — Japanese logographic and syllabic ‌script (kanji plus kana where attested), attested in the classical Japanese sources. The script is written top-to-bottom in traditional contexts and left-to-right in modern reproductions.

The scholarly transliteration is Amaterasu (Hepburn romanization ​with initial capitalization), giving the normalized reading //a.ma.te.ɾa.sɯ//.

The two characters 天 (ama, heaven) and 照 (terasu, to illuminate) compose a classic Japanese ateji or semantic transcription of the divine name. The ​form 天照大神 (Amaterasu-ōmikami) is the full cult title used in ritual and imperial sources.

Pronunciation

The conventional reading of the name is /a.ma.te.ɾa.sɯ/ — Modern Japanese (Standard Japanese).

For the modern ​speaker, the closest approximation is: 'AMATERASU' — pronounce each vowel clearly and avoid English diphthongs. Japanese is mora-timed, so each syllable receives roughly equal weight.

Amaterasu is a simpler restoration because the ​ASCII form amaterasu already represents the same phonemic sequence; no stress mark, length mark, or distinctive letter is required to recover the spoken name.

Japanese is a mora-timed language: each short ​syllable receives roughly equal temporal weight, and long vowels or double consonants count as separate morae. The name Amaterasu therefore falls into a regular rhythmic pattern that is lost if an English ‌speaker stresses one syllable heavily. The Hepburn romanization used here records the modern standard pronunciation; it does not preserve the historical pitch accent of the word, which differs across dialects. When reading ​the name aloud, keep the vowels pure and avoid English diphthongs: the restoration Amaterasu is the conventional scholarly citation form, not a phonetic transcription with added diacritics. Mythology

The central myth of Amaterasu is the episode of the Heavenly Rock Cave. After Susanō ​rampaged through her rice fields and desecrated her weaving hall, Amaterasu withdrew into a cave and sealed it with a boulder, plunging the world into darkness. The eight hundred myriad kami ‌gathered outside; the goddess Ame-no-Uzume performed an ecstatic dance on an overturned tub, exposing herself and making the assembled gods laugh. Curious, Amaterasu peeked out; Tajikarao seized her hand and pulled her ​into the light. A rope (shimenawa) was stretched before the cave to prevent her return. The myth establishes kagura (sacred dance), divination, and communal ritual as the means by which cosmic order ‌is restored.

The narrative of Amaterasu participates in the larger mythic program of the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki: to explain the origins of the imperial house, the division of divine labour, ​and the relationship between the human and kami worlds. Because these chronicles were compiled in the eighth century under imperial patronage, the myths also serve a political theology, grounding earthly authority in ‌divine descent and cosmic order. Read comparatively, the story of amaterasu illustrates how Japanese myth uses genealogy, descent from heaven, and the negotiation of rival domains to construct a coherent sacred ​history.

From the Left Eye of Izanagi (The Birth)

When Izanami died giving birth to the fire god and Izanagi failed to bring her back from Yomi, the grieving creator purified himself ​in a river. As he washed his face, three sovereign deities were born: Amaterasu from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right eye, and Susanoo from his nose (Kojiki 1). Delighted, Izanagi ‌removed his own necklace of magatama and gave it to her, charging her with rule of Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven. Her full style, Amaterasu-ōmikami — the Heaven-Illuminating Great August ‌Deity — is written with the very character for sky. The Nihon Shoki preserves several variant birth accounts, a reminder that these texts were compiled at court rather than received as fixed ‌scripture. The myth makes the sun the eldest child of purification: light is what emerges once pollution has been washed away.

Susanoo's Rampage in Heaven (The Outrage)

Susanoo wept so violently ​at the death of his mother that mountains shook and rivers swelled; when Izanagi expelled him from the land, the storm god climbed to heaven to bid his sister farewell. Wary, Amaterasu ​went out armed, but Susanoo protested his innocence and proposed an ukei, a test by which the siblings exchanged ornaments and produced divine children (Kojiki 1). No sooner was the pledge sealed ‌than he reversed the ridges of her rice-fields, filled the ditches, and flayed the heavenly piebald horse, hurling its carcass through the roof of her weaving hall. The shock killed her ​weaving-maiden. Enraged and grieving, Amaterasu withdrew into the rock cave and sealed the door. The myth insists that the gravest danger to cosmic order is not foreign invasion but violence inside the ​family — the sun itself can be driven out by her own brother.

The Rock Cave of Heaven (The Withdrawal)

With Amaterasu hidden, the world went dark and the eight hundred ‌myriad deities gathered on the bed of the tranquil heavenly river to devise a remedy. The goddess Ame-no-Uzume overturned a tub, stamped upon it, and danced so bawdily that the assembled gods ​roared with laughter (Kojiki 1). Curious, Amaterasu opened the rock-door a crack: why did they rejoice in darkness? Uzume answered that they had found a deity more splendid than her — and ​held up the yata mirror. As the sun leaned out to look, the strong-handed Ame-no-Tajikarao, concealed behind the door, seized her wrist and drew her into the light; Ame-no-Futodama stretched the ‌first shimenawa across the cave mouth so she could never withdraw again. The myth is a charter for communal restoration: light returns not through force but through laughter, cunning, and a mirror ​that lets the goddess see her own radiance.

Symbols & Iconography

  • Mirror (Yata no Kagami) — one of the three imperial regalia, said to have been used to lure her from ‌the cave; it embodies the sun and the emperor's divine reflection.
  • Magatama curved jewel — another imperial regalia, associated with her mythic pact.
  • Sword (Kusanagi) — recovered from the tail of Yamata ​no Orochi by Susanō and presented to her, later passed to Ninigi.
  • Cock — the crowing bird that announces dawn and marks the successful lure from the cave.
  • Sakaki tree — the ​evergreen (Cleyera japonica) to which the shide streamers were fastened during the kagura dance.
  • Cave of Heaven (Ama-no-Iwato) — The rock-door of her withdrawal; in ritual memory the sealed cave marks ​every crisis in which order must be coaxed back
  • Sun-disc — The radiant disk she embodies, echoed today in the red circle of the Japanese flag
  • Magatama beads — The curved jewels ​Izanagi gave her; her own necklace generated the gods of the ukei rite with Susanoo
  • Shimenawa rope — The sacred rope Ame-no-Futodama stretched across the cave mouth — the first boundary ‌between retreat and the common world

Archaeology & Evidence

The Ise Grand Shrine (Ise Jingū) in Mie Prefecture is the principal material cult site of Amaterasu. Rebuilt on adjacent plots every twenty ‌years since at least the seventh century CE, the shrine preserves the shinmei-zukuri architectural style, thought to reproduce ancient raised-granary forms. The Shikinen Sengū cycle of 2013 was the sixty-second recorded rebuilding. ​Archaeological work in the Ise region has clarified the pre-shrine landscape and the historical development of the saikū, the imperial princesses' residence that administered the shrine.

Realm & Domain

Amaterasu is ‌the sun itself made kami. Her realm is Takamagahara, the High Plain of Heaven, from which she bestows light and fertility upon the world. As ancestor of the imperial line, she also ​guarantees political legitimacy: the emperor rules as her direct descendant. Her withdrawal into the Heavenly Rock Cave (Ama-no-Iwato) is the primal Shinto myth of cosmic crisis and restoration.

The domain of ​Amaterasu is understood within the broader Japanese religious landscape as both a specific functional sphere and a node in a network of kami, buddhas, and local spirits. Shrines, temples, and household rites ‌all invoke the figure in ways that blur strict boundaries between Shinto and Buddhist practice. The restoration Amaterasu therefore names not only a mythic person but a living field of ritual activity: ‌pilgrimage, festival, iconography, and prayer continue to define what the name means in contemporary Japan. This combinatory or "syncretic" context is essential for interpreting the domains listed above.

Across Cultures

​Amaterasu was historically identified with Buddhist solar deities, most notably Dainichi Nyorai (Mahāvairocana), in the shinbutsu-shūgō system of medieval Japan. At Ise, however, the outer shrine (Gekū) dedicated to Toyouke and the ‌inner shrine (Naikū) dedicated to Amaterasu maintained a Shinto-centred cult that resisted complete Buddhist assimilation. The Meiji separation of kami and buddhas (shinbutsu bunri) restored her purely Shinto identity. In the modern ​period she remains the symbol of the Japanese state in imperial ritual and the national emblem of the rising sun.

Cultural Legacy

Amaterasu is the namesake of Japan itself — Nihon ​or Nippon, "origin of the sun." The Ise Grand Shrine, rebuilt every twenty years in the shikinen-zōtai ceremony, is her principal cult centre and one of the most important religious sites in ​Japan. The imperial regalia (sanshu no jingi) descended from her form the legitimating treasures of the Chrysanthemum Throne. She appears constantly in anime, manga, video games, and contemporary fiction as the ‌archetype of the benevolent solar deity.
