Pronunciation

The reconstructed pronunciation of the name is /ˈdiːti/ — Sanskrit (IAST: Diti).

Phoneme by phoneme:

  • -ti — The feminine abstract ending; the name means “the bound one”.
  • ** — The sister of Aditi — boundless — and mother of the anti-gods.

The name is a mirror of her sister’s: where Aditi is “un-bound”, Diti is the “bound” — and her sons are the gods’ enemies.

Mythology

Diti’s mythology is her maternity: everything ​the Daityas do, she enables — and one thing she undoes, in the Maruts episode, where Indra’s violence against her pregnant body produces the storm-gods from the womb’s fragments. She is the anti-mother ‌of the anti-gods, the bound one whose children strain against every bond except hers.

The Maruts from the Broken Womb (Ṛgveda 1.99)

The oldest Diti-story has Indra hurl the vajra into her ​womb, shattering a hundred sons in embryo; the fragments become the Maruts, the storm-gods who yet serve Indra. Diti’s grief and the storm-gods’ loyalty are the hymn’s tension: the anti-mother’s broken children, ‌adopted by her enemy.

The Boon of Hiraṇyakaśipu (Purāṇic Cycle)

Her son Hiraṇyakaśipu, devastated by his brother Hiraṇyākṣa’s death at Varāha’s tusk, won from Brahmā the boon that he could not die by ​day or night, indoors or out, by man or beast, on earth or sky. The boon made him lord of the three worlds — until Narasiṃha, the man-lion at dusk on a ‌threshold, ended him. Diti’s line is the engine of the avatāra cycle.

The Grandson Who Worshipped the Enemy (Prahlāda Cycle)

Prahlāda, son of Hiraṇyakaśipu and thus Diti’s grandson, loved Vishnu from the ​womb — the devotee born in the anti-god’s house. The Purāṇas make his devotion the theology of bhakti itself: the bound dynasty’s finest fruit was the boy who could not be burned, poisoned, ‌or thrown, for his god held him.

Daughter of Dakṣa, Wife of Kaśyapa (Genealogical Frame)

The Purāṇic theogonies make Diti one of Dakṣa’s thirteen daughters given to Kaśyapa: Aditi mothers the Ādityas, ​Diti the Daityas, Danu the Dānavas, and the rest of creation’s factions follow the wives. The marriage-bed of Kaśyapa is, in the genealogists’ scheme, the factory of the cosmos’s parties.

Across Cultures

Diti is the shadow-twin of Aditi — the Purāṇic genealogists built the whole deva-asura divide from the two sisters’ names. Her line’s fate — Hiraṇyakaśipu slain, Prahlāda ​saved — was read by the bhakti commentators as the triumph of devotion over lineage; and modern comparative study pairs her with the bound Titans of Greek genealogy.

Within the Sanskrit tradition, ‌closely related names in the corpus include Aditi, Agni, Ambikā, Aṃśa, Arjuna, and Bhakti.

Cultural Legacy

The Prahlāda story, carried by her line, is the subcontinent’s most retold tale of devotional defiance ​— every schoolchild’s Sanskrit reader has it. The Daitya-vaṃśa genealogies structured the Purāṇas’ entire mythology of conflict; and the name Diti survives in modern India as a girl’s name, the bound one given ​with a prayer for release.
