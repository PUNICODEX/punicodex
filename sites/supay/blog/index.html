Lord of Uku Pacha (The Inner World)

The earliest European witness is the corregidor Polo de Ondegardo, ​whose 1571 report De los errores y supersticiones de los indios explains Inca cosmology as three stacked worlds: hanaq pacha, the upper world; kay pacha, this present world; and uku pacha, the ​inner world within the earth. The dead descended to uku pacha, Polo reports, and there remained in the power of the being they called Supay. Bernabé Cobo repeats the scheme a century later ‌(Historia del Nuevo Mundo 13.10), adding that the dead were furnished with food, tools, and treasure for the journey down. Nowhere in these accounts is Supay a god of a temple or a ​priesthood: he is the one who holds the dead, and the offerings made at funerals are essentially payments that keep him from reaching further into the world of the living. The Inca underworld ‌was not a place of punishment but of retention, and Supay was its jailer as much as its king. The belief encodes a sober arithmetic: every death adds to his household, so ​the living must forever feed the boundary between the worlds.

The Caves of Tambillos (Huarochirí)

The Quechua-language Huarochirí manuscript, recorded by the extirpator Francisco de Ávila in the first years of the seventeenth ‌century (trans. Salomon & Urioste, 1991), treats supay not as a single lord but as a class of dangerous beings — the supaykuna — who dwell in caves, springs, hills, and crossroads and ​send illness to those who offend them. The most famous haunt is the cavern of Tambillos, near San Pedro de Huarochirí, where the people made offerings of coca and maize beer so ‌that the spirits of the rock would not take their children. Scholars have long debated whether the chroniclers' single 'Supay, lord of the inner world' is a Spanish simplification of this older, plural ​spirit-world, or whether the supaykuna were always understood as the lord's subjects. The manuscript itself does not resolve the question; it simply shows communities negotiating, offering, and appeasing. Whatever the hierarchy, the logic ‌is constant: the earth's interior is populated, and it must be paid. Supay's power was never worshipped from a temple — it was managed at the cave mouth, one offering at a time.

​Supay Becomes the Devil (Conquest)

The most consequential chapter of Supay's history was written not by his believers but by his enemies. To convert the Andes, the missionaries needed Satan to have ​a local name, and they chose Supay's. González Holguín's Quechua Vocabulario (1608) glosses supay plainly as 'devil', and Pablo José de Arriaga's manual for destroying idolatry, the Extirpación de la idolatría (1621), names ‌Supay as the demon who still receives the offerings poured at the Indian shrines. The campaign worked as language policy: within a generation Quechua speakers used supay for the Christian devil, and the ‌pre-conquest meaning — 'spirit' — survived only in scholarly memory. Every colonial confession, sermon, and catechism pressed the word deeper into its new meaning. The transformation is a case study in conversion by ‌redefinition: the church did not first destroy the lord of the inner world; it renamed him, and let the new name do the slow work of displacement. That the word, not the ​being, was conquered is proven by the mines, where the lord returned under another alias.
