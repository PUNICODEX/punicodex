Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

The name Supay reaches the modern world through scholarly transliteration of the incan tradition. Because the conventional spelling uses only Latin letters, its Unicode and ASCII forms coincide — the tradition it carries is in the name itself, not in diacritics.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Quechua — supay, 'spirit, demon' — the common noun González Holguín glosses as 'diablo' in his 1608 Vocabulario de la lengua general de todo el Perú
  • Aymara — supaya, the parallel Aymara word for a spirit of the dead or demon (Bertonio, Vocabulario de la lengua aymará, 1612)
  • Colonial equation — Spanish chroniclers equated Supay with the Christian Devil; Polo de Ondegardo (1571) reports the Inca dead descending to uku pacha in the power of the being they called Supay

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Supay in Later Traditions

Supay's entire afterlife is a history of forced translations. The conquering church mapped him onto Satan, and the mapping was so successful that modern Quechua and Aymara use supay as the ordinary word for devil. Yet the older figure survived the translation in the mines, where El Tío — the Uncle — continues the uku pacha lord's contract with those who take wealth from below. Scholars such as Taussig (1980) and Tristan Platt have read this double life as the emblem of colonial capitalism: the indigenous underworld lord re-employed as the owner of the silver that fed Europe. The Diablada dance traditions of Oruro and the miner rites at Potosí keep both identities visible at once — Christian devil masks performing unmistakably pre-Christian obligations.

Modern Legacy

Supay remains embedded in Andean language itself: to this day supay means devil in Quechua and Aymara, a living fossil of the extirpation campaigns. In the mining regions of Bolivia the Tío effigies are still fed and still feared, and the Diablada festivals stage his masked dance before crowds of thousands. For scholarship, Supay is a key witness to how colonialism rewrote indigenous cosmology — studied by Taussig, Platt, and Duviols as the paradigmatic case of a deity demonized, renamed, and secretly retained. The name also circulates in global culture: Andean diaspora communities, museum exhibitions on Inca religion, and contemporary indigenous revival movements all return to the older, pre-Christian meaning of the word — spirit, not devil — as part of recovering what the extirpations tried to erase.

In the Earth

No temple of Supay is known — he was propitiated at cave mouths, springs, graves, and mine faces rather than in built sanctuaries. The material record is therefore indirect: the rock shelters and cave shrines of the Huarochirí region, including Tambillos, where colonial extirpation records describe offerings of coca and maize beer; colonial-era dictionaries and confessionals that fixed supay as 'devil' (González Holguín 1608; Bertonio 1612); the Tío effigies and their offerings still maintained at Potosí and Olmillos; and the abundant documentary archive of the extirpation campaigns (Arriaga 1621; Duviols). The evidence honestly shows a cult of place rather than of monuments.

The Meditation

What Supay means — beyond the dictionary

Supay is the lord of the underground who was renamed but never dethroned. Colonial priests thought that calling him 'devil' would finish him; instead the name became a mask, and behind the mask the old contract continued in the dark of the mines. There is a lesson in the economics of belief here: a power that controls what you need — passage for your dead, safety for your labor, metal for your markets — cannot be argued away. It can only be paid.

The restoration of Supay is therefore more than philological courtesy. It returns to the figure his own name, before the extirpators rewrote it, and it asks the reader to hold both histories at once: the lord of uku pacha and the colonial devil, the cave offerings and the Tuesday cigarettes at Cerro Rico. The earth, this name insists, keeps its own accounts, and its oldest creditor is still collecting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Supay, Underworld, Death, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Supay?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Supay is /supay/ — approximately SOO-pie — two syllables, stress on the first; the '-ay' is the 'y' of 'fly'.

02What does Supay mean?

Supay means The spirit in the incan tradition.

03What are the symbols of Supay?

Supay is associated with Cave mouth or mine shaft (The visible entrance to uku pacha; every shaft sunk into the earth is a doorway into Supay's jurisdiction), Coca and chicha (The standard offerings that made the underground lord generous toward the living who disturbed his realm), Funerary goods (Food, tools, and treasure interred with the dead as provision for the journey down to Supay's household), The horned devil figure (The post-conquest iconography in which the extirpation campaigns re-imagined Supay as the Christian Devil).

04Why restore Supay in Unicode?

Supay is written entirely in ASCII letters, so its restoration is about canonical fidelity rather than recovered diacritics: the temple fixes the name in its scholarly standard spelling, attested in the source tradition, instead of leaving it to drift.

05What is the most important myth about Supay?

“Lord of Uku Pacha” — No Inca myth cycle of Supay survives on its own terms. Everything we know comes through colonial chroniclers, Quechua-language manuscripts, and the living memory of Andean communities — the record of a lord of the inner world who was propitiated, feared, demonized, and finally renamed, yet never quite dethroned. The full telling lives on the lore page.
