Scholarly Notes and the Fluid Geography of the Sacred

One of the most interesting features of Mamaqucha is the slipperiness of her domain. The same name can refer to the Pacific Ocean, to Lake Titicaca, or to any large body of water that functions as a maternal source. This fluidity challenges modern distinctions between sea, lake, river, and groundwater. In the Andean view, water is a continuous sacred substance that moves between these forms, and Mamaqucha names its personified power regardless of container.

This has made Mamaqucha a useful figure for scholars of environmental humanities. She offers a way of thinking about water that is not reducible to property, infrastructure, or commodity. Coastal fishing communities, highland lake communities, and urban migrants all relate to water through concepts that echo the old sea goddess, even when they no longer use her name. The restoration of Mamaqucha is therefore not only a philological act but an ecological one: it brings a water-centered theology back into public language.

The PuniCodex restoration underscores this by giving the name a stable, searchable presence. In a world of climate crisis and water conflict, the figure who personifies the sea as mother deserves to be spelled correctly. Mamaqucha is not a footnote to the Incan pantheon; she is a reminder that the most important resources are also relatives.

The restored name Mamaqucha invites us to think of water as kin, and of the sea not as a boundary but as a mother whose memory survives in language. The tides that once carried fishermen now carry data, and Mamaqucha sails both waters under her restored name.
