Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Original Script क्षितिगर्भ Kṣitigarbha — "The Earth-Womb — bodhisattva of the great vow to empty the hells (kṣiti 'earth' + garbha 'womb')"
Unicode Restoration Kṣitigarbha Restored stress, length, and script
Modern ASCII ksitigarbha Plain-ASCII fallback

Kṣitigarbha is kṣiti, "earth" (from √kṣi, "to dwell, abide" — Monier-Williams), + garbha, "womb, inner chamber, treasury": "the Earth-Womb," the earth as the storehouse that holds and hides all treasure. East Asia translated rather than transliterated: Chinese Dìzàng, "Earth-Store," Japanese Jizō. The restoration's whole distinctiveness is carried by one letter — the retroflex ṣ inside the kṣ conjunct — which the ASCII "Ksitigarbha" writes but cannot show. Tier-1 by rule.

04

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern Unicode

Ancient Domain

Kṣitigarbha is the bodhisattva of the greatest vow ever sworn: not to attain Buddhahood until the hells are emptied — the monk-robed guardian of the dead, the traveler, and the child, who accepted the loneliest commission in Buddhism: to fill the age between one Buddha's passing and the next one's coming.

Kṣitigarbha in Later Traditions

East Asia rebuilt him in its own image without touching the vow: China seated him over the Ten Kings of the underworld bureaucracy and wove him into the Ghost Festival's calendar; Korea's Jijang shares the Jiuhua inheritance; Japan fused Jizō with the roadside protector-deities of the old highways and the cult of dead children. Tibet received him as Sa yi snying po among the eight great bodhisattvas, though without the underworld portfolio. Comparatists place him among the great psychopomps — but note the difference: he does not guide the dead out; he goes in and stays until the last one leaves.

Modern Legacy

Kṣitigarbha is the working religion of grief in East Asia: the mizuko kuyō services in which Japanese parents memorialize lost pregnancies are held before him; the bibbed stone Jizō is Japan's commonest roadside sight; and Dìzàng's temples anchor the Chinese underworld's moral map. His vow has become the Mahāyāna's most quoted sentence — the standard against which every other pledge of compassion is measured — and his monk's robe the proof that the highest bodhisattva need not look like a king.

Unicode Restoration as Cultural Act

Restoring Kṣitigarbha in a domain name is more than orthographic accuracy. It is a statement that the internet should recognize the full range of human writing — not only the ASCII keyboard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Kṣitigarbha, Earth Womb, Savior of Hell Beings, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Kṣitigarbha?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Kṣitigarbha is /kʂi.ti.ˈgɐr.bʱɐ/ — approximately kshih-tih-GUR-bhuh — the kṣ is one conjunct sound; the bh is aspirated..

02What does Kṣitigarbha mean?

Kṣitigarbha means The Earth-Womb — bodhisattva of the great vow to empty the hells (kṣiti 'earth' + garbha 'womb') in the buddhist tradition.

03What are the symbols of Kṣitigarbha?

Kṣitigarbha is associated with The monk's robe and shaven head (The only great bodhisattva who refuses princely ornament: humility as the uniform of the vow), The staff of six rings (The sound of mercy approaching: the rings ring so beings may step aside, and hell's doors not stay shut), The red bib (The cloth mothers tie on roadside Jizō: the grief and gratitude of the living made visible).

04Why restore Kṣitigarbha in Unicode?

Plain ASCII ksitigarbha strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Kṣitigarbha?

The Sūtra of the Past Vows of Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva is set in the heaven of the Thirty-Three, where the Buddha preaches for his mother's sake before his passing. There he recounts Kṣitigarbha's long record of vows and formally entrusts to him all beings of the coming age — the interval between Śākyamuni's parinirvāṇa and Maitreya's arrival. The sūtra's most quoted line is the vow itself: until the hells are empty, he shall not become a Buddha; only when every being is saved will he accept awakening. The Sanskrit original is lost; the text survives chiefly in Chinese and Tibetan, and its promise survived everything.

Archaeology & Art History

  • Material evidence — iconography, inscriptions, and temple archaeology — for Kṣitigarbha and related cults.
  • His surviving landscape is vast: Jiuhuashan's temple-mountain, with the flesh-body hall of the Silla prince, remains an active pilgrimage city; the Dunhuang caves preserve banner-paintings of Dìzàng enthroned among the Ten Kings of hell, the earliest great cycle of his iconography. Japan's material record is the stone Jizō itself — uncounted thousands of carved figures at village edges, crossroads, and temple yards, from medieval stelae to today's bibbed statues — the densest distribution of any Buddhist image in the archipelago.
