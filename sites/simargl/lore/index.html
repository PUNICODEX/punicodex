01

Mythology

Stories of Simargl

Simargl has no mythology in the ordinary sense — no tale was ever told about him. He exists as a single line in a chronicle, a name of Iranian ancestry, and a century of scholarly argument about what a winged guardian was doing in a Slavic pantheon.

Kyiv, 988

The Idols in the Dnieper

In 988 the Primary Chronicle's procession of drowned gods includes, between Stribog and Mokosh, a name no Slavic tongue built: Simargl. The chronicler lists him without curiosity, one idol among six, but modern scholarship has never been able to leave him alone — because his name betrays a long journey, from the Iranian Simurgh, through the speech of steppe peoples who traded and raided along the Black Sea, into the capital of Christianizing Rus'. A pantheon is a nation's self-portrait; Kyiv's included a god borrowed from Persia's. The chronicle line is the whole dossier: no oath, no gloss, no sorcerer, no saint. One drowning, recorded by the enemies who arranged it — and a foreign accent still audible in the name as it went under. No later source ever asked who had carried him there; only modern scholarship heard the accent at all.

Iranian Origin

A Name from Persia

The Simurgh of Iranian tradition is one of the oldest great birds of world mythology. In the Avesta she roosts in the branches of the sacred tree; across Sogdian and Sasanian art she spreads composite wings over the Tree of Life; and in Ferdowsi's Shahnameh she rears the hero Zal and guides his son Rostam through every impossible task — part dog, part lion, part bird, and wholly wise. How her name crossed into Slavic as Simargl is the ordinary work of steppe history: Alanic- and Sarmatian-speaking peoples of the north Pontic steppe served as carriers, and a worn-down Simurgh entered East Slavic speech generations before a chronicler wrote it down. The borrowing tells its own myth: a god can be an import, and a pantheon a port — Kyiv's sky had been in conversation with Persia's for centuries before either had a church.

The Rybakov Hypothesis

Guardian of Seeds?

Boris Rybakov, the most influential historian of Slavic religion, gave Simargl his modern job description: guardian of seeds and plant life. The argument runs on two legs — the Simurgh's Iranian role as protector of the Tree of Life and of vegetation, and the presence in Slavic folklore of the Sey Div, a chthonic being who guards sown fields and must be placated at sowing and harvest. If Simargl is a worn Simurgh, and the Sey Div is his folk memory in reverse, then the god who drowned in the Dnieper was the protector of every furrow in Rus'. The hypothesis is ingenious and untestable: no text calls Simargl a seed-guardian, and the Sey Div connection rests on names and functions, never on an attestation. It is cited here exactly as Rybakov framed it — a structure built from parallels, honest about its own materials, and better than any rival precisely because it admits what it is.

Honest Silence

The Sphinx of the Pantheon

Like Stribog, Simargl must be described by what is missing. No treaty swears by him; the Malalas gloss that rescued Dažbog passes him by; no saint inherits his day; South Slavic folklore preserves no Dajbog-like fossil of his name; and no archaeological find — not the Zbruch idol, not the axe-amulets, not the Peryn' enclosure — can be tied to him with any confidence. What remains is structural: a pantheon needs a vegetation-guardian, the name is Iranian, and the Iranian original is the greatest plant-guardian of the Near Eastern myth-world. Modern scholarship has filled the gap with a functional god; modern paganism has filled it with a feathered angel of gardens. Both are guesses at a shape the sources never drew. Simargl is the Kyiv pantheon's sphinx: a riddle posed in a foreign accent, guarding a seed no one has ever seen.

02

Simargl

Winged Guardian

Simargl is the strangest god in the Kyiv pantheon: a name borrowed from the Iranian Simurgh, worn smooth by Slavic tongues, standing sixth among Vladimir's idols in 988 with no story, no oath, and no gloss to say what he did. Scholars have made him a guardian of seeds and plant life by inference; the sources themselves are silent.

The Idols

One of the six gods of Kyiv drowned in the Dnieper in 988, listed without comment by the Primary Chronicle.

Iranian Wings

His name is the Simurgh of Persian epic — a loan carried north by steppe contact, like amber carried by rivers.

Guardian of Seeds?

Rybakov's influential hypothesis made him protector of sown fields — a reconstruction, not an attestation.

The Silent One

No oath, gloss, festival, or folk name survives — the least documented god of the six.

Sacred Symbols

Wings From the Simurgh lineage: the composite winged guardian of Iranian iconography, adapted to Slavic soil
Grain and seed Under Rybakov's hypothesis, the sprouting seed he guards against the chthonic adversary
Lion's paws, dog's head The Simurgh's composite body — part dog, part lion, part bird — carried inside the loanword's meaning
Fire on the hill The 980 sacrifices on Kyiv's hill included him; whatever received the offering, flame was its medium

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Scholarly Transliteration

Simargl

The name survives in scholarly transliteration. Simargl is the standard Slavic romanisation, documented in academic sources — “From Simurgh (Iranian)”. Because the spelling uses only Latin letters, the form is the same in both ASCII and Unicode.

No indigenous writing system is securely attested for individual slavic names. The form shown is a modern scholarly transliteration.

ASCII Constraint

simargl

The plain simargl form is identical to the Unicode restoration. Because this name is already written in Latin letters, no diacritics, stress, or script information were lost — only capitalization differs.

Unicode Restoration

Simargl

The Unicode restoration does not need to recover lost marks for Simargl. Its value is canonical spelling and consistent cataloguing, not the reconstruction of erased orthography. The domain is readable as-is to both DNS and humanity.

Extended Lore

Simargl is a parable about what survives. Nothing of him survives — no story, no prayer, no image — except a name, and the name is a survivor twice over: the Simurgh outlived Zoroastrian empires to be sung by Ferdowsi, and then outlived the journey north to be drowned, once, in a river, by a chronicle that did not understand what it was erasing. We know him only as a translation — a word carried across languages and centuries until it meant less and stood for more. Moderns have given him back his wings, his seeds, his gardens; perhaps that is what translation always is, the endless human work of deciding what a worn-down name should guard. The Simurgh reared heroes; Simargl guards furrows; both are the same old impulse — to post a winged thing at the boundary between what we plant and what we fear. A god made entirely of borrowing teaches that no tradition is pure, and none the poorer for it.
