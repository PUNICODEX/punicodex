01

Mythology

Stories of Jizō

Jizō's mythology blends Indian scripture, Chinese translation, and Japanese folk belief. He is one of the most actively worshipped Buddhist figures in Japan — a bodhisattva whose statues outnumber his temples.

Sutra

The Original Vow

The Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva Sūtra recounts that in a previous life the bodhisattva was a Brahmin maiden whose mother had slandered the Dharma. After her mother's death, the daughter made vast offerings at a temple and was transported in vision to hell, where a guardian demon told her the offerings had not yet redeemed her mother. Moved by the suffering around her, she vowed before the Buddha to save all beings in the hell realms, life after life, until not a single one remained. The sūtra frames this as an intercessory chain — the Buddha entrusts the helpless, in the long night between one buddha's passing and the next's arrival, to Kṣitigarbha's keeping. The myth's engine is refusal: heaven has no vacancy so long as hell has a single prisoner, and the bodhisattva is content to wait.

Japanese lore

Sai no Kawara

On the banks of Sai no Kawara, the stony riverbed where dead children must labor, the little souls pile stones as penance for having caused their parents grief — and each night demons scatter their work. Jizō comes in the guise of a wandering monk, hides the children in the sleeves of his robe, and comforts them until their karma is spent. The red bibs and caps set on his statues are gifts intended to warm these child-spirits, since the dead feel the cold. The tale first flowers in the medieval picture scrolls and story collections, where the riverbed's naked little laborers are shown weeping over their scattered stones — and Jizō arrives with his staff ringing. It is the sentimental heart of Japanese Buddhism: hell is real, labor is endless, and yet someone keeps coming down to the riverbed, night after night, to hold the children until morning.

Folk tale

The Monk Who Was Jizō

Countless Japanese tales tell of a wandering monk who helps a traveller in distress — carrying the load, pointing the road, sharing the fire — only to vanish, leaving the traveller to realize that the monk was the roadside Jizō himself. In the most beloved version, a poor but kind farmer sweeps the snow from the statues each New Year and, having no offering, gives them his own straw hat; in the night the Jizō come in procession to thank him, and his house is blessed. The stories encode a popular theology: Jizō is not distant in a pure land but present on the road, in the rain, at the crossroads where ordinary people lose their way. In a religion of vast vows, the tales insist on small gifts — a hat, a bow, a swept stone.

Ritual

The Thirteen Gifts of the Memorial

Japanese esoteric Buddhism folded Kṣitigarbha into its map of the afterlife. In the rites of the Thirteen Buddhas, each of the memorial services after a death — the seventh day, the forty-ninth, the hundredth, the anniversaries — is entrusted to a different bodhisattva or buddha. Jizō's own watch falls on the thirty-fifth day, when the soul has crossed the first judgment and most needs a guide; the rites of his day kept his name in the villages, and the summer Jizō-bon observances with their lanterns still light the roads for the returning dead. The assignment is telling: the church gave the freshly dead, still close to the living, to the one bodhisattva who specializes in the helpless. Between the funeral and the first anniversary, salvation is less a doctrine than a relay, and Jizō runs the longest leg of it.

02

Earth Treasury Bodhisattva

Guardian of Children, Travelers, and the Dead

Jizō is the Bodhisattva Kṣitigarbha as known and loved in Japan: a gentle monk-like figure who refuses buddhahood until all beings, especially those in hell and the spirits of dead children, are saved. His statues stand by roadsides, cemeteries, and temples, clothed in red bibs and caps offered by grieving parents.

Guardian of Children

He protects children, including mizuko, the spirits of miscarried and aborted foetuses.

Savior of Hell-beings

He descends into the hell realms to rescue those tormented by their karma.

Patron of Travelers

Roadside Jizō guide and protect those on journeys, both living and dead.

Bodhisattva Vow

He postpones his own enlightenment until every hell is emptied.

Sacred Symbols

Staff (khakkhara) The ringing staff that warns small creatures underfoot and opens the gates of hell.
Wish-fulfilling jewel (cintāmaṇi) The gem that grants all needs and dispels the darkness of the six realms.
Red bib or cap Offerings by parents for the protection of their children in the afterlife — the folk garment of Sai no Kawara.
Lotus seat Purity within the muddy world of suffering.

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

地蔵

The name in its original Japanese form. Jizō (地蔵) is attested in the source tradition — “Earth treasury”. Its macron-length vowels carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

jizo

Reduced to plain jizo, the name loses everything that made it specific: macron-length vowels. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Jizō

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Jizō restores macron-length vowels, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Jizō.com → xn--jiz-sxa.com

The non-ASCII characters in Jizō are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Jizō.

Pronunciation

How Jizō was spoken

/dʑi.zoː/ Japanese / Sanskrit Reconstruction
J- Voiced palatal affricate [dʑ], like English 'j' but with the tongue closer to the hard palate.
-i- Short close front vowel [i].
-zō- Voiced alveolar fricative [z] plus long [oː], marked by the macron.

Modern Approximation

'JEE-zoh' — the first syllable is like 'jee', the second is a long, drawn 'zoh'.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Jizō is Tier 1: the macron on ō marks the long vowel of the Japanese reading. The full Sanskrit name would require retroflex and aspirated consonants not registrable in the DNS root zone.

Etymology

Sanskrit kṣiti 'earth' + garbha 'womb, embryo, interior'; rendered into Chinese as 地藏 'earth store' and borrowed into Japanese as 地蔵.

Meaning

Earth Treasury Bodhisattva; guardian of children, travelers, firefighters, and beings in hell.

From original to transliteration

  1. Sanskrit Kṣitigarbha ('Earth Treasury' or 'Earth Womb') was translated into Chinese as 地藏 (Dìzàng).
  2. Japanese borrowed the compound as 地蔵, read in Sino-Japanese as Jizō.
  3. Hepburn romanisation writes the long vowel with a macron: Jizō.
  4. The ASCII form jizo loses the length mark, collapsing the distinction between short and long ō.
  5. The Unicode restoration Jizō is registrable in .com; the kanji form is not supported in the .com IDN table.

Extended Lore

Jizō is the Bodhisattva of refusal: he refuses to leave until everyone is safe. That sounds impossible, and it is. But the vow is not a plan; it is a posture. It says that the last and least worthy soul matters as much as the first enlightened one.
