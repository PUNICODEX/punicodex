01

Mythology

Stories of Avalokiteśvara

Avalokiteśvara's mythology is a geography of mercy: the Indian lord who hears cries, the Chinese princess who gave her eyes and arms, the Tibetan ancestor of a nation, the Japanese savior of the drowning. His stories share one grammar — someone suffers, and compassion arrives already looking.

The Name

The Lord Who Looks Down

The name is an act of attention: ava-lokita, 'perceived, looked down upon,' joined to īśvara, 'lord' — the Lord Who Looks Down, as Monier-Williams parses the Sanskrit. The Lotus Sūtra's twenty-fifth chapter, the Universal Gateway, turns the etymology into a program: the Buddha declares that whoever calls Avalokiteśvara's name in the moment of terror — surrounded by fire, swept by flood, bound in chains, cornered by demons — will be saved, because this bodhisattva scans the world the way a parent scans a crowd for a child. Kumārajīva translated the name into Chinese as Guanshiyin, 'Perceiver of the World's Sounds,' and the translation stuck: East Asia knows him by the listening. The name is the whole doctrine — compassion is not a mood but a gaze that never lifts.

Lotus Sūtra

Thirty-Three Forms for Every Kind of Suffering

The Lotus Sūtra (ch. 25) closes its gateway chapter with a list that reads like a census of the human condition: to beings who can be saved through a Buddha's body, Avalokiteśvara appears as a Buddha; likewise as a pratyekabuddha, a śrāvaka, a king, a householder, a government official, a monk, a nun, a layman, a laywoman, a boy, a girl, a god, a nāga, a yaksha, a gandharva, an asura, a garuḍa, a kinnara, a mahoraga — the sūtra counts thirty-three bodies in all. Śāriputra asks why he is called Perceiver of Sounds, and the answer is simplicity itself: he perceives the world's sounds and responds to each. The list is the myth's meaning: suffering has no shape the bodhisattva will not borrow to reach the sufferer, and mercy wears every uniform the world issues.

Princess Miaoshan

The Eyes and Arms She Gave Away

China's most beloved Guanyin legend is also its latest — recorded in the Song-dynasty Xiangshan baojuan, the 'Precious Scroll of the Fragrant Mountain.' King Miao Zhuang's youngest daughter Miaoshan refused every suitor and every palace command, asking only to become a nun; exiled and persecuted, she held to her practice while her father's hatred hardened. When the king fell mortally ill, the diviners declared that only the eyes and arms of a person without resentment could cure him. A mountain ascetic — Miaoshan in disguise — gave them, and died; as her body was carried off, she was received into the Fragrant Mountain, revealed as Guanyin herself, with a thousand hands and an eye in every palm. The late legend gives the bodhisattva's iconography its human face: every hand is a sacrifice once made, and compassion is the scar tissue of self-giving.

Nīlakaṇṭha

The Blue-Throated One

Indian Buddhism borrowed even wrath from Śiva. In the seventh-century dhāraṇī sūtras — Xuanzang's translation of the Nīlakaṇṭha-dhāraṇī and the Sūtra of the Great Compassionate One of a Thousand Hands and Eyes — Avalokiteśvara takes the Destroyer's own epithet: Nīlakaṇṭha, 'blue-throated,' the one whose throat holds the poison of a churned cosmos without flinching. Here the gentle perceiver multiplies into a thousand-armed sovereign whose Great Compassion Dhāraṇī subdues demons and burns away the gravest karma, and whose wrathful faces stand at the gates of mandalas across Asia. The texts themselves show the borrowing plainly — the bodhisattva of mercy wears the destroyer's name and drinks his poison. The myth's claim is that compassion has a frightening side: it will enter any form, even terror's, to reach those only terror can move.

Tibetan Ancestor

The Monkey, the Demoness, and the First Tibetans

Tibet's own creation myth, recorded in the twelfth-century Mani bka' 'bum and the treasure-texts of the Nyingma school, makes Avalokiteśvara — there called Chenrezig — the father of the entire nation. Gazing from his mountain, the bodhisattva saw that the snowy plateau was empty of beings fit for the Dharma; rays of his compassion fell upon a monkey who had withdrawn into meditation in a mountain cave and upon the rock-demoness who haunted the cliffs. Their union, blessed by the bodhisattva's light, produced six children — red-faced, hairy, the ancestors of Tibet's clans — and when the infants had nothing to eat, Chenrezig scattered barley over the snows so they could live and, in time, hear the teaching. The myth means that for Tibetans compassion is not one virtue among many: it is the literal parent of the people, and every Tibetan carries Avalokiteśvara's gaze as a birthright.

02

Compassion

The Lord Who Looks Down in Compassion — Bodhisattva of Mercy, Lord of the World's Cries

Avalokiteśvara is compassion made a person: the bodhisattva who looks down upon every cry of suffering and answers it, postponing his own Buddhahood until no being is left unsaved. From the Lotus Sūtra's Universal Gateway to Tibet's patron deity and China's beloved Guanyin, he is the most widely venerated bodhisattva in the world.

The Universal Gateway

Lotus Sūtra ch. 25: whoever calls his name in fire, flood, chains, or the jaws of demons is delivered at once.

Thousand Arms, Thousand Eyes

His Great Compassion form: an arm for every cry, an eye in every palm so that seeing and saving happen together.

Perceiver of Sounds

ava-lokita, 'perceived, looked down upon': his very name is an act of attention that never lifts.

Om Mani Padme Hum

His mantra on the lips of half the Himalaya — the jewel in the lotus — recited for every suffering being.

Sacred Symbols

The lotus Purity rising unstained from the mud of samsara — the flower on which he stands above the world he refuses to leave
The vase of amrita The nectar of compassion in his left hand, poured out drop by drop on the burning places of beings
Thousand arms with an eye in each palm His vow rendered anatomically: a hand for every cry, an eye in every palm so that seeing and saving are one motion
Om Mani Padme Hum The six-syllable mantra — 'the jewel in the lotus' — recited across Tibet, Mongolia, and the Himalaya for a thousand years
The blue throat of Nīlakaṇṭha The wrathful form's throat stained blue from drinking the world's poison — compassion willing to take on even Śiva's darkest work

04

The Name

From the original script to the living URL

Original Script

अवलोकितेश्वर

The name in its original Buddhist form. Avalokiteśvara (अवलोकितेश्वर) is attested in the source tradition — “Lord who looks down in compassion”. Its palatal/retroflex sibilants carry the full phonetic and orthographic weight of the source tradition.

ASCII Constraint

avalokiteshvara

Reduced to plain avalokiteshvara, the name loses everything that made it specific: palatal/retroflex sibilants. What remains is an ASCII string that machines can parse but that no longer speaks with its original voice.

Unicode Restoration

Avalokiteśvara

The Unicode restoration recovers what ASCII flattened. Avalokiteśvara restores palatal/retroflex sibilants, returning the name to its original written dignity. The domain encodes to Punycode, but the browser displays the truth.

Punycode Encoding
Avalokiteśvara.com → xn--avalokitevara-4rc.com

The non-ASCII characters in Avalokiteśvara are encoded while the ASCII remains visible. To the DNS, it is Punycode. To humanity, it is Avalokiteśvara.

Pronunciation

How Avalokiteśvara was spoken

/ɐ.ʋɐ.loː.kɪ.teː.ɕʋɐ.ɾɐ/ Sanskrit Reconstruction
a-va- Short open [ɐ] with voiced labio-dental [ʋ]; the prefix ava-, 'down, upon'
-lō- Long open [oː] — lokita, 'seen, perceived, looked down upon'
-ki- Short palatal [kɪ]; the connecting vowel of the compound
-tē- Long front [eː] — the first of two long vowels the ASCII form flattens
-śva-ra Palatal [ɕ] like the 'sh' of 'ship,' short [ʋɐ], trilled [ɾɐ]; īśvara, 'lord'

Modern Approximation

'uh-vuh-LOH-kee-TAY-shvuh-ruh' — the ś is the soft 'sh' of 'ship'; hold both long vowels; the name ends lightly.

The full word family — cognates, derivatives, and variant spellings — is traced in Extended Lore → Etymology.

The Accent / Script Rule

Avalokiteśvara is Tier 1 by rule: the restoration preserves three features ASCII loses — the long ō of lōkita, the long ē of tēśvara, and the palatal ś. The Chinese translated rather than transliterated (Guanshiyin, 'Perceiver of the World's Sounds'), which is why East Asia knows him by the listening, not the Sanskrit.

Extended Lore

Avalokiteśvara begins with an embarrassing question for the rest of us: when did you last actually look at the person suffering next to you? His name is not a compliment but a job description — the Lord Who Looks Down — and his myths anatomize the looking: the gaze that scans crowds, that multiplies arms so no cry goes unanswered, that takes the sufferer's own form as the only form that will do. In this he is Buddhism's answer to a problem the philosophers struggled with: how compassion can be both boundless and particular. His solution is attention. He does not love humanity in general; he hears one drowning sailor, one prisoner, one frightened child, and the hearing is total. The Lotus Sūtra's promise that the name itself saves is the practical corollary: anyone can borrow the gaze by borrowing the name. To recite it is to practice looking until the looking becomes involuntary — until, like the eye in the palm, seeing and reaching are a single motion.
