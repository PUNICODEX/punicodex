Radgosc, the God Among the Lakes (The Temple-City)

Our earliest witness is an enemy. ‌Thietmar of Merseburg, bishop and chronicler, wrote in book VI of his Chronicon (c. 1012–1018) about Riedegost — the Germans called it Radgosc — the chief seat of the Redarii, a Slavic people ​between the Elbe and the Oder. There stood a vast wooden temple, heaped with the offerings of many tribes, and in it the image of the god who gave the city his ‌name: Radigost. Thietmar wrote with open contempt — he was a soldier of the Church that wanted this cult destroyed — which is precisely why his testimony matters: a propagandist does not invent ​the power of what he attacks. Radgosc was not a village shrine but a confederate sanctuary, the religious capital of the Elbe Slavs, where tribes that quarreled in peacetime met in the ‌god's shadow. The myth Radagast never got to tell survives as architecture: a god whose legend is a great wooden hall, and whose worship was the politics of a people.

Answers Carved in ​Wood (The Oracle)

Adam of Bremen, writing his history of the Hamburg church some sixty years later (Gesta Hammaburgensis II.18, c. 1075), adds what Thietmar omits: the oracle. Radgosc, Adam says, was ‌consulted by all the Slavic peoples; the god answered, and the answers were carved and carried home to govern war and settlement alike. His temple had bronze doors and walls hung with ​weapons — an arsenal of vows. Helmold of Bosau (Chronica Slavorum I.2, c. 1170) supplies the scandalous coda: even Christian Saxons consulted the oracle, crossing the religious frontier the Church was drawing. An ‌oracle crosses borders because necessity does — the god of a single tribe can be refused, but a god who answers the questions everyone must ask cannot. Radagast's carved judgments made him ​the judge of peoples who shared no language but his: in the eleventh-century north, a Slavic god sat for a while on the throne of uncertainty where every nation seeks counsel.

The Temple ​Falls (The Burning)

The chronicle of the god's end is brief. Adam of Bremen records that the temple of Radgosc burned — around 1068, in the Saxon margraves' wars against the Elbe ‌Slavs, as the empire pushed its frontier eastward with sword and baptism. With the hall went the oracle, the standard, and whatever priesthood kept the god's house. No Slavic lament for Radagast survives ‌— no Slavic chronicle survives at all — so the burning is known only from the winning side, in the flat tone with which victors note a difficulty removed. Yet the destruction ‌was the making of the god's legend: a cult that had concentrated the wealth and allegiance of a confederation went up in a single campaign, and what could not be burned was the ​name. Read backwards through the smoke, the fire is Radagast's passion — the moment when hospitality's door was nailed shut, and the god became a guest in other people's stories.

A Meditation

Radagast is the patron of everything we almost ‌lost. His entire dossier comes from men who wanted him gone — a bishop, two monks, writing the god into extinction — and yet the name refused to die, surfacing three centuries later ‌on the lips of a fantasy writer who liked its sound. There is a lesson in that: worship is louder than witnesses, and a god of hospitality is especially hard to kill, ‌because hospitality is the one divine office every culture keeps open.

He also asks an uncomfortable question of the scholarly record. When a people's own voices are silenced, what remains is the enemies' ‌paperwork — and we build temples of the imagination on it anyway. Radagast's temple is real now precisely because it is contested: nobody's property, everybody's guest. That, in the end, is the ​meaning of his name. The god who gladly receives survives by being received — by chroniclers, by philologists, by children who never heard of the Redarii and love the wizard anyway.
