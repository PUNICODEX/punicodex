Sanskrit Bhaiṣajyaguru, from bhaiṣajya 'healing, remedy' + guru 'teacher'; the Medicine Buddha of lapis-lazuli radiance.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Indo-Iranian root — bhaiṣajya 'medicine, healing' from bhiṣaj 'healer' — cognate with Avestan baēšaza- 'healing, cure' (the Zoroastrian term baēšazaŋh-), from Indo-Iranian *bhaiš-.
  • Indo-European root — guru 'heavy, weighty; venerable teacher' — cognate with Greek βαρύς (barús) 'heavy', Latin gravis, and English 'grave' (PIE *gʷerh₂-).
  • Name-form — Yàoshī Rúlái 藥師如來 — the Chinese 'Medicine Master Thus-Come-One,' the form the name took across East Asia.

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Bhaiṣajyaguru in Later Traditions

Every Buddhist culture adopted the Medicine Master and filed him differently. In China he heads the 'Three Saints of the East' with Sūryaprabha and Candraprabha, and his sutra joined the scriptures chanted for the dead and the dying in the standard funerary liturgy alongside the Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara texts. Japan made him one of the most-culted buddhas of all: Emperor Tenmu vowed a temple to Yakushi for his ailing empress in 680 — the temple is Nara's Yakushi-ji — and esoteric Buddhism paired him with Amida as healer of body and soul. Tibet received him as one of the Eight Medicine Buddhas, invoked against illness through the dhāraṇī Yijing translated. Korean Buddhism chants his name in the Yaksa-je rite still held for the sick. Across all of it, the constant is concrete: medicine for the body first, metaphysics second.

Modern Legacy

Yakushi-ji in Nara — UNESCO-listed, its East Pagoda a surviving seventh-century original — is his most famous monument, and the temple's Hossō-school triad became the template for a thousand Japanese healers' images; Shin Yakushi-ji, also in Nara, preserves the finest set of the twelve generals in clay, a designated National Treasure. In the modern West, 'the Medicine Buddha' has become a familiar presence: Birnbaum's The Healing Buddha (1979) introduced his cult to English readers, his image hangs in hospitals and hospices, and the dhāraṇī is chanted in meditation centers from Kyoto to California. The myrobalan in his bowl — harītakī, the 'remover of disease' — is still stocked in Ayurvedic pharmacies. His oldest promise, that no one should die for want of medicine, reads now like a plank of public health policy.

In the Earth

The iconographic record is rich and datable. Dunhuang's Mogao caves preserve Tang-dynasty wall paintings of the Bhaiṣajyaguru paradise — lapis ground, jewel cords, celestial musicians — among the most complete sutra illustrations surviving. In bronze, gilt-copper Yakushi triads of the Asuka and Hakuho periods mark the cult's arrival in Japan; the Yakushi-ji founding of 680 under Emperor Tenmu is documented in the Nihon Shoki, and the temple's excavated foundations and surviving East Pagoda anchor the tradition in seventh-century fact. Shin Yakushi-ji in Nara preserves its eighth-century Twelve Generals in near-original clay — the single most important sculptural set of the cult. Dedicatory inscriptions across northern China and the Korean peninsula record the sutra's recitation for named donors, giving the healing cult thousands of individual faces.

The Meditation

What Bhaiṣajyaguru means — beyond the dictionary

Bhaiṣajyaguru is the answer to a question most religions dodge: does the sacred care about bodies? His twelve vows say yes, at length — and not only about the dramatic body in crisis but about the poor body, the imprisoned body, the body that goes cold for want of clothing. Healing, in his charter, is social or it is nothing: you cannot cure a fever in a person who will starve tomorrow.

What he models is a vow as a technology — the bodhisattva who looked at the world's suffering, itemized it, and swore an itemized remedy. It is an oddly modern figure: the physician as bodhisattva, the hospital as pure land, medicine as a form of compassion you can measure. When modern health systems debate whether care is a right or a market, the Medicine Master sits at the corner of the debate, bowl in hand, having answered long ago.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Bhaiṣajyaguru, Healing, Medicine, Lapis-Lazuli Light, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Bhaiṣajyaguru?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Bhaiṣajyaguru is /bhaisajyaguru/ — approximately 'BY-shaj-yah-GOO-roo' — the 'sh' is retroflex, tongue curled back; the first syllable is aspirated and the last is lengthened.

02What does Bhaiṣajyaguru mean?

Bhaiṣajyaguru means Medicine master in the buddhist tradition.

03What are the symbols of Bhaiṣajyaguru?

Bhaiṣajyaguru is associated with Lapis-lazuli light (The radiance of his eastern paradise — in his second vow, his very body is to shine like vaidūrya, the blue stone that gives him his title.), The medicine bowl (Held in his left hand, it carries the myrobalan (harītakī) — the one attribute no other major buddha needs.), The myrobalan fruit (The healing berry he offers, called in Ayurveda 'the remover of disease' — medicine as a concrete object, not a metaphor.), The eastern direction (His pure land lies in the east, balancing Amitābha's western paradise — dawn against sunset.), Sun and moon radiance (The bodhisattvas Sūryaprabha and Candraprabha attend him — light and its reflection as healing's witnesses.).

04Why restore Bhaiṣajyaguru in Unicode?

Plain ASCII bhaisajyaguru strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Bhaiṣajyaguru?

“What He Promised Before He Was Buddha” — The stories of Bhaiṣajyaguru are sutras, not epics: scripture built from his twelve vows, his lapis-lit eastern paradise, and the liturgy of healing his name still powers across Asia. The full telling lives on the lore page.
