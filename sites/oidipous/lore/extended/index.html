Etymology & Word Family

From original script to Unicode restoration

Proto-indo-european *h₁eyd- to swell
Original Script Οἰδίπους Oidípous — "Swollen-foot (from οἰδάω + πούς)"

Modern ASCII oidipous Plain-ASCII fallback

Greek Οἰδίπους 'swollen-foot', from οἰδάω 'to swell' and πούς 'foot'; the name encodes the injury inflicted on the infant exposed by his parents.

Derivatives & Descendants

Etymological Kin

  • Greek — Οἰδίπους (Oidípous) — the attested form of Sophocles and Homer; the acute accent on the í is the mark the restoration preserves
  • PIE *h₁eyd- — ‘to swell’ — the root of οἰδέω; cognate with Sanskrit edhate, ‘swells, thrives’ (Beekes, Etymological Dictionary of Greek)
  • PIE *pṓds — ‘foot’ — the root of πούς; cognate with Latin pēs, Sanskrit pád- and English foot

04

Cultural Significance

From ancient cult to modern memory

Oidípous in Later Traditions

The Romans received Oidipous chiefly as a name of terror and gave him a Latin tragedy of his own, Seneca's Oedipus, which rebuilt the story around oracular dread and kept the play alive through the Middle Ages into the Renaissance. Aristotle's Poetics, working from Oidipous Tyrannos, made its plot — peripeteia and anagnorisis combined in one stroke — the theory of tragedy for two millennia. In 1899 Freud named the ‘Oedipus complex’ after the hero, translating public fate into private childhood, and the twentieth century ran the change back through the arts: Cocteau's La Machine infernale, Gide's Oedipe, O'Neill's Mourning Becomes Electra, Pasolini's Edipo Re, Stravinsky's Oedipus Rex staged in Latin. The Greek original survives every translation because its subject — the unbearable speed of self-knowledge — is not antique.

Modern Legacy

The hero left two cults and a vocabulary. At Colonus the grove he sanctified remained sacred ground beside the Academy; at Thebes Pausanias (9.5) records the tomb of Oidipous and the tradition that his bones were brought back from Attica to protect the city that had exiled him — the polluted body as talisman. From the name come ‘oedipal’ and its compounds in every modern psychology; from the play come the critic's terms — peripeteia, anagnorisis, hamartia — that Aristotle coined while reading this one tragedy. Every retelling, from Seneca to the present, rehearses the same discovery: the story is not about incest and patricide, which Oidípous committed in ignorance, but about the moment a man learns what he is.

In the Earth

Oidipous is a Bronze Age memory wearing a Classical costume. Excavations on the Kadmeia at Thebes have uncovered a Mycenaean palace with its archive of Linear B tablets — a real Theban kingdom of the thirteenth century BC whose dynastic troubles could seed a legend of cursed kingship. The Sphinx he defeated still stands in stone: the Sphinx of the Naxians, dedicated at Delphi around 560 BC, is the very monster type, and Attic red-figure cups of the fifth century show Oidípous seated before her with the answer on his lips. No sanctuary of Oidipous has been excavated; his cult was a literary and civic one, kept at Colonus in the grove Sophocles describes and at Thebes in the tomb tradition Pausanias records.

The Meditation

What Oidípous means — beyond the dictionary

Oidípous is the man who answered the riddle of the human condition and failed the examination of his own life, and the two events are one. What the play understands is that intelligence and self-knowledge are different faculties: the Sphinx tested the first, the oracle the second, and only the second is fatal to get wrong. The restored name keeps the acute accent — the stress on the syllable where the wound lives — and the accent is the argument. In an age that trusts answers, Oidípous is the standing reminder that the right answer to the wrong question is the classical definition of tragedy: he knew what walks on four, two and three legs, and never once thought to ask whose son he was.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about Oidípous, Fate, Riddles, Thebes, and Unicode restoration

01How do you pronounce Oidípous?

In reconstructed pronunciation, Oidípous is /oi̯.dí.puːs/ — approximately 'oy-DEE-poos' — stress the middle syllable; the diphthong is the 'oy' of 'boy', and the final syllable is long.

02What does Oidípous mean?

Oidípous means Swollen-foot (from οἰδάω + πούς) in the greek tradition.

03What are the symbols of Oidípous?

Oidípous is associated with The pierced ankles (The wound in the name: the pins that made the exposed child ‘Oidípous’), The Sphinx (The daughter of Typhon and Echidna who held Thebes hostage on her mountain (Hesiod, Theogony 326–329)), The golden brooches (Jocasta's dress-pins, with which Oidípous put out his own eyes at the hour of recognition (Sophocles, OT 1268–1279)), The crossroads (The place where three roads meet — the geometry of a prophecy fulfilling itself), The grove at Colonus (The Eumenides' ground outside Athens where the exile found his rest (Sophocles, Oidipous at Colonus 84–110)).

04Why restore Oidípous in Unicode?

Plain ASCII oidipous strips the stress, length, and script that make the name specific. Unicode restoration returns the name to its original written dignity.

05What is the most important myth about Oidípous?

“Pinned Ankles on Kithairon” — Oidípous is the subject of the most perfectly constructed tragedy in the Greek corpus. Homer, Hesiod and Pindar preserve fragments of his story; Sophocles gave it its final form in two plays set forty years apart — one the anatomy of self-discovery, the other the redemption of the polluted man. His fate is never a secret: the audience knows it from the first line, and that is the point. The full telling lives on the lore page.
