01

Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Ginnungagap (ginnungagap) ​— Primordial ‌Void · the ‌Yawning Gap ​— is ​the Norse cosmogony's ​answer to ​the question of ​what was ‌there first: the ​vast, mild, ​windless chasm ‌that stretched between ​the rime ‌and fog of ​Niflheimr in ‌the north and ​the fire ​and sparks ​of Muspellsheimr in ​the south, ​in whose middle ‌the two ​extremes met, melted, ‌and quickened ​into Ymir — ‌the first ​shape, from ‌whose body the ​gods would ‌later build the ​world.[1]

The ​term is a ‌transparent Old ‌Norse compound: ‌the genitive plural ​ginnunga — ​of disputed sense, ‌read either ​as 'magic-charged, mighty' ​(the ginn- ‌of ginnregin ​and ginnheilagr) or ​as 'deceptive' ‌(the verb ginna, ​'to dupe, ‌bewilder') — plus ​gap, 'chasm, ​yawning void', ​the very word ​Old Norse ‌lent to English. ‌The Poetic ​Edda knows the ‌phrase in ​its own ​word order: Völuspá's ‌gap var ‌ginnunga, 'a gap ‌of ginnungs ‌there was, but ​grass nowhere'.[2]

‌PuniCodex restores ​the name as ​Ginnungagap — ​identical to the ‌ASCII input ​but for the ​capital, because ​normalized Old Norse ‌writes no ​diacritic in ​this compound. The ​mechanical rule ‌therefore places it ​in Tier ‌2: nothing distinctive ​is lost ‌in transit. ​The project holds ‌no domain ‌for this name; ​the plain-ASCII ​ginnungagap.com is registered ​by a ​third party ‌outside the collection ‌and watched ​for release.[3]

  2. Völuspá 3 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, The Poetic Edda, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The name ​is attested in ‌Old Norse as ginnungagap, ‌a neuter compound ​whose second element ​is the everyday noun ​gap — 'chasm, ​gap, yawning void', ​the Old Norse word ‌English borrowed as ​gap in the ​Viking Age.[1] The first ‌element is the ​genitive plural of ‌ginnung, and here ​honesty requires registering a ‌genuine dispute. One ​line, followed by ​the lexicon's gloss, connects ​ginn- with the ​intensive or numinous ​prefix of ginnregin 'the ‌mighty powers' and ​ginnheilagr 'most holy', ‌yielding 'the magic-charged, mighty ​void'; the other ‌derives it from ​the verb ginna ‌'to deceive, dupe, bewilder', ​yielding 'the void ‌of deception'.[2] Cleasby–Vigfusson ​glosses the compound simply ​as 'the great ‌gap of ginnungs' ‌and leaves the ginnungs ‌unexplained; Simek and ​de Vries record ​both readings without settling ‌them.[3]

The ASCII ​form is not ​a compromise here: ‌normalized Old Norse ginnungagap ​is already written ​in plain ASCII ‌letters. There is no ​diacritic and no ‌distinctive letter for ​the restoration to recover, ​and the mechanical ​tier rule therefore ​classifies the name ‌Tier 2 — a ‌statement about orthography, ​not about standing. ‌The Unicode form Ginnungagap ​differs from the ​input only in ‌its capital.

The letter-by-letter ‌transformation runs:

  • ‌g → G ‌— Same, capitalized
  • ​i → i ‌— Same
  • ​n → n ​— Same
  • n ​→ n — ‌Same
  • u ​→ u — Same

​- n → ​n — Same
‌- g → g ​— Same

  • ​a → a ​— Same
  • g ‌→ g — ​Same
  • a ‌→ a — ​Same
  • p → ‌p — Same

​The project holds ‌no domain for this ‌name: ginnungagap.com is ​registered by a ​third party outside the ​PuniCodex collection and ​is watched for ‌release. This temple claims ‌nothing it does ​not hold.

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/ˈɡinː.uŋ.ɡa.ɣap/ — ​Old Norse ​Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme by ​phoneme:

  • ​Gi- — Voiced ​velar stop ‌[ɡ] as ​in English give, ​short close ‌[i], and the ​long geminate ‌nasal [nː] ​written by the ‌doubled n; ​Old Norse, like ​modern Icelandic, ​holds the ​double consonant
  • ​-nnunga — ‌Short back [u], ​then the ‌velar nasal [ŋ] ​before the ‌following stop

​- -gap — ‌Hard [ɡ] ​at the head ‌of the ​second element, ​short open [a], ‌and final ‌unaspirated [p]; the ‌word's own ​everyday noun, ​said as plainly ‌as it ​is written

For ​the modern ‌speaker, the ​closest approximation is: ​'GIN-noong-ahp' — ‌stress the first ​syllable, hold ‌the double n, ​keep the ​u short ​as in put, ​and clip ‌the final p.

‌Kindred and ​historical forms ‌of the name:

​- Old ​Norse — ginnungagap, ‌'gap of ‌the ginnungs'; ‌Völuspá's own order ‌is gap ​ginnunga (st. 3)[2]
‌- Modern ​Icelandic — ​ginnungagap, the learned ​word for ‌the primordial void
​- English ​— gap, the ​Viking-Age loan ‌that is ​the compound's second ​element

Ginnungagap ​is Tier 2 ‌because the ​restoration preserves ‌nothing the ASCII ​form loses: ‌the normalized Old ​Norse spelling ‌is already ‌plain ASCII, and ​only the ​capital changes. The ​void was ​never hard ‌to type — ‌only hard ​to imagine.

Original Script & Provenance

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Original writing system, transliteration steps, uncertainty markers, font/display notes.

The name ​is preserved ‌in medieval ‌Icelandic manuscripts in ​the Latin ​alphabet as ​ginnungagap — Old ​Norse (North ​Germanic), attested ‌13th c. CE ​in the ​Codex Regius ‌of the Poetic ​Edda (as ‌the phrase ​gap ginnunga in ‌Völuspá 3) ​and in ​the codices of ​the Prose ​Edda (as ​the compound place-name), ‌recording material ​centuries older.[1] Before ‌the Latin ​bookhand, Old ‌Norse was carved ​in the ‌younger futhark; ​no runic attestation ‌of the ​compound survives, ​so the manuscript ‌form is ‌the primary ‌witness.

The scholarly ​rendering proceeds ​step by ‌step:

  • The ​compound is ​ginnunga (genitive ‌plural of ginnung, ​sense disputed: ​'magic-charged, mighty' ‌or 'deceptive') + ​gap 'chasm, ‌yawning void' ​— the word ​Old Norse ​lent to ​English.[2]
  • Old ‌Norse orthography ‌writes no ​diacritic in this ‌word: vowels ​are short ​throughout, and every ‌letter is ‌plain ASCII.

‌- The doubled ‌nn marks ​a long ‌geminate consonant, held ​in pronunciation ​as in ​modern Icelandic.

  • ‌In the ​Younger Futhark the ​word would ​have been ‌carved ᚴᛁᚾᚢᚾᚴᛅᚴᛅᛒ — ​the k-rune ​writing g, ​the b-rune writing ‌final p, ​the geminate ‌written once.[3]
  • ​The Unicode ‌restoration Ginnungagap ​therefore changes nothing ‌but the ‌capital; the ​ASCII form loses ​nothing, which ​is exactly ​why the mechanical ‌rule classifies ‌the name ​Tier 2.

Sources

  2. Cleasby & Vigfússon, An Icelandic-English Dictionary, 2nd ed. (Clarendon Press, 1957), s.v. ginnunga-gap, gap, ginna.
  3. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. Taylor (Clarendon Press, 1957).

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Ginnungagap is ​catalogued under ‌the domain ‌Primordial Void, and ​it is ​the one ​entry in the ​lexicon whose ​domain is itself: ‌the emptiness ​that precedes ​and permits the ‌world.[1]

​The Primordial ‌Void

Before earth, ​sea, or ‌sky, there ​was the gap ​— 'a ​gap of ginnungs, ​but grass ​nowhere', as ‌Völuspá puts it. ​Snorri adds ‌the detail ​that makes it ‌habitable to ​thought: Ginnungagap ‌was as mild ​as a ‌windless sky, neutral ​weather between ​two extremes.

‌### The Meeting ‌of Opposites

‌The void's ​function is to ​hold rime ‌and fire ​apart and then ​let them ‌touch: where the ​sparks and ​embers flying ‌out of Muspellsheimr ​met the ‌rime drifting ​from Niflheimr, the ​gap thawed, ​dripped, and ​the drops quickened ‌into Ymir.

‌### The Cradle ​of Gods ‌and Giants

​Everything that lives ​descends through ‌the gap: ‌the giants from ‌Ymir, the ‌gods from ​Búri, whom the ‌cow Auðhumla ​licked free of ​the rime-stones ​— the ‌ancestor of Óðinn ​himself.

​The Frame ​of the Made ‌World

The ​world was ​not made elsewhere ​and moved ‌in: Óðinn and ​his brothers ‌dragged Ymir's ​body into the ‌middle of ​Ginnungagap and ‌built the earth, ‌sea, and ​sky out ​of it there. ​The gap ​is the world's ‌frame as ‌well as ​its beginning.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 4–8; Völuspá 3, trans. Faulkes (1987) and Larrington (2014).

Symbols

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Iconography, attributes, and their meanings.

A ​place rather ‌than a ‌person, Ginnungagap ​carries no ​regalia and ​receives ​no cult; ​its iconography ‌is the ​inventory of ​the creation ‌scene itself:[1]

​- ‌The gap ​itself — ‌Emptiness as ​precondition: the ​room in ​which ​opposites can ​meet and ‌anything at ​all can ‌begin

  • ​Rime ‌and spark ​— The ‌two weathers ​of the ‌void: ice ​and ​freezing fog ‌drifting from ‌the Élivágar ‌of Niflheimr, ​embers and ​glowing sparks ‌flying ​out of ​Muspellsheimr
  • ‌The quickening ​drops — ​Life condensing ‌out ​of the ‌meltwater where ​the extremes ​collided, the ​drops that ​'took ‌the likeness ‌of a ​man' and ‌became Ymir

​- The ​windless mildness ‌— ‌Snorri's strangest ‌detail, that ‌the gap ​between ice-world ‌and fire-world ​was ​as mild ​as a ‌windless sky: ​the void ​is neutral, ​not ‌hostile

  • ​The rime-stones ​and Auðhumla ​— The ‌salt-licked ice ​from ‌which the ​first ancestor ‌of the ​gods, Búri, ‌emerged over ‌three days: ​even ​the divine ​line begins ​as something ‌the void's ‌weather set ​free[2]

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

Ginnungagap has no ​adventures, because it is not a ‌character but the condition of there ‌being characters at all. Its mythology ​is the first chapter of everything: ​the Norse creation, told in Völuspá's ​three terse lines and unpacked by ​Snorri into the finest cosmogony the ​Germanic world preserved.

The Gap ‌of Ginnungs (Völuspá 3)

The seeress ​reaches back before memory: early in ​time, when Ymir made his settlement, ‌there was no sand nor sea ​nor cool waves; earth was found ‌nowhere, nor the sky above — ​gap var ginnunga, en gras hvergi, ‌'a gap of ginnungs there was, ​but grass nowhere'. That is the ​oldest notice of the void, and ​everything Snorri builds hangs on its ​two words.[1]

Fog in the ​North, Fire in the South (Gylfaginning ‌4–5)

Asked what was before the ​earth, Hár tells Gylfi that Niflheimr ‌was made many ages before the ​world: a dark world of mist ‌with the spring Hvergelmir at its ​heart, from which run the rivers ‌called the Élivágar, which hardened far ​from their source like slag from ‌a forge and spread rime northward ​into the gap. But the southern ​part of Ginnungagap faced Muspellsheimr, the ‌bright, burning world no foreigner can ‌enter, where Surtr stands at the ‌frontier with a flaming sword — ​and the sparks that flew out ​of it lightened the gap from ‌below.[2]

The Mild Void and ​the Quickening (Gylfaginning 5–6)

Between the ​two, Ginnungagap was as mild as ‌a windless sky. Where the warm ​air met the rime, the ice ​thawed and dripped, and by the ‌power of the one who sent ​the heat the drops quickened into ‌the likeness of a man: Ymir, ​whom the rime-giants call Aurgelmir. Asked ​whether Ymir was a god, Hár ​answers flatly that he and all ​his kin were evil. From the ‌same rime-drops came the cow Auðhumla, ‌whose milk fed the giant and ​who licked the salty rime-stones until, ‌over three days, Búri emerged — ​the grandfather of Óðinn.[2]

The ​Flood and the Fashioning (Gylfaginning 7–8)

‌Búri's grandson Óðinn and his brothers ‌Vili and Vé killed Ymir, and ‌so much blood ran from his ‌wounds that the race of rime-giants ​drowned, save Bergelmir, who escaped with ‌his wife onto his lúðr. The ​brothers dragged Ymir's body into the ​middle of Ginnungagap and made the ​world of it: earth from his ‌flesh, sea from his blood, mountains ​from his bones, sky from his ​skull — held up by the ​dwarfs Norðri, Suðri, Austri, and Vestri ‌— and clouds from his brains; ​the sparks of Muspellsheimr they set ​in the sky as stars. The ​gap was not filled so much ‌as furnished: everything that exists is ​a giant's body arranged inside the ‌old emptiness.[2]

The Poets' Version ​(Vafþrúðnismál 21 · Grímnismál 40–41)

The ‌Poetic Edda confirms the outline from ​its own witnesses: Vafþrúðnir tells Óðinn ‌that out of the Élivágar sprang ‌poison-drops that grew until a giant ​came from them, and Grímnismál gives ​the fashioning in verse — 'from ​Ymir's flesh the earth was shaped, ​and the mountains from his bones; ‌the sky from the skull of ‌the frost-cold giant, and from his ​blood the sea'.[3]

Sources

  1. Völuspá 3 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 4–8, trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  3. Vafþrúðnismál 21 and Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

The obvious ​cross-border twin ‌is Hesiod's ‌Chaos: 'first ​of all ​came Chaos' ​opens the ​Greek theogony, ​and the ‌Greek word ​itself derives ​from a ‌root meaning ​'to yawn, ‌gape' — ​chaos and ‌gap are ​the same ​image in ​two languages, ​the world ​beginning as ‌an open ​mouth.[1] The ‌parallel is ​real but ‌easy to ​overdraw: Hesiod's ‌Chaos is ​a begetter, ‌parent of ​Erebos and ​Night, while ‌Ginnungagap begets ‌nothing by ‌itself — ​it is ​the neutral ‌stage on ​which rime ​and fire ‌do the ​begetting, and ​the North ‌reserves the ​making for ‌the gods ​who arrive ​later.

The ​deeper comparison ​is structural. ‌Scholars such ‌as Lindow ​have set ‌the Norse ​account among ​the broader ‌Indo-European creation ‌patterns — ‌the primordial ‌being dismembered ​into the ‌world has ​cousins from ​Vedic Puruṣa ​onward — ‌but the ​void itself, ​cold at ​one end ‌and burning ​at the ​other, is ​the corpus's ‌own signature.[2] ​Within the ‌pantheon, the ​gap's closest ‌kin are ​the two ‌worlds that ‌flank it, ​Niflheimr and ​Muspellsheimr, and ​Ragnarǫk, the ​fire that ‌ends what ‌the gap's ​fire began.

Sources

  1. Hesiod, Theogony 116 (Chaos first of all), ed. and trans. Glenn W. Most, Loeb Classical Library (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2006).
  2. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. creation, Ginnungagap.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Ginnungagap ​is the ‌rare cosmological ‌term that ​never had ​to ​be revived, ​because it ​was never ‌allowed ​to die: ​Modern Icelandic ‌keeps ginnungagap ​as its ‌word ​for the ‌primordial void, ​and Icelandic ​science writing ​reaches ​for it ​when describing ‌the nothing ​before ‌the Big ​Bang.[1]

The ‌nineteenth-century Norse ​revival made ‌the ​scene a ‌set-piece: the ​Kearys' The ​Heroes ‌of Asgard ‌(1857), the ‌first popular ​English retelling ​of ‌the myths, ​opens its ​cosmology in ‌the great ​gulf ​between ice ‌and fire, ​and every ‌handbook ​since — ​from Simek ​to Orchard ​— has ‌treated ‌the word ​as the ‌North's answer ​to chaos.[2] ​Modern ‌Heathenry honours ‌the creation ‌narrative as ‌its ​cosmogony, and ‌popular culture ​borrows the ​name wherever ​a ‌story needs ​a primal ​abyss, from ​Marvel's ‌Thor comics ​to fantasy ​games. Its ​deepest legacy ‌is ​conceptual: the ‌idea, natural ​to the ‌North, that ​the ‌precondition of ‌creation is ​not a ​maker's ​word but ​an empty ‌space where ‌opposites can ​meet.

Sources

  1. Orchard, Andy, Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend (London: Cassell, 1997), s.v. Ginnungagap.
  2. Keary, Annie, and Eliza Keary, The Heroes of Asgard: Tales from Scandinavian Mythology (London: Macmillan, 1857).

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

A ​void can ‌leave no ‌votives: ​Ginnungagap's evidence ​is entirely ​manuscript ​evidence, and ​unusually good ‌of ​its kind. ​Völuspá, ‌Vafþrúðnismál, and ​Grímnismál survive ‌in ​the Codex ‌Regius of ​the ​Poetic Edda ​(GKS 2365 ​4to, ​c. 1270), ‌the Reykjavík ​codex ‌that preserves ​nearly the ‌whole ​mythological corpus.[1] ‌Snorri's ​Gylfaginning, with ‌its systematic ​account ​of the ‌gap, is ‌transmitted ‌in the ​Codex Regius ​of ‌the Prose ​Edda (GKS ​2367 ‌4to, early ​14th century), ​the ‌Codex Wormianus, ​and ‌the Codex ​Upsaliensis, the ​last ​sometimes held ​to preserve ‌an ‌earlier redaction.[1]

​No runic ‌inscription ​names the ​compound — ‌a ‌silence it ‌shares with ‌every ​other item ‌of ​cosmological furniture ​— and ​no ‌picture stone ​can be ​securely ​read as ‌the meeting ​of ​rime and ​fire.[2] The ‌archaeology ​of Ginnungagap ‌is thus ​the ‌archaeology of ​the ‌bookhand: a ‌phrase in ​thirteenth-century ​Icelandic codices, ​preserving the ​first ‌sentence of ‌the northern ​world.

Poetic Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Poetic ​Edda's notice ‌of the ‌void is one ​stanza, and ​it is ​the foundation ​of everything Snorri ​says. Völuspá's ‌seeress, reaching ​back before memory, ​sets the ‌scene in ​three lines: ‌early in time, ​when Ymir ‌made his ​settlement, there was ​no sand ​nor sea ​nor cool waves; ​earth was ‌found nowhere, ​nor the ‌sky above — ​gap var ‌ginnunga, en ​gras hvergi, 'a ‌gap of ​ginnungs there ‌was, but grass ​nowhere'.[1] The ​poem never ‌explains the ‌ginnungs; it simply ‌registers the ​emptiness where ​the world will ‌later be, ​with the ​first giant ‌already housed at ​its edge.

​The Edda's ‌other witnesses supply ​the inventory ‌without the ​name. Vafþrúðnismál, Óðinn's ​wisdom-contest with ​the giant, ​gives the ‌origin of giantkind: ‌out of ​the Élivágar ‌sprang poison-drops, which ​grew until ​a giant ‌came from ‌them — the ‌quickening Snorri ‌stages inside ​the gap.[2] Grímnismál ‌gives the ​fashioning in ​verse: from Ymir's ​flesh the ‌earth was ​shaped, from ​his blood the ​sea, from ‌his bones ​the mountains, from ​his skull ​the sky ‌— and adds ​that the ‌gracious powers ​made Miðgarðr ‌for men's sons ​from his ‌eyebrows, and ‌the hard-mooded clouds ​from his ​brains.[3] The ​poems and ​Snorri agree on ‌the structure; ‌only the ​staging differs.

Sources

  1. Völuspá 3 (Poetic Edda), trans. Carolyne Larrington, 2nd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014).
  2. Vafþrúðnismál 21 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).
  3. Grímnismál 40–41 (Poetic Edda), trans. Larrington (2014).

Prose Edda

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The Prose ​Edda is Ginnungagap's ‌great witness: the ‌compound appears as ​a fixed cosmological ​term only here, ​and Snorri's Gylfaginning ​4–9 gives the ​fullest creation narrative ‌the Germanic ​world preserved.[1] The ​account is built ‌as a geography ​of the void. ‌North lies Niflheimr, ​made many ages ‌before the world, ​with the spring ​Hvergelmir feeding the ​rivers called the ​Élivágar, whose waters ​hardened far from ‌their source and ​spread rime and ‌freezing fog into ​the gap. South ‌lies Muspellsheimr, ​bright and burning, ‌impassable to any ​not native to ‌it, with Surtr ​standing at the ​frontier with a ‌flaming sword, its ‌sparks and embers ‌lighting the gap ​from below. Between ​them, Snorri insists, ‌Ginnungagap was as ​mild as a ​windless sky — ‌the neutral medium ​in which the ​extremes could meet.

‌The meeting ​itself is told ‌with a physicist's ​patience: the warm ​air thawed the ​rime, the ice ​dripped, and by ‌the power of ‌the one who ​sent the heat ‌the drops quickened ​into the likeness ​of a man ‌— Ymir, called ‌Aurgelmir by the ‌rime-giants, and emphatically ‌no god. Then ​come the cow ‌Auðhumla, the ​salt-licked rime-stones, and ​Búri; then the ​killing of Ymir ‌by Óðinn and ​his brothers, the ​blood-flood that drowns ​the giants save ‌Bergelmir, and the ​fashioning of earth, ​sea, mountains, sky, ​clouds, and stars ‌from the giant's ​body in the ‌middle of the ​gap.[1] Snorri's method ‌deserves note: he ​anchors his ‌systematic prose in ‌quotation, opening the ​account with Völuspá's ​own stanza on ​the gap of ​ginnungs, so that ‌the poem's two ‌words carry the ​whole edifice.[2]

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 4–9, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 4, quotation of Völuspá 3, trans. Faulkes (1987).

Runic Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​runic corpus ‌contains ‌no attestation ​of ​Ginnungagap. No ​stone ​invokes the ​void, ‌no amulet ​names ​it, and ‌no ​inscription yet ‌found ​preserves the ‌compound ​— a ​silence ​it shares ​with ​every other ‌item ​of ‌Norse cosmological ​furniture, ‌since runestones ​commemorate ‌people and ​their ‌deeds, not ​the ​furniture of ‌the ‌sky.[1]

What ‌the ​runic record ​offers ‌is orthographic ​context. ​Had a ‌Viking-Age ​carver written ​the ‌compound in ​the ‌Younger Futhark, ​the ​normalized rendering ​would ​be ᚴᛁᚾᚢᚾᚴᛅᚴᛅᛒ ‌(k-i-n-u-n-k-a-k-a-p): ‌the k-rune ​ᚴ ‌writes both ​k ​and g, ‌the ‌b-rune ᛒ ‌writes ‌both b ​and ‌p, and ​runic ​practice economizes ​geminates, ‌so ​the doubled ​n ​of ginnunga ‌would ​be carved ​once.[2] ​The manuscript ‌form ​in the ‌Latin ​bookhand — ‌plain ​ginnungagap — ‌is ‌therefore the ​primary ​witness, and ​the ​restoration changes ‌nothing ‌but the ​capital.

Sources

  1. Lindow, John, Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001), s.v. Ginnungagap.
  2. Gordon, E. V., An Introduction to Old Norse, 2nd ed. rev. A. R. Taylor (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1957), on runic orthography and the Younger Futhark.

Sagas & Medieval Sources

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Beyond the ​Eddas, the ‌void keeps ‌a low profile. ​The sagas ​proper never stage ​the creation: ​the fornaldarsǫgur ​and the Íslendingasǫgur ‌begin long ​after the world ​is built, ‌and even ​Snorri's own Heimskringla ‌opens with ​the euhemerized ‌geography of Óðinn's ​migration rather ​than with cosmogony. ​Ginnungagap belongs ​to the ​learned stratum of ‌the tradition ​— the stratum ‌of encyclopedic ​prose and ‌mythological verse — ​and it ‌is there that ​its medieval ‌life is ​spent.[1]

Two scholarly ​questions frame ‌that life. ‌The first is ‌Snorri's own ​framing: the Prologue ​to his ‌Edda offers ​a rationalizing, creationist ​account of ‌how men came ​to speculate ​about the ‌world's making, so ​that the ‌pagan cosmogony ​he then retells ​arrives already ​enclosed in a ​Christian scholar's ‌commentary.[2] The ‌second is the ​modern debate ‌over the term ​itself: because ​the compound ‌ginnungagap as a ‌fixed place-name ‌appears only ‌in Snorri, while ​the Poetic ‌Edda has the ​freer phrase ​gap ginnunga, ​scholars have asked ‌how far ​Snorri systematized a ​looser poetic ​image into ‌cosmic geography — ​Simek registers ​the question, and ​the honest ‌answer is ​that the poem's ‌phrase is ​old and ‌the tidy map ​may be ‌younger.[3] The word's ‌afterlife, in ​any case, ​is secure: Modern ​Icelandic keeps ​ginnungagap as its ‌learned word ‌for the ​primordial void.

Sources

  1. Jónas Kristjánsson, Eddas and Sagas: Iceland's Medieval Literature, trans. Peter Foote (Reykjavík: Hið íslenska bókmenntafélag, 1988).
  2. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Prologue, ed. and trans. Anthony Faulkes (London: Everyman, 1987).
  3. Simek, Rudolf, Dictionary of Northern Mythology, trans. Angela Hall (Cambridge: D. S. Brewer, 1993), s.v. Ginnungagap.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

Every ​cosmogony must ‌answer ‌the same ​rude question: ​what ​was there ​before ​anything was? ‌The ​North's answer ​is the ‌strangest ​and, in ‌its ​way, the ‌most ​honest — ​room. Not ​a ​god, not ​a ‌word, not ​even ‌darkness with ​a ‌personality: a ​gap, mild ‌as ​a windless ‌sky, ​with ice ​weather ‌at one ‌end and ‌fire ​weather at ​the ‌other.[1]

Creation, ​in ​this telling, ‌is not ​commanded; ​it condenses. ‌Two ​climates that ‌could ​never touch ​are given ​somewhere ​to meet, ‌and ‌the world ​is ‌what drips ​out of ​the ‌meeting. Ginnungagap ‌asks ‌a quieter ‌question ​than most ‌voids — ​not ​what made ​you, ‌but what ​made ​room for ​you ‌— and ​it suggests ​that ​every beginning, ‌cosmological ​or personal, ‌starts ​the same ‌way: with ​an ‌emptiness patient ‌enough ​to hold ​the ​opposites apart ​until they ‌are ‌ready to ​collide.

Sources

  1. Snorri Sturluson, Edda, Gylfaginning 5 (the mildness of the gap), trans. Faulkes (1987).
