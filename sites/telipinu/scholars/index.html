Overview

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Concise scholarly summary of the figure, name, tradition, and significance.

Telipinu (telipinu) ​— Agriculture, Vanishing ‌God · ‌Unknown — belongs ​to the Hittite ​tradition, where ​it is catalogued ​under the domain ​"Agriculture, Vanishing ‌God". The name ​means "Unknown"[1].

Telipinu ​is the ‌Hittite god who ​makes the grain ‌grow — ​and the god ‌who, when offended, ​takes his ​gift away. His ​myth is not ​a cosmic ​battle but a ‌domestic crisis of ​divine temper: the ‌deity storms ​out of the ‌assembly, slams the ​door, and ‌wanders off in ​a rage. Behind ‌him the ​fields wither, cattle ​abandon their calves, ‌mothers forget ‌their children, and ‌even the cooking ​fire goes ​out. The world ‌does not end ​by flood ​or dragon; it ‌ends because a ​god has ​left the room.

‌The Telipinu Myth ​(CTH 324) is ‌one of ​the best-preserved Hittite ​religious narratives. It ​is also ​a ritual text: ‌the story is ‌performed in ​order to undo ‌the very disaster ​it describes. ​Telipinu is therefore ‌a god of ‌restorable order ‌— his absence ‌is catastrophic, but ​his return, ‌properly negotiated by ​magic and apology, ​brings everything ​back.[2]

PuniCodex restores ‌the name as ​Telipinu and serves ​its temple ​at its temple. ‌The restoration needs ​no distinctive ​letters or diacritics ​its ASCII form ‌would lose, ​which places the ‌name in Tier ​2. The ‌plain ASCII form ​telipinu survives as ‌a modern ‌convenience imposed by ​the early domain-name ​system; the ​restoration, not the ​fallback, is the ‌form the ‌project defends as ​philologically complete[3].

Sources

  1. CTH 324 — Telipinu Myth.
  2. Hoffner, Hittite Myths (2nd ed., 1998).
  3. CHD (Chicago Hittite Dictionary).

The Name

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Etymology, ASCII constraint, Unicode restoration, name variations, tier classification.

The ​name is ‌attested ‌in Cuneiform ​(Hittite) ​as ​𒀭𒋼𒂊𒇷𒁉𒉡𒌑. Etymologically ​it ​means "Unknown"[1].

‌The ​ASCII form ​telipinu ‌survives only ​because ‌the ​early domain-name ‌system ​could not ​carry ​diacritics; it ​is ​a technological ‌compromise, ​not ‌an ancient ​spelling. ‌The Unicode ​restoration ‌Telipinu recovers ​the ‌full diacritic ​detail ​of ‌the scholarly ‌transliteration ‌directly in ​the ​address bar. ‌The ​restoration needs ​no ‌distinctive ​letters or ​diacritics ‌its ASCII ​form ‌would lose, ​which ​places ​the name ​in ‌Tier 2.

​- ​p → ‌p ​— ‌Same

  • ‌n ​→ n ​— ​Same

Pronunciation

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

IPA reconstruction, phoneme breakdown, approximation, kin forms.

The reconstructed ​pronunciation of ‌the name is ‌/teliˈpiːnu/ — ​Hittite Reconstruction.[1]

Phoneme ​by phoneme:

​- Te- — ​Voiceless alveolar ​stop [t] followed ‌by close-mid ​front vowel [e]; ​the first ‌syllable is light ​and unstressed ‌in the Hittite ​accent.

  • ‌-li- — Alveolar ​lateral [l] ​between two light ​vowels; the ​name's melodic center.

​- -pī- ‌— Bilabial stop ​[p] plus long ‌close front ​vowel [iː]; the ‌macron marks ​a reconstructed length, ‌not a ​sign-given fact.

  • ‌-nu — ​Nasal [n] plus ​close back ‌rounded vowel [u]; ‌the name's ‌calm, open ending.

​For the ​modern speaker, the ‌closest approximation ​is: 'teh-lee-PEE-noo' — ​stress the ‌long third syllable ​and let ​the final vowel ‌settle gently, ​like rain returning ‌to a ​field.

Kindred and ​historical forms ​of the name:

​- Hittite ‌cuneiform — 𒀭𒋾𒇷𒁉𒉡 ‌(dTe-li-pi-nu), the ​vanishing god of ‌agriculture and ​vegetation

  • Hattic ​— Telipinu ‌is probably a ‌Hattic loan ‌or theonym, like ‌many central ​Anatolian deities adopted ‌into Hittite ​cult
  • Luwian ​comparison — ​Parallel 'disappearing god' ‌myths attach to ​other Anatolian ​weather and vegetation ​deities

Telipinu ‌is Tier 2: ​the canonical ​Unicode form preserves ​only capitalization ‌against the ASCII ​form, with ‌no distinctive diacritic ​or non-Latin ‌letter. The macron ​in /teliˈpiːnu/ ‌is a philological ‌reconstruction of ​vowel length; it ​is not ​part of the ​registrable restoration ‌and therefore does ‌not change ​the tier.

Sources

  1. CTH 324 — Telipinu Myth.

Domains & Attributes

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sphere of influence, titles, epithets, domain cards.

Telipinu is ​the Hittite god ‌who makes the ‌grain grow — ​and the god ​who, when offended, ​takes his gift ​away. His myth ​is not a ‌cosmic battle but ​a domestic crisis ​of divine temper: ‌the deity storms ​out of the ‌assembly, slams the ​door, and wanders ‌off in a ​rage. Behind him ​the fields wither, ​cattle abandon their ​calves, mothers forget ​their children, and ‌even the ​cooking fire goes ‌out. The world ​does not end ‌by flood or ​dragon; it ends ‌because a god ​has left the ‌room.

The Telipinu ​Myth (CTH 324) ​is one of ‌the best-preserved Hittite ‌religious narratives. It ‌is also a ​ritual text: the ​story is performed ‌in order to ​undo the very ​disaster it describes. ‌Telipinu is therefore ​a god of ​restorable order — ‌his absence is ​catastrophic, but his ‌return, properly negotiated ​by magic and ​apology, brings everything ​back.[1]

The ​Vanishing God

When ‌offended, Telipinu puts ‌his right shoe ​on his left ‌foot and his ​left shoe on ​his right foot, ‌then storms into ‌the wilderness — ‌a formal gesture ‌of inverted order.

​### Famine & ‌Forgetfulness

His absence ​dries up springs, ​kills the grain, ​and unbinds social ‌bonds: sheep ​reject their lambs ​and mothers turn ​away from their ‌children.

The ​Bee Messenger

The ​gods send a ​bee to find ‌Telipinu in the ​meadow; it stings ‌him awake, inflaming ​his rage until ‌the goddess Kamrušepa ​performs the calming ‌ritual.

Ritual ‌Return

Kamrušepa, goddess ​of magic and ​healing, recites the ​ritual that turns ​Telipinu's anger aside ‌and leads him ‌home, restoring fertility ​and plenty.

07

Mythology

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Core myths, primary narratives, and textual evidence.

The mythology of ​Telipinu survives in Hittite ‌tablets from Ḫattuša, above all ‌in CTH 324. It is ​a myth about the ​fragility of fertility: the grain, ​the cattle, and the ​family all depend on a ​god who can choose to ‌leave. The drama is ​not cosmic combat but divine ​temperament and its ritual ‌repair.[1]

The God Who ​Stormed Out (CTH 324)

‌The myth opens in the ​divine assembly, where something unknown ‌offends Telipinu. He rises, ​puts his shoes on the ​wrong feet, and leaves ​in a fury. As he ​goes, 'the mist seized the ​windows, smoke seized the ‌house, in the fireplace the ​logs were stifled, at ‌the altars the gods were ​stifled, in the fold the ‌sheep were stifled, in ​the corral the cattle were ‌stifled; the mother ignored ​her child, the ewe ignored ‌her lamb.' The cosmos does ​not collapse; it simply ​forgets how to flourish.[2]

‌The Search in the ‌Wilderness (CTH 324)

The great ‌gods send out the ​Storm-God of Heaven, the Sun-Goddess ​of Arinna, and others to ‌find Telipinu, but none ​can locate him. Finally the ​bee is dispatched: 'Go, ‌search for Telipinu in the ​high country, in the meadow, ​in the swamp, and ‌st him!' The bee finds ​him asleep in a ‌meadow or among the mountains, ​stings him, and wakes him ​— but this only ​enrages him further. He multiplies ​the disasters he has ‌already caused.

Kamrušepa's Spell ‌(CTH 324)

The goddess Kamrušepa, ​'mother of the gods' ‌and expert in magic, takes ​charge. She performs a ​ritual of appeasement: she anoints ‌Telipinu, sprinkles him with ‌fine oil, and recites formulae ‌that transfer his anger to ‌objects of no consequence ​— a spindle, a reed, ‌a pot, or the ​underworld itself. 'Let Telipinu's anger, ​his wrath, his sin, and ​his vexation depart!' With ‌each symbolic action the god's ​fury is peeled away ​and the world is prepared ​for his return.

The ‌Return of Plenty (CTH ​324)

When Telipinu comes home, ​the myth turns to ​restoration: 'The mist let go ‌of the windows, the smoke ​let go of the ‌house, the logs in the ​fireplace let go of ‌their stifling, the gods at ​the altars let go ‌of their stifling.' The grain ‌grows thick, the vines heavy, ​the sheep and cattle ​multiply, and the king and ​queen are blessed. The ​ritual re-enacts this return, making ‌the myth a performance that ‌heals the land by ​rehearsing its healing.

Sources

  1. Hoffner, Hittite Myths (2nd ed., 1998).
  2. CHD (Chicago Hittite Dictionary).
08

Syncretism & Reception

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Cross-cultural identification, later adaptations, and interpretatio.

Telipinu ​belongs to ‌a ‌broader ​Anatolian type: ​the ​'disappearing ​god' (der ​verschwundene ‌Gott), ​a category ​first ‌named by ​Hittitologists ‌for ​deities whose ‌absence ​causes ​agricultural and ​social ​collapse. ​Similar patterns ‌appear ​for the ‌weather-god ​of ‌Nerik, the ​vegetation ‌god ​Halki, and, ‌in ​Greek ​Anatolia, figures ‌such ‌as Attis ‌and ​Adonis ​whose death ‌or ​departure ​marks the ‌failing ​year. ​Unlike the ‌Mesopotamian ​flood or ‌the ​Egyptian ​solar eclipse, ​the ​Hittite ‌crisis is ‌not ​cosmic ‌but relational: ​the ​god is ‌offended, ‌leaves, ‌and must ‌be ​coaxed ‌back. This ​pattern ​influenced ​later Anatolian ‌and ​Greek conceptions ​of ​dying-and-rising ‌vegetation gods, ​though ​direct ​genealogical links ‌remain ​debated.[1]

‌Within the ​Hittite ‌tradition, closely ​related ‌names ‌in the ​corpus ​include ​Alalu, Cerēs, ​Dēmētēr, ‌Gefjun, ‌Hoori, and ​Ninurta.

Sources

  1. CTH 324 — Telipinu Myth.

Cultural Legacy

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Modern influence, literature, art, popular culture, and contemporary practice.

Telipinu ​is ‌one of ‌the ​most ​accessible ​Hittite deities ​for ​modern ‌readers because ​his ​myth ‌is ​so recognisably ‌human: ​a ‌family ​member storms ​out, ​the ​household falls ​apart, ‌and ​a ‌careful apology ​is ‌required ​to ‌restore harmony. ​In ‌Assyriology ​and Hittitology, ​the ‌Telipinu ‌Myth ‌is a ​standard ​text ‌for ​teaching Hittite ​language ‌and ​religion. Its ​legacy ‌is ​the ‌idea that ​fertility ​is ​a ​relationship, not ‌a ‌machine ​— maintained ‌by ​ritual ​attention ‌and capable ‌of ‌being ‌ruptured ​by neglect ‌or ​offense. ​For PuniCodex, ​Telipinu ‌also ​illustrates ​the plain ​Tier-2 ‌form: ​a ​name whose ​restoration ‌preserves ​only its ‌proper ​capitalization, ‌inviting ​visitors to ‌ask ‌what ​the ​cuneiform recorded ​and ​what ‌the voice ‌once ​said.[1]

Archaeology & Material Evidence

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Sites, inscriptions, artifacts, and physical attestations.

The ​Telipinu ‌‌myth ​is ​​attested ​on ​cuneiform ‌​tablets ​excavated ‌​at ‌Ḫattuša ​(modern ‌​Boğazkale), ​the ​​Hittite ​capital, ‌and ​‌in ​fragments ‌​from ‌other ​Anatolian ‌​sites. ​The ‌‌best-preserved ‌manuscripts ​belong ​‌to ​the ​‌archives ​of ​the ‌​Great ‌Temple ​and ​​the ​House ‌‌on ​the ‌Slope. ​​Telipinu ‌also ‌‌had ‌a ​cult ‌​seat ​at ​‌the ​city ​of ​‌Tawiniya, ​and ​​offerings ‌to ​him ‌​appear ‌in ​‌Hittite ‌festival ​texts ​​and ​royal ‌‌oracle ​inquiries.[1]

Hittite Texts

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​principal ‌witness ‌to ​Telipinu is ​the ​Telipinu ​Myth ​(CTH ‌324), ​preserved ​in ‌Hittite ​Empire copies ‌from ​Ḫattuša ‌and ​other ​Anatolian ​centres. ​The ​narrative ‌is liturgical ​as ‌well ​as ‌literary: ​it ‌is ​recited ‌as ​part of ​a ‌ritual ‌of ‌appeasement ​designed ​to ‌undo ​the ​divine anger ‌it ​describes. ​Related ‌compositions ​under ‌CTH ​325 ​develop the ​same ​motif ‌through ‌ritual ​parables ‌that ​dramatise ​the ‌search and ‌restoration ‌of ‌order. ​The ‌language ​is ​classic ​New ‌Kingdom Hittite ​cuneiform, ​occasionally ​embedded ‌with ​Hurrian ​or ​Hattic ‌ritual ​formulae. These ‌tablets ​show ‌Telipinu ​as ‌a ‌focus ​of ​royal ​cult, not ​merely ‌a ‌mythic ​figure.

Cuneiform Archives

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

The ​Telipinu ‌tablets ‌belong to ​the ​palace ​and ​temple ​archives ‌excavated at ​Ḫattuša ​(modern ‌Boğazkale), ​the ‌Hittite ​capital, ‌principally from ​the ​Great ​Temple ​precinct ​and ‌the House ​on ‌the ​Slope. ‌Key ​witnesses ‌include KUB ​17.10 ‌and ​KBo ​19.83, ‌both ‌containing ‌versions of ​the ​Telipinu ‌Myth, ​as ​well ‌as festival ​and ​oracle ‌texts ​that ‌mention ​offerings to ​Telipinu ​at ​Tawiniya. ‌The ‌cuneiform ​hand is ‌the ​standard ​Hittite ‌ductus ‌of ‌the ‌New Kingdom, ​with ‌the ​divine ​name ​written ‌with the ​determinative ​dingir ​(𒀭). ‌These ​archives ​preserve the ​name ‌in ​a ‌scribal ​tradition ‌that ​spanned the ‌empire ‌and ​continued ​to ​copy ​and adapt ‌older ‌Anatolian ​material.

Meditation & Reflection

Contributed by PuniCodex Team

Contemplative or interpretive essay on the figure's enduring meaning.

The ​myth of ‌Telipinu is ‌a meditation ​on absence ​as an ​active force. ​When ​the god ‌leaves, nothing ​is destroyed; ​everything is ‌merely left. ​The fire ‌smothers, the ​mother ‌forgets, the ​ewe rejects ​her lamb ​— these ​are not ​attacks but ‌withdrawals. ​Fertility, in ‌this theology, ​is not ‌a default ​state of ‌nature; it ​is ‌a gift ​that requires ​divine presence ‌and human ‌care. The ‌ritual that ​brings Telipinu ​back ‌does not ​overpower him; ​it persuades ‌him, soothes ​him, and ​offers him ‌a ​way to ‌return without ​losing face.

​In this ​sense Telipinu ​is the ‌god ‌of reconciliation ​with the ‌source of ​life. His ​anger is ‌legitimate, his ‌departure understandable, ‌and ‌his restoration ​conditional. The ‌Hittite king ​and queen ​participate in ​the ritual ‌not ​as tyrants ​commanding nature ​but as ‌householders mending ​a quarrel. ​Every farm ​that ‌depends on ​rain, every ‌family that ​depends on ‌patience, and ​every society ‌that depends ‌on ​the return ​of spring ​rehearses, in ​its own ‌way, the ‌return of ​Telipinu.[1]
